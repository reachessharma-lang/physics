Electric Current

Electric current is the rate at which electric charge flows through a conductor. In metallic conductors, current is produced by the directed motion of free electrons. The conventional direction of electric current is taken to be the direction in which positive charge would flow, opposite to the drift direction of electrons.

  • Electric current is the rate of flow of positive charge.
  • The SI unit of current is ampere (A).
  • \(1\ \mathrm{A}=1\ \mathrm{C\,s^{-1}}\).
  • For a conductor, the current is related to the number density of charge carriers by \(I=nAvq\).
  • The average drift velocity is the small net velocity acquired by free charge carriers due to an applied electric field.

Electric Current as the Rate of Flow of Charge

Definition: Electric current is defined as the rate of flow of positive charge through a cross-section of a conductor.

If a charge \(Q\) flows through a cross-section of a conductor in time \(t\), then the electric current is

\[ \boxed{I=\frac{Q}{t}} \] where \(I\) is electric current, \(Q\) is charge flowing through the conductor, and \(t\) is the time taken.

Rearranging the above relation gives

\[ \boxed{Q=It} \]
Important relation:
\[ \boxed{Q=It} \]

The total charge passing through a cross-section is equal to the product of current and time.

SI Unit of Electric Current

From

\[ I=\frac{Q}{t} \]

the SI unit of current is coulomb per second.

\[ 1\ \mathrm{A}=1\ \mathrm{C\,s^{-1}} \]

Thus, a current of \(1\ \mathrm{A}\) means that \(1\ \mathrm{C}\) of positive charge passes through a cross-section of the conductor every second.

Average Current

If the rate of charge flow is not constant, the average current over a time interval \(\Delta t\) is

\[ \boxed{I_{\mathrm{avg}}=\frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta t}} \]

For steady current, the current remains constant and

\[ Q=It \]

Conventional Direction of Current

By convention, the direction of electric current is the direction of flow of positive charge. In a metallic conductor, however, the actual mobile charge carriers are electrons, which are negatively charged and drift opposite to the direction of conventional current.

Exam point: Although electrons move from lower potential towards higher potential inside a metallic conductor, conventional current is considered to flow from higher potential to lower potential in the external circuit.

Derivation of \(I=nAvq\) Using Charge Flow and Drift Velocity

Consider a uniform conductor of cross-sectional area \(A\). Let the free charge carriers have:

  • number density \(n\), i.e. number of free charge carriers per unit volume;
  • charge \(q\) on each charge carrier;
  • average drift velocity \(v\).

Step 1: Distance Travelled by Charge Carriers

If the average drift velocity of the charge carriers is \(v\), then in time \(t\), the average distance travelled along the conductor is

\[ \boxed{l=vt} \]

Step 2: Volume of the Conductor Containing the Charge Carriers

The charge carriers that cross a particular cross-section in time \(t\) occupy a cylindrical volume of length \(l=vt\) and cross-sectional area \(A\).

Therefore, the volume is

\[ V_{\mathrm{volume}}=Al \]

Substituting \(l=vt\),

\[ V_{\mathrm{volume}}=Avt \]

Step 3: Number of Charge Carriers

The number density \(n\) is defined as the number of free charge carriers per unit volume:

\[ n=\frac{N}{V_{\mathrm{volume}}} \]

Hence,

\[ N=nV_{\mathrm{volume}} \]

Using \(V_{\mathrm{volume}}=Avt\),

\[ \boxed{N=nAvt} \]

Step 4: Total Charge Passing Through the Cross-Section

If each charge carrier has charge \(q\), the total charge passing through the cross-section in time \(t\) is

\[ Q=Nq \]

Substituting \(N=nAvt\),

\[ Q=(nAvt)q \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{Q=nAvqt} \]

Step 5: Use \(Q=It\)

From the definition of electric current,

\[ Q=It \]

But from the charge-carrier calculation,

\[ Q=nAvqt \]

Equating the two expressions for \(Q\),

\[ It=nAvqt \]

Dividing both sides by \(t\),

\[ \boxed{I=nAvq} \]
Current in terms of drift velocity:
\[ \boxed{I=nAvq} \] where \(n\) is number density of free charge carriers, \(A\) is cross-sectional area, \(v\) is average drift velocity, and \(q\) is charge on each carrier.

Physical Meaning of \(I=nAvq\)

The relation

\[ I=nAvq \]

shows that electric current depends on four factors:

  • Number density \(n\): Greater the number of free charge carriers per unit volume, greater the current.
  • Cross-sectional area \(A\): A larger cross-sectional area allows more charge carriers to pass per second, increasing current.
  • Drift velocity \(v\): Greater average drift velocity produces greater current.
  • Charge \(q\): Greater charge carried by each particle produces greater current.

Thus,

\[ I\propto n \qquad I\propto A \qquad I\propto v \qquad I\propto q \]

Derivation in Compact Examination Form

Exam-ready derivation:

Let \(n\) be the number density of free charge carriers in a conductor of cross-sectional area \(A\). If the average drift velocity is \(v\), then in time \(t\), the distance travelled by the charge carriers is

\[ l=vt \]

The volume of the conductor through which the carriers pass is

\[ V=Al=Avt \]

Hence, the number of charge carriers crossing the cross-section is

\[ N=nV=nAvt \]

If each carrier has charge \(q\), the total charge crossing the section is

\[ Q=Nq=nAvqt \]

But

\[ Q=It \]

Therefore,

\[ It=nAvqt \]

or

\[ \boxed{I=nAvq} \]

Average Drift Velocity

Average drift velocity: The average velocity acquired by free charge carriers in a conductor due to the applied electric field is called average drift velocity.

In the absence of an applied electric field, free electrons in a metal move randomly because of thermal motion. Their random velocities cancel out on average, so there is no net current.

When an electric field is applied, the electrons acquire a small average drift velocity in a direction opposite to the electric field. This directed drift produces electric current.

Do not confuse: Drift velocity is not the same as the random thermal velocity of electrons. The random velocities may be large, but their vector average is zero without an applied electric field. Drift velocity is the small average directed velocity responsible for current.

Alternative Form of the Current Equation

For electrons in a conductor, the magnitude of the charge of each electron is \(e\). Therefore,

\[ \boxed{I=neAv_d} \] where \(e\) is the magnitude of electronic charge and \(v_d\) is the magnitude of electron drift velocity.

For a general charge carrier of charge magnitude \(q\), the relation is

\[ \boxed{I=nAqv_d} \]

Problem: A current of \(4\ \mathrm{A}\) flows through a copper wire of cross-sectional area \(1\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m^2}\). If the number density of free electrons is \(8\times10^{28}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}\), find the drift velocity. Take \(e=1.6\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{C}\).

\[ I=neAv_d \]

Therefore,

\[ v_d=\frac{I}{neA} \]

Substituting the values,

\[ v_d= \frac{4} {(8\times10^{28})(1.6\times10^{-19})(1\times10^{-6})} \]

Thus,

Common Mistakes

  • Do not write \(I=Q/t^2\). The correct relation is \(I=Q/t\).
  • Remember that \(Q=It\) is valid directly for constant current.
  • Do not confuse conventional current direction with electron drift direction.
  • In a metallic conductor, electrons drift opposite to the direction of conventional current.
  • Do not confuse number density \(n\) with total number of charge carriers \(N\).
  • Number density is number of charge carriers per unit volume: \(n=N/V\).
  • The volume through which charge carriers pass in time \(t\) is \(Av_dt\).
  • Do not forget the charge \(q\) of each carrier while calculating total charge.
  • For electrons, use the magnitude \(e=1.6\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{C}\) when calculating the magnitude of current.
  • Always convert the time into seconds when calculating current in amperes.
  • Always use SI units for \(A\), \(n\), \(v_d\), and \(q\) in \(I=nAvq\).

Key Points Summary

  • Electric current is the rate of flow of positive charge.
  • \(I=\dfrac{Q}{t}\).
  • \(Q=It\).
  • The SI unit of current is ampere.
  • \(1\ \mathrm{A}=1\ \mathrm{C\,s^{-1}}\).
  • In metallic conductors, free electrons are the charge carriers.
  • Conventional current is opposite to the drift direction of electrons.
  • If \(n\) is the number density of charge carriers, \(A\) is cross-sectional area, \(v\) is average drift velocity and \(q\) is charge on each carrier, then \(I=nAvq\).
  • The charge crossing a cross-section in time \(t\) is \(Q=nAvqt\).
  • For electrons, the magnitude of current is \(I=neAv_d\).
  • Drift velocity is the small average directed velocity acquired by charge carriers due to an applied electric field.
  • The derivation of \(I=nAvq\) follows directly from \(Q=It\) and \(Q=Nq\).

Every electrical device, from a torch to a mobile charger, depends on how charge flows through a circuit. This chapter explains what limits the current (resistance), how resistance depends on the size and material of a conductor, how sensors use changes in resistance, how resistors combine in series and parallel, how a potential divider gives a variable voltage, why a real battery loses voltage when it supplies current (internal resistance), and how electrical energy is converted into heat and other forms (power).

Quantities and units used in these notes:

  • Current \(I\) in ampere (A), potential difference \(V\) in volt (V), resistance \(R\) in ohm (\(\Omega\)), power \(P\) in watt (W), energy \(W\) in joule (J).
  • Conventional current is taken to flow from the positive terminal of a cell, through the external circuit, to the negative terminal.
  • Meters are treated as ideal (an ammeter has zero resistance and a voltmeter has infinite resistance) and connecting wires have zero resistance, unless stated otherwise.
  • In circuit diagrams, the longer line of a cell symbol is the positive terminal.

23.2 Electrical Resistance, Resistivity and Conductivity

Electric resistance

Definition: The electrical resistance of a component is the ratio of the potential difference across it to the current through it.

\[ R = \frac{V}{I} \]

A large resistance means a small current for a given p.d. Resistance arises because the moving charge carriers (free electrons in a metal) collide with the vibrating lattice ions and with impurities, and so lose energy.

The ohm

Definition: One ohm (1 \(\Omega\)) is the resistance of a conductor through which a current of 1 ampere flows when a potential difference of 1 volt is applied across it.

\[ 1\ \Omega = 1\ \text{V A}^{-1} \]

The equation \(R = V/I\) defines resistance for any component, whether or not it obeys Ohm's law (see 23.3).

Factors affecting the resistance of a wire (at constant temperature)

  • The resistance is directly proportional to the length: \(R \propto l\). A longer wire means more collisions along the path.
  • The resistance is inversely proportional to the cross-sectional area: \(R \propto \dfrac{1}{A}\). A thicker wire gives the charges more paths to flow through.
  • The resistance depends on the material.

Combining the first two, \(R \propto \dfrac{l}{A}\). Introducing a constant of proportionality \(\rho\), which depends on the material,

\[ \boxed{R = \frac{\rho\, l}{A}} \]

Resistivity

Definition: The resistivity \(\rho\) of a material is the resistance of a wire of that material of unit length and unit cross-sectional area.

\[ \rho = \frac{RA}{l} \]

The SI unit of resistivity is the ohm metre (\(\Omega\ \text{m}\)).

Resistivity is a property of the material (and its temperature), not of the shape or size of the sample. Resistance depends on both material and shape.

Conductance and conductivity

Definition: Conductance \(G\) is the reciprocal of resistance, \(G = \dfrac{1}{R}\). Its unit is the siemens (S), where \(1\ \text{S} = 1\ \Omega^{-1}\).

Definition: Conductivity \(\sigma\) of a material is the reciprocal of its resistivity.

\[ \sigma = \frac{1}{\rho} = \frac{l}{RA} \]

The SI unit of conductivity is the siemens per metre (\(\text{S m}^{-1}\) or \(\Omega^{-1}\ \text{m}^{-1}\)).

A good conductor has low resistivity and high conductivity.

Change of resistance with temperature

For a metal, resistivity increases with temperature, because the lattice ions vibrate more and collide more often with the electrons. For a semiconductor, resistivity decreases with temperature, because more charge carriers become free (see the thermistor below).

Wire dimensions and the variable resistor

From \(R = \dfrac{\rho l}{A}\), the resistance of a wire of fixed material can be changed by changing its dimensions:

  • Changing the length: a variable resistor (rheostat) has a long coil of resistance wire (such as nichrome) wound on an insulating cylinder. A sliding contact selects how much of the wire is in the circuit. A longer length in the circuit gives a larger resistance (\(R \propto l\)). A slide-wire used in the laboratory works the same way.
  • Changing the area: a thicker wire has a smaller resistance. A thin wire is used where high resistance is needed, for example in a heater element.
  • Stretching a wire: stretching changes both length and area. The volume of the metal stays constant, \(Al = \) constant. If the length becomes \(k\) times the original, then \(l' = kl\) and \(A' = \dfrac{A}{k}\). So
\[ R' = \frac{\rho\, l'}{A'} = \frac{\rho\, (kl)}{A/k} = k^2\,\frac{\rho l}{A} = k^2 R \]

If a wire is stretched to double its length, its resistance becomes four times as large.

Strain gauge (pressure sensor)

A strain gauge is a thin metal foil, usually a long thin wire folded back and forth in a grid pattern, stuck on a flexible insulating backing. It has two terminals.

Working. The gauge is bonded to a surface (for example a diaphragm or a beam). When the pressure or force changes, the surface deforms and the gauge is stretched or compressed with it.

  • When the foil is stretched, its length \(l\) increases and its cross-sectional area \(A\) decreases. By \(R = \dfrac{\rho l}{A}\), its resistance increases.
  • A greater pressure gives a greater strain and so a greater increase in resistance.
  • The fractional change in resistance is proportional to the strain \( \varepsilon = \dfrac{\Delta l}{l} \):
\[ \frac{\Delta R}{R} = G_f\, \varepsilon \]

Here \(G_f\) is the gauge factor, about 2 for metal foil. (For a wire of constant volume, \(R \propto l^2\), which gives \(\dfrac{\Delta R}{R} \approx 2\dfrac{\Delta l}{l}\).) The change is very small, so the gauge is connected in a potential divider or bridge circuit, and the resulting change in voltage is measured.

Use as a pressure sensor. The gauge is mounted on a diaphragm that bends when the pressure changes. The change in resistance is converted into a change in voltage that is displayed as the pressure reading. Examples are blood-pressure monitors, tyre-pressure sensors and weighing scales.

Light-dependent resistor (LDR, light sensor)

An LDR is made of a semiconductor material such as cadmium sulphide. In the dark, most electrons are bound to atoms, so there are few free charge carriers and the resistance is very high (of the order of a megohm). When light falls on it, photons give energy to bound electrons and free them. The number of charge carriers increases, so the resistance decreases as the light intensity increases (to a few hundred ohms in bright light).

n-type (NTC) thermistor (electronic temperature sensor)

A thermistor is a resistor made of a semiconducting metal oxide whose resistance is very sensitive to temperature. The n-type thermistor has a negative temperature coefficient (NTC): its resistance decreases as the temperature rises. At a higher temperature, more electrons gain enough thermal energy to become free, so there are more charge carriers. The effect of more carriers is larger than the effect of more lattice vibration, so the net resistance falls. The fall is non-linear.

23.3 Current-Voltage Relations: Ohmic and Non-ohmic Conductors

Ohm's law

Ohm's law: The current through a conductor is directly proportional to the potential difference across its ends, provided its temperature and other physical conditions remain constant.

\[ I \propto V \quad\Rightarrow\quad V = IR \quad (R \text{ constant}) \]

Ohm's law is not the same as the definition \(R = V/I\). The definition applies to every component. Ohm's law says that for some conductors \(R\) stays constant as \(V\) changes.

Ohmic and non-ohmic conductors

  • An ohmic conductor obeys Ohm's law. Its \(I\)-\(V\) graph is a straight line through the origin, and its resistance is constant. Example: a metallic conductor (a resistor) at constant temperature.
  • A non-ohmic conductor does not obey Ohm's law. Its \(I\)-\(V\) graph is curved, and its resistance changes with \(V\) or \(I\). Examples: a filament lamp, a semiconductor diode, a thermistor.

For any point on an \(I\)-\(V\) graph, the resistance at that point is \(R = \dfrac{V}{I}\), the ratio of the coordinates at that point. It is not the slope of the tangent for a curved graph.

(a) Metallic conductor at constant temperature

The graph is a straight line through the origin, so \(I \propto V\) and \(R\) is constant. The gradient of the graph is \(1/R\). A steeper line means a smaller resistance. This holds only while the temperature is constant. If the current is large enough to heat the wire, the graph bends (see the filament lamp).

(b) Filament lamp

Explanation. The filament is a metal (tungsten) wire. As the current increases, the filament becomes hotter (it can reach over 2000 \(^\circ\)C). A higher temperature means more vigorous lattice vibrations and more collisions with the electrons, so the resistance \(R = V/I\) increases. The ratio \(V/I\) therefore grows as \(V\) grows, and the graph bends towards the voltage axis. The lamp is non-ohmic. The graph is symmetrical about the origin because the filament behaves the same in both directions.

(c) Semiconductor diode

  • Forward bias (p-side positive): almost no current until the p.d. exceeds the threshold (about 0.6 V for silicon, 0.2 V for germanium). Above the threshold, a small increase in p.d. causes a large increase in current, so the resistance becomes very small.
  • Reverse bias (p-side negative): only a tiny leakage current flows, so the resistance is very high. (At a very large reverse voltage the diode breaks down and a large current flows.)
  • The diode conducts in one direction only. It is non-ohmic and is used as a rectifier.

23.4 Resistors in Series and in Parallel

Two conservation laws are used to derive the formulae:

  • Conservation of charge: charge is not created or destroyed, and it does not pile up at any point in a steady circuit. The current into a junction equals the current out of it.
  • Conservation of energy: the energy a source gives to each coulomb of charge equals the total energy the coulomb transfers in the components of the circuit. Equivalently, the p.d. across a source equals the sum of the p.d.s across components in series. Between two points, the p.d. is the same along every path.

Resistors in series

Derivation. Let resistors \(R_1\), \(R_2\), \(R_3\) be connected end to end across a source of p.d. \(V\). Let the equivalent resistance be \(R\).

Step 1 (conservation of charge). There are no junctions where charge can leave or collect, so the same current \(I\) flows through every resistor:

\[ I_1 = I_2 = I_3 = I \]

Step 2 (conservation of energy). Each coulomb leaving the source gains energy \(V\) joules, and it loses \(V_1\), \(V_2\), \(V_3\) joules as it passes through the three resistors. Energy gained equals energy lost:

\[ V = V_1 + V_2 + V_3 \]

Step 3. Apply \(V = IR\) to each resistor and to the combination:

\[ IR = IR_1 + IR_2 + IR_3 \]

Divide by the common current \(I\):

\[ \boxed{R = R_1 + R_2 + R_3} \]

For \(n\) resistors, \(R = R_1 + R_2 + \dots + R_n\). The combined resistance is greater than the largest individual resistance. The p.d.s are in proportion to the resistances: \(V_1 : V_2 : V_3 = R_1 : R_2 : R_3\).

Resistors in parallel

Derivation. Let resistors \(R_1\), \(R_2\), \(R_3\) be connected between the same two points, across a source of p.d. \(V\). Let the equivalent resistance be \(R\) and the total current be \(I\).

Step 1 (conservation of energy). Each branch connects the same two points, so each coulomb that passes through any branch transfers the same energy per coulomb, which is the same p.d.:

\[ V_1 = V_2 = V_3 = V \]

Step 2 (conservation of charge). Charge does not pile up at the junction, so the current entering equals the sum of the currents leaving:

\[ I = I_1 + I_2 + I_3 \]

Step 3. Apply \(I = V/R\) to each branch and to the combination:

\[ \frac{V}{R} = \frac{V}{R_1} + \frac{V}{R_2} + \frac{V}{R_3} \]

Divide by the common \(V\):

\[ \boxed{\frac{1}{R} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} + \frac{1}{R_3}} \]

For two resistors, this becomes

\[ R = \frac{R_1 R_2}{R_1 + R_2} \quad \left(\frac{\text{product}}{\text{sum}}\right) \]
  • The combined resistance is less than the smallest individual resistance, because the current has more paths.
  • The currents divide in inverse proportion to the resistances: \(I_1 : I_2 = R_2 : R_1\). The smaller resistor carries more current.
  • \(n\) identical resistors \(r\) in parallel give \(R = \dfrac{r}{n}\).

Method for mixed circuits

  1. Combine each parallel group into one equivalent resistor.
  2. Add the series resistors to get the total resistance \(R_T\).
  3. Find the total current \(I = V/R_T\).
  4. Work back through the circuit to find the p.d. across each part, and then the current in each branch.

23.5 Potential Divider

Potential divider: a potential divider is a circuit of two (or more) resistors in series across a supply. It splits the supply p.d. into smaller parts, and the output is taken across one of the resistors.

Principle and formula

The resistors are in series, so the same current flows through both:

\[ I = \frac{V_{in}}{R_1 + R_2} \]

The output is the p.d. across \(R_2\):

\[ \boxed{V_{out} = IR_2 = \frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2}\, V_{in}} \]

The p.d. across \(R_1\) is \(\dfrac{R_1}{R_1+R_2} V_{in}\). The resistor with the larger resistance takes the larger share of the supply p.d. The output is always smaller than the input, and it is controlled by the ratio of the resistances. The formula assumes that no current is drawn from the output terminals.

Variable potential divider (potentiometer)

A variable resistor with three terminals can be used as a potential divider. The whole resistance track is connected across the supply, and the output is taken between a sliding contact (the slider) and one end of the track. Moving the slider changes the ratio \(R_2 : R_1\), so the output can be varied smoothly from 0 to \(V_{in}\). This is how a volume control works.

Effect of a load

If a load is connected across the output terminals, it is in parallel with \(R_2\). The effective lower resistance is smaller than \(R_2\), so the output p.d. falls. The load resistance should therefore be much larger than \(R_2\) if the output is to stay close to the no-load value.

Use of sensors in a potential divider

A sensor whose resistance changes with the quantity being measured is used as one of the two resistors. The output p.d. then changes with that quantity, and can be measured, displayed, or used to switch a circuit on or off.

Key rule: in a series divider, the p.d. across a resistor increases when its own resistance increases (it takes a bigger share), and decreases when its resistance decreases.

Examples of use: a thermistor divider in a fire alarm or thermostat, an LDR divider in an automatic street light (a switching circuit turns the lamp on when the output passes a set value), and a strain-gauge divider in a pressure gauge.

23.6 Electromotive Force and Internal Resistance

Electromotive force (e.m.f.)

Definition: The electromotive force (e.m.f.) \(E\) of a source is the energy transferred from other forms (chemical energy in a cell) to electrical energy by the source per unit charge in driving the charge round a complete circuit.

\[ E = \frac{W}{Q} \]

The unit is the volt: 1 V = 1 J C\(^{-1}\).

An e.m.f. is not a force. The name is historical.

Potential difference (p.d.)

Definition: The potential difference \(V\) between two points in a circuit is the energy transferred from electrical energy to other forms (heat, light, mechanical) per unit charge passing from one point to the other.

\[ V = \frac{W}{Q} \]

Internal resistance

Definition: The internal resistance \(r\) of a source is the resistance of the material inside the source (electrolyte and electrodes of a cell) to the flow of charge through it.

Every real source has internal resistance. When current flows, some of the energy provided by the source is converted into heat inside the source itself, so less energy per coulomb is available to the external circuit.

Terminal p.d. and internal resistance

Consider charge \(Q\) passing round the circuit in time \(t\), with current \(I = Q/t\). Using conservation of energy, the energy supplied by the source equals the energy dissipated in the external resistor plus the energy dissipated in the internal resistance:

\[ EQ = (IR)Q + (Ir)Q \]

Divide by \(Q\):

\[ \boxed{E = IR + Ir = V + Ir} \]
  • The terminal p.d. is \(V = IR\) (the p.d. across the external circuit).
  • The "lost volts" is the p.d. across the internal resistance, \(Ir\).
\[ \boxed{V = E - Ir} \qquad \boxed{I = \frac{E}{R + r}} \]

Effects of internal resistance on the terminal p.d.:

  • With no current (open circuit, \(I = 0\)), \(V = E\).
  • When a current is drawn, \(V = E - Ir\) is less than \(E\). The larger the current, the greater the lost volts and the lower the terminal p.d.
  • A source with a small \(r\) keeps its terminal p.d. nearly constant (a good battery). A source with a large \(r\) has a terminal p.d. that falls a lot when current is drawn.
  • If the terminals are short-circuited (\(R = 0\)), the current is the maximum possible, \(I_{max} = \dfrac{E}{r}\), and the cell heats up.

Graph of terminal p.d. against current

A practical method: vary the external resistance with a rheostat, record pairs of ammeter and voltmeter readings, plot \(V\) against \(I\), and find \(E\) from the intercept and \(r\) from the magnitude of the gradient.

23.7 Work and Power in an Electrical Circuit

Electrical energy and power: \(P = IV\)

Derivation. By the definition of potential difference, when charge \(Q\) moves through a p.d. \(V\), the energy transferred is

\[ W = QV \]

By the definition of current, \(I = \dfrac{Q}{t}\), so \(Q = It\). Substituting,

\[ W = VIt \]

Power is the rate of transfer of energy, \(P = \dfrac{W}{t}\):

\[ \boxed{P = \frac{W}{t} = IV} \]

The SI unit of power is the watt: 1 W = 1 J s\(^{-1}\) = 1 V A. The energy transferred in time \(t\) is \(W = Pt = VIt\).

Power in a resistor: \(P = I^2R = \dfrac{V^2}{R}\)

For a resistor, Ohm's definition \(V = IR\) holds. Substitute into \(P = IV\):

\[ P = I(IR) = I^2R \qquad \text{and, using } I = \frac{V}{R}: \qquad P = \frac{V}{R}\, V = \frac{V^2}{R} \] \[ \boxed{P = IV = I^2R = \frac{V^2}{R}} \]
  • Use \(I^2R\) when the same current flows through different resistors (series). The larger resistance dissipates more power.
  • Use \(V^2/R\) when the same p.d. is across different resistors (parallel). The smaller resistance dissipates more power.

Heating effect of an electric current (Joule heating)

When a current flows through a resistor, the charge carriers collide with the lattice ions and transfer energy to them. The ions vibrate more, so the resistor gets hotter. All the electrical energy supplied to a pure resistor appears as heat:

\[ \boxed{H = I^2Rt = VIt = \frac{V^2}{R}t} \]

Applications: electric heaters, kettles, irons and toasters use wires of high resistivity and high melting point (nichrome). Filament lamps heat a tungsten filament until it glows. A fuse is a thin wire of low melting point. When the current exceeds its rating, \(I^2R\) heating melts it and breaks the circuit.

Unwanted heating: in transmission lines, the power lost is \(P_{loss} = I^2R\). For a given power \(P = VI\) to be delivered, a higher transmission voltage gives a lower current and so a much smaller loss. This is why electricity is transmitted at high voltage.

The kilowatt-hour

The commercial unit of electrical energy is the kilowatt-hour (kWh), the energy used by a 1 kW appliance in 1 hour:

\[ 1\ \text{kWh} = 1000\ \text{W} \times 3600\ \text{s} = 3.6 \times 10^6\ \text{J} \]

Power in the whole circuit and maximum power transfer

For a source of e.m.f. \(E\) and internal resistance \(r\) driving a current \(I\) through an external resistance \(R\):

\[ EI = I^2R + I^2r \]

The power delivered by the source (\(EI\)) is the power in the external resistor (\(I^2R\)) plus the power wasted in the internal resistance (\(I^2r\)). The power in the external resistor is

\[ P = I^2R = \frac{E^2R}{(R+r)^2} \]

This is a maximum when \(R = r\) (the load resistance equals the internal resistance), and then \(P_{max} = \dfrac{E^2}{4r}\). Only half of the total power is then delivered to the load.

Worked Examples

Example 1: resistance from p.d. and current

A p.d. of 12 V across a resistor produces a current of 0.40 A. Find its resistance. What current flows if the p.d. is changed to 18 V (assuming Ohm's law holds)?

Solution.

\[ R = \frac{V}{I} = \frac{12}{0.40} = 30\ \Omega \] \[ I' = \frac{V'}{R} = \frac{18}{30} = 0.60\ \text{A} \]

Example 2: resistance of a copper wire

Find the resistance of a copper wire of length 2.0 m and diameter 0.50 mm. (Resistivity of copper \(= 1.7 \times 10^{-8}\ \Omega\ \text{m}\).)

Solution. Cross-sectional area:

\[ A = \frac{\pi d^2}{4} = \frac{\pi (0.50 \times 10^{-3})^2}{4} = 1.96 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^2 \] \[ R = \frac{\rho l}{A} = \frac{(1.7 \times 10^{-8})(2.0)}{1.96 \times 10^{-7}} = 0.17\ \Omega \]

Example 3: resistivity and conductivity from measurements

A wire of length 1.5 m and cross-sectional area \(0.30\ \text{mm}^2\) has resistance 0.90 \(\Omega\). Find its resistivity and conductivity.

Solution. \(A = 0.30\ \text{mm}^2 = 0.30 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{m}^2 = 3.0 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^2\).

\[ \rho = \frac{RA}{l} = \frac{0.90 \times 3.0 \times 10^{-7}}{1.5} = 1.8 \times 10^{-7}\ \Omega\ \text{m} \] \[ \sigma = \frac{1}{\rho} = \frac{1}{1.8 \times 10^{-7}} = 5.6 \times 10^{6}\ \text{S m}^{-1} \]

Example 4: a stretched wire

A wire of resistance 5.0 \(\Omega\) is uniformly stretched to three times its original length. Find its new resistance.

Solution. The volume is constant, so when \(l' = 3l\), \(A' = \dfrac{A}{3}\).

\[ R' = \frac{\rho (3l)}{A/3} = 9\,\frac{\rho l}{A} = 9R = 9 \times 5.0 = 45\ \Omega \]

Example 5: strain gauge

A strain gauge of resistance 120 \(\Omega\) and gauge factor 2.0 is stretched so that the strain is \(1.0 \times 10^{-3}\). (a) Find the change in resistance. (b) The gauge is the upper resistor of a potential divider with a fixed 120 \(\Omega\) resistor and a 5.0 V supply, with the output across the fixed resistor. Find the change in output p.d.

Solution.

(a) \( \dfrac{\Delta R}{R} = G_f \varepsilon = 2.0 \times 1.0 \times 10^{-3} = 2.0 \times 10^{-3} \), so \( \Delta R = 0.0020 \times 120 = 0.24\ \Omega \).

(b) Before stretching: \(V_{out} = 5.0 \times \dfrac{120}{240} = 2.500\ \text{V}\). After stretching, the gauge is 120.24 \(\Omega\):

\[ V_{out}' = 5.0 \times \frac{120}{120.24 + 120} = 5.0 \times \frac{120}{240.24} = 2.4975\ \text{V} \]

The output falls by about 2.5 mV. This is a very small change, so it is amplified before it is displayed.

Example 6: resistors in series

Resistors of 2 \(\Omega\), 3 \(\Omega\) and 5 \(\Omega\) are connected in series across a 20 V supply. Find the current and the p.d. across each resistor.

Solution. \(R = 2 + 3 + 5 = 10\ \Omega\), so \( I = \dfrac{20}{10} = 2.0\ \text{A} \).

\[ V_1 = 2.0 \times 2 = 4\ \text{V},\quad V_2 = 2.0 \times 3 = 6\ \text{V},\quad V_3 = 2.0 \times 5 = 10\ \text{V} \]

Check: \(4 + 6 + 10 = 20\ \text{V}\). ✓

Example 7: resistors in parallel

A 6 \(\Omega\) resistor and a 3 \(\Omega\) resistor are connected in parallel across a 12 V supply. Find the combined resistance, the total current and the current in each resistor.

Solution.

\[ R = \frac{6 \times 3}{6 + 3} = 2\ \Omega \qquad I = \frac{12}{2} = 6.0\ \text{A} \] \[ I_1 = \frac{12}{6} = 2.0\ \text{A}, \qquad I_2 = \frac{12}{3} = 4.0\ \text{A} \]

Check: \(2.0 + 4.0 = 6.0\ \text{A}\). ✓ The smaller resistor carries the larger current.

Example 8: series-parallel circuit

A 4 \(\Omega\) resistor is in series with a parallel combination of 6 \(\Omega\) and 12 \(\Omega\). The circuit is connected to a 24 V supply. Find the total resistance, the total current, and the current in each of the parallel resistors.

Solution. Parallel part: \( R_p = \dfrac{6 \times 12}{6 + 12} = \dfrac{72}{18} = 4\ \Omega \).

Total resistance: \( R_T = 4 + 4 = 8\ \Omega \). Total current: \( I = \dfrac{24}{8} = 3.0\ \text{A} \).

P.d. across the 4 \(\Omega\) resistor \(= 3.0 \times 4 = 12\ \text{V}\). So the p.d. across the parallel part is \(24 - 12 = 12\ \text{V}\).

\[ I_{6} = \frac{12}{6} = 2.0\ \text{A}, \qquad I_{12} = \frac{12}{12} = 1.0\ \text{A} \]

Check: \(2.0 + 1.0 = 3.0\ \text{A}\). ✓

Example 9: potential divider with a load

A 9.0 V supply is connected across \(R_1 = 6.0\ \text{k}\Omega\) and \(R_2 = 3.0\ \text{k}\Omega\) in series. (a) Find the output p.d. across \(R_2\). (b) Find the output if a 6.0 k\(\Omega\) load is connected across \(R_2\).

Solution.

(a) \( V_{out} = \dfrac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2} V_{in} = \dfrac{3.0}{9.0} \times 9.0 = 3.0\ \text{V} \)

(b) The load is in parallel with \(R_2\): \( R_2' = \dfrac{3.0 \times 6.0}{3.0 + 6.0} = 2.0\ \text{k}\Omega \).

\[ V_{out}' = \frac{2.0}{6.0 + 2.0} \times 9.0 = 2.25\ \text{V} \]

The load lowers the output from 3.0 V to 2.25 V.

Example 10: thermistor in a potential divider

A 6.0 V supply is connected across an n-type thermistor (upper) and a fixed 2.0 k\(\Omega\) resistor (lower). The output is across the fixed resistor. The thermistor resistance is 4.0 k\(\Omega\) at 20 \(^\circ\)C and 0.50 k\(\Omega\) at 60 \(^\circ\)C. Find the output at each temperature.

Solution.

At 20 \(^\circ\)C: \( V_{out} = \dfrac{2.0}{4.0 + 2.0} \times 6.0 = 2.0\ \text{V} \)

At 60 \(^\circ\)C: \( V_{out} = \dfrac{2.0}{0.50 + 2.0} \times 6.0 = 4.8\ \text{V} \)

The output rises with temperature because the thermistor resistance falls, so the fixed resistor takes a greater share of the 6.0 V.

Example 11: LDR in a potential divider

An LDR (upper) and a fixed 10 k\(\Omega\) resistor (lower) are connected across a 9.0 V supply, and the output is across the fixed resistor. The LDR resistance is 20 k\(\Omega\) in the dark and 2.0 k\(\Omega\) in bright light. Find the output in each case.

Solution.

Dark: \( V_{out} = \dfrac{10}{20 + 10} \times 9.0 = 3.0\ \text{V} \)

Bright: \( V_{out} = \dfrac{10}{2.0 + 10} \times 9.0 = 7.5\ \text{V} \)

The output increases as the light gets brighter.

Example 12: internal resistance

A cell of e.m.f. 1.5 V and internal resistance 0.50 \(\Omega\) is connected to a 2.5 \(\Omega\) resistor. Find the current, the terminal p.d. and the lost volts.

Solution.

\[ I = \frac{E}{R + r} = \frac{1.5}{2.5 + 0.50} = 0.50\ \text{A} \] \[ V = IR = 0.50 \times 2.5 = 1.25\ \text{V} \qquad \text{Lost volts} = Ir = 0.50 \times 0.50 = 0.25\ \text{V} \]

Check: \(V = E - Ir = 1.5 - 0.25 = 1.25\ \text{V}\). ✓ Power in the resistor \(= 0.625\ \text{W}\), power in the internal resistance \(= 0.125\ \text{W}\), and their sum \(0.75\ \text{W} = EI\). ✓

Example 13: finding E and r from two readings

When a cell supplies a current of 0.20 A, its terminal p.d. is 1.40 V. When it supplies 0.60 A, the terminal p.d. is 1.20 V. Find its e.m.f. and internal resistance.

Solution. Use \(V = E - Ir\) for each reading:

\[ 1.40 = E - 0.20\,r \quad \text{... (i)} \qquad 1.20 = E - 0.60\,r \quad \text{... (ii)} \]

Subtract (ii) from (i): \(0.20 = 0.40\,r\), so \(r = 0.50\ \Omega\). Substitute in (i): \(E = 1.40 + 0.20 \times 0.50 = 1.50\ \text{V}\).

Example 14: power of a kettle

An electric kettle is rated 2.3 kW at 230 V. Find (a) the current and resistance of the element, (b) the time to heat 1.0 kg of water from 20 \(^\circ\)C to 100 \(^\circ\)C, assuming all the energy heats the water. (Specific heat capacity of water \(= 4200\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}\).)

Solution.

(a) \( I = \dfrac{P}{V} = \dfrac{2300}{230} = 10\ \text{A} \) and \( R = \dfrac{V}{I} = \dfrac{230}{10} = 23\ \Omega \). Check: \(I^2R = 100 \times 23 = 2300\ \text{W}\). ✓

(b) Energy needed: \( Q = mc\Delta\theta = 1.0 \times 4200 \times 80 = 3.36 \times 10^5\ \text{J} \).

\[ t = \frac{Q}{P} = \frac{3.36 \times 10^5}{2300} = 146\ \text{s} \approx 2.4\ \text{minutes} \]

The energy used is \(3.36 \times 10^5\ \text{J} = 0.093\ \text{kWh}\).

Example 15: power loss in transmission lines

A power of 100 kW is sent along a line of total resistance 5.0 \(\Omega\). Find the power lost in the line if the power is transmitted at (a) 2000 V and (b) 20 000 V.

Solution.

(a) \( I = \dfrac{P}{V} = \dfrac{100\,000}{2000} = 50\ \text{A} \), so \( P_{loss} = I^2R = 50^2 \times 5.0 = 12\,500\ \text{W} = 12.5\ \text{kW} \).

(b) \( I = \dfrac{100\,000}{20\,000} = 5.0\ \text{A} \), so \( P_{loss} = 5.0^2 \times 5.0 = 125\ \text{W} \).

Increasing the voltage by 10 times reduces the loss by 100 times.

Example 16: maximum power transfer

A battery of e.m.f. 12 V and internal resistance 2.0 \(\Omega\) is connected to a variable resistor \(R\). Find the power in \(R\) for \(R = 1.0\ \Omega\), \(2.0\ \Omega\) and \(4.0\ \Omega\), and state the maximum power.

Solution. \(I = \dfrac{12}{R + 2.0}\) and \(P = I^2R\).

\(R\) (\(\Omega\))\(I\) (A)\(P = I^2R\) (W)
1.04.016
2.03.018
4.02.016

The power is greatest when \(R = r = 2.0\ \Omega\). Then \( P_{max} = \dfrac{E^2}{4r} = \dfrac{144}{8} = 18\ \text{W} \). ✓

Common Mistakes

  • Confusing resistance with resistivity. Resistance (\(\Omega\)) depends on the length, area and material of the sample. Resistivity (\(\Omega\ \text{m}\)) depends on the material (and temperature) only.
  • Using the diameter instead of the area in \(R = \rho l/A\). Calculate \(A = \pi d^2/4\) (or \(\pi r^2\)) and convert mm\(^2\) to m\(^2\) (\(1\ \text{mm}^2 = 10^{-6}\ \text{m}^2\)).
  • When a wire is stretched, forgetting that the area decreases. The resistance becomes \(k^2 R\), not \(kR\).
  • Saying that Ohm's law is \(V = IR\) in every case. Ohm's law applies only to ohmic conductors at constant temperature. \(R = V/I\) is the definition of resistance for any component.
  • Saying that a filament lamp has a constant resistance. The resistance rises with temperature, so its \(I\)-\(V\) graph curves towards the \(V\)-axis.
  • Drawing the diode graph as a straight line, or with current flowing from the origin. There is almost no current until about 0.6 V in forward bias.
  • Saying that the resistance of an NTC thermistor increases with temperature. It decreases. Mixing this up with a metal wire, whose resistance increases.
  • Adding the reciprocals for series resistors, or adding resistances directly for parallel resistors. Series: add \(R\). Parallel: add \(1/R\).
  • Forgetting to invert at the end of a parallel calculation (\(\dfrac{1}{R} = \dots\) gives \(\dfrac{1}{R}\), not \(R\)).
  • Using the potential divider formula with the wrong resistor in the numerator. The numerator is the resistor across which the output is taken.
  • Ignoring the load across a potential divider output, which lowers the output p.d.
  • Saying that the e.m.f. of a cell is the same as its terminal p.d. They are equal only when no current flows. Also, saying the e.m.f. is a force.
  • Writing \(V = E + Ir\) for a cell supplying current. The correct relation is \(V = E - Ir\).
  • Using \(P = V^2/R\) with the supply voltage when the p.d. across the resistor is smaller (for example a resistor in series). Use the p.d. across that resistor.
  • Not converting units: kWh to J (\(\times 3.6 \times 10^6\)), minutes to seconds, k\(\Omega\) to \(\Omega\), cm to m.
  • Saying that a high-voltage line is "dangerous because high power is lost". The reason for high-voltage transmission is that it gives a lower current, so \(I^2R\) loss is much smaller.

Key Points Summary

  • Resistance: \(R = V/I\). One ohm is 1 V per ampere.
  • For a wire, \(R = \dfrac{\rho l}{A}\). Resistivity \(\rho = \dfrac{RA}{l}\) (unit \(\Omega\ \text{m}\)). Conductivity \(\sigma = 1/\rho\) (unit \(\text{S m}^{-1}\)). Conductance \(G = 1/R\) (unit S).
  • Changing the length (rheostat), the area, or stretching the wire (\(R \to k^2R\) for a length factor \(k\)) changes the resistance.
  • Strain gauge: stretching makes the foil longer and thinner, so \(R\) increases. \(\dfrac{\Delta R}{R} = G_f\,\varepsilon\), with \(G_f \approx 2\). It is a pressure sensor.
  • LDR: resistance decreases as light intensity increases. n-type (NTC) thermistor: resistance decreases as temperature increases. Both are due to more charge carriers.
  • Ohm's law: \(I \propto V\) at constant temperature. Ohmic: straight \(I\)-\(V\) line through the origin. Non-ohmic: filament lamp (resistance rises with temperature) and diode (conducts only in forward bias above about 0.6 V).
  • Series: \(R = R_1 + R_2 + \dots\). Same current, p.d.s add (charge and energy conservation).
  • Parallel: \(\dfrac{1}{R} = \dfrac{1}{R_1} + \dfrac{1}{R_2} + \dots\). Same p.d., currents add. For two resistors, \(R = \dfrac{R_1 R_2}{R_1 + R_2}\).
  • Potential divider: \(V_{out} = \dfrac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2} V_{in}\). The p.d. across a resistor increases when its resistance increases. With a sensor in a divider, \(V_{out}\) varies with temperature, light or strain.
  • E.m.f. is the energy converted from other forms to electrical per unit charge round a complete circuit. P.d. is the energy converted from electrical to other forms per unit charge between two points.
  • With internal resistance: \(E = V + Ir\), \(V = E - Ir\), \(I = \dfrac{E}{R + r}\). The terminal p.d. falls as the current increases. The \(V\)-\(I\) graph has intercept \(E\) and gradient \(-r\).
  • Power: \(P = IV = I^2R = \dfrac{V^2}{R}\). Energy: \(W = VIt\). Heating: \(H = I^2Rt\). \(1\ \text{kWh} = 3.6 \times 10^6\ \text{J}\).
  • Power loss in cables is \(I^2R\), so electricity is transmitted at high voltage and low current. Maximum power transfer to a load occurs when \(R = r\), with \(P_{max} = \dfrac{E^2}{4r}\).
