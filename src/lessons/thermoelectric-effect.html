15.1 Seebeck Effect & Thermocouples

In 1821, German physicist Thomas Johann Seebeck discovered that when two dissimilar metal wires are joined together at their ends to form a closed loop, and the two junctions are maintained at different temperatures, an electric current flows continuously through the loop. This phenomenon is known as the Seebeck Effect.

The electric current generated in the loop is called thermoelectric current, and the electromotive force that drives this current is called thermo-electromotive force (thermo-emf). The arrangement of two dissimilar conductors used to produce thermo-emf is called a thermocouple.

Thermoelectric Series

The magnitude and direction of thermo-emf depend on the nature of the metals chosen and the temperature difference between the two junctions. Scientists arranged various metals in a sequence such that when any two metals from the list form a thermocouple, the thermoelectric current at the hot junction flows from the metal occurring earlier in the series to the metal occurring later in the series.

Standard Thermoelectric Series

Sb — Fe — Cd — Zn — Ag — Au — Cr — Mo — Sn — Pb — Hg — Mn — Cu — Pt — Ni — Bi

Rule of Direction: At the hot junction, current flows from the metal earlier in the series to the metal later in the series. For example, in a Copper-Iron (Cu-Fe) thermocouple, since Iron (Fe) precedes Copper (Cu) in the series, current flows from Fe to Cu at the hot junction (and from Cu to Fe at the cold junction).

Origin of Seebeck Effect

Every metal contains free electrons whose density differs from one metal to another. When two different metals are brought into contact, electrons diffuse from the metal with higher free-electron density to the one with lower density until a contact potential difference is established across the junction.

When both junctions are kept at the same temperature, the potential differences at both junctions are equal and opposite, resulting in zero net emf. However, when the two junctions are at different temperatures, the contact potential difference at the hot junction becomes greater than that at the cold junction. This potential imbalance produces a net electromotive force (thermo-emf) in the circuit.

15.2 Thermocouple as a Temperature Measuring Device

To use a thermocouple as a thermometer, the cold junction is maintained at a constant reference temperature (usually ice temperature \(T_c = 0^\circ\text{C}\)), while the hot junction is placed in the medium whose temperature \(T_h\) is to be measured.

Variation of Thermo-emf with Temperature

Experimentally, as the temperature of the hot junction \(T_h\) is gradually raised while holding the cold junction at \(T_c = 0^\circ\text{C}\), the thermo-emf \(E\) varies according to a parabolic relation:

Thermo-emf Equation

E = α T + ½ β T2

Where α and β are thermoelectric constants depending on the nature of the two metals forming the thermocouple, and T = Th - Tc is the temperature difference.

Key Critical Temperatures

  1. Neutral Temperature (Tn): The temperature of the hot junction at which the thermo-emf reaches its maximum value (Emax). At Tn, the rate of change of thermo-emf with temperature is zero (dE/dT = 0).
    Important Note: Neutral temperature is a constant property for a given pair of metals and is completely independent of the cold junction temperature Tc.
  2. Temperature of Inversion (Ti): The temperature of the hot junction at which the thermo-emf becomes zero and reverses its sign upon further heating. Unlike Tn, Ti depends on the cold junction temperature Tc.

Mathematical Relation Between Tc, Tn, and Ti

Differentiating the thermo-emf equation E = α T + ½ β T2 with respect to temperature difference T:

dE / dT = α + β T

At neutral temperature (T = Tn - Tc), thermo-emf is maximum, so dE/dT = 0:

0 = α + β (Tn - Tc) &implies; Tn - Tc = -α / β

Since the parabola is symmetrical about the neutral temperature Tn, Tn lies exactly halfway between Tc and Ti:

Symmetry Relation

Tn - Tc = Ti - Tn &implies; Tn = (Tc + Ti) / 2

Ti = 2Tn - Tc

Thermoelectric Power (Seebeck Coefficient)

Thermoelectric Power (S) or Seebeck coefficient is defined as the rate of change of thermo-emf with respect to temperature difference:

S = dE / dT = α + β T

At neutral temperature (Th = Tn), the thermoelectric power becomes zero (S = 0). Beyond Tn, thermoelectric power becomes negative.

15.3 Peltier Effect

In 1834, French physicist Jean Charles Athanase Peltier discovered the exact inverse of the Seebeck effect. When an electric current is passed through a junction formed by two dissimilar metals, heat is either absorbed or evolved at the junction, depending on the direction of the electric current. This phenomenon is known as the Peltier Effect.

The Peltier effect is strictly a junction phenomenon and is entirely reversible.

Figure 15.3: Peltier Effect at Cu-Fe junctions when external electric current is applied.

Peltier Coefficient (π)

The Peltier Coefficient (π) of a junction is defined as the amount of heat energy absorbed or evolved at the junction when unit electric charge passes through it.

π = (Heat energy H) / (Total charge q) = H / (I · t)

S.I. Unit: Joule per Coulomb (J/C) or Volts (V).

Comparison: Peltier Effect vs. Joule Effect

Property Peltier Effect Joule Effect (H = I2Rt)
Nature Reversible phenomenon Irreversible phenomenon
Location Occurs strictly at junctions of dissimilar metals Occurs throughout the bulk conductor
Thermal Behavior Heat can be absorbed or evolved depending on current direction Heat is always evolved regardless of current direction
Current Dependency Proportional to current (H ∝ I) Proportional to square of current (H ∝ I2)

15.4 Thermopile

A Thermopile is a sensitive device used for detecting and measuring minute quantities of heat radiation (thermal radiation). It operates on the principle of the Seebeck effect.

Construction

A thermopile consists of a large number of thermocouples (usually 20 to 50 pairs) made of Antimony (Sb) and Bismuth (Bi) strips connected in series to increase the net thermo-emf.

  • The strips are insulated from one another by thin sheets of mica or varnish.
  • Alternate junctions are arranged on two opposite faces (Face A and Face B).
  • The active junctions on Face A are coated with lampblack to absorb maximum thermal radiation.
  • The reference junctions on Face B are shielded and protected by a metal cover to remain at constant room temperature.
  • A conical reflector horn is attached in front of Face A to concentrate incoming heat radiation.

Figure 15.4: Construction and circuit layout of a Thermopile with Sb-Bi series junctions.

Working Principle

When thermal radiation falls on Face A through the conical reflector, lampblack absorbs the radiation rapidly and raises the temperature of Face A junctions (Th). Face B remains at ambient room temperature (Tc). This temperature difference generates a thermo-emf across each Sb-Bi pair.

Since n thermocouples are connected in series, the total thermo-emf generated is Etotal = n · E. This cumulative emf produces a measurable deflection in the sensitive micro-ammeter connected across the terminals. The galvanometer deflection is directly proportional to the intensity of incident thermal radiation.

15.5 Thomson Effect

In 1854, Scottish physicist William Thomson (Lord Kelvin) concluded theoretically and verified experimentally that heat is absorbed or evolved along the length of a single homogeneous metal conductor when an electric current passes through it while different parts of the conductor are maintained at different temperatures. This is known as the Thomson Effect.

1. Positive Thomson Effect

A metal is said to exhibit a Positive Thomson Effect if heat is absorbed when current flows from the colder end to the hotter end, and heat is evolved when current flows from the hotter end to the colder end.

Examples: Copper (Cu), Antimony (Sb), Silver (Ag), Zinc (Zn), Cadmium (Cd).

2. Negative Thomson Effect

A metal is said to exhibit a Negative Thomson Effect if heat is evolved when current flows from the colder end to the hotter end, and heat is absorbed when current flows from the hotter end to the colder end.

Examples: Iron (Fe), Bismuth (Bi), Platinum (Pt), Nickel (Ni), Cobalt (Co).

3. Zero Thomson Effect

In Lead (Pb), no heat absorption or evolution takes place along its length when current passes through it under a temperature gradient. Hence, Lead exhibits Zero Thomson Effect and is used as a standard reference metal in thermoelectricity.

Thomson Coefficient (σ)

The Thomson Coefficient (σ) is defined as the amount of heat energy absorbed or evolved per unit charge when it flows through a conductor between two points having a temperature difference of 1°C.

σ = dH / (q · dT) = dH / (I · dt · dT)

S.I. Unit: Volt per Kelvin (V/K).

15.6 NEB Board Exam Solved Numericals

Example 1 (NEB Board Model) Topic: Neutral & Inversion Temperature

Question: The thermo-emf E (in microvolts, μV) of a thermocouple is given by E = 16 T - 0.04 T2, where T is the temperature difference between the hot and cold junctions in °C. If the cold junction is kept at 0°C, calculate: (a) Neutral Temperature (Tn), (b) Temperature of Inversion (Ti), and (c) Thermoelectric power at 100°C.

Solution:

Given equation: E = 16 T - 0.04 T2 (μV)

Comparing with standard equation E = α T + ½ β T2:

α = 16 μV/°C, ½ β = -0.04 &implies; β = -0.08 μV/°C2.

(a) Neutral Temperature (Tn):

At neutral temperature, dE/dT = 0.

dE/dT = 16 - 0.08 T = 0 &implies; 0.08 Tn = 16 &implies; Tn = 16 / 0.08 = 200°C

(b) Temperature of Inversion (Ti):

Using symmetry formula Ti = 2 Tn - Tc, with Tc = 0°C:

Ti = 2(200°C) - 0°C = 400°C

(c) Thermoelectric Power (S) at T = 100°C:

S = dE/dT = 16 - 0.08(100) = 16 - 8 = 8 μV/°C

Example 2 (NEB Board Past Question) Topic: Shifted Cold Junction Temperature

Question: The neutral temperature of a thermocouple is 270°C when the cold junction is at 15°C. Find the temperature of inversion. If the cold junction temperature is raised to 25°C, what will be the new neutral temperature and inversion temperature?

Solution:

Case I: Tn = 270°C, Tc = 15°C.

Using relation Ti = 2 Tn - Tc:

Ti = 2(270) - 15 = 540 - 15 = 525°C

Case II: Cold junction temperature raised to Tc' = 25°C.

  • Neutral temperature Tn depends ONLY on the nature of metals forming the thermocouple. Thus, Tn remains unchanged at 270°C.
  • New Inversion Temperature Ti' = 2 Tn - Tc':
    Ti' = 2(270) - 25 = 540 - 25 = 515°C

15.7 Conceptual Short Questions & Answers

Q1: Why is Lead (Pb) chosen as the standard reference metal in thermoelectricity?

Answer: Lead (Pb) exhibits zero Thomson effect (σ = 0) across a wide temperature range. When lead is used as one arm of a thermocouple, the thermoelectric properties measured correspond solely to the other metal, making lead an ideal standard reference element.

Q2: Can a thermocouple be used to measure very high temperatures, such as inside a furnace?

Answer: Yes. Thermocouples made of high-melting-point metals (e.g., Platinum vs. Platinum-Rhodium alloy) can measure temperatures up to 1600°C accurately, provided the operating temperature remains well below the inversion temperature Ti.

Q3: Differentiate fundamentally between Peltier heating and Joule heating.

Answer: (1) Joule heating occurs throughout the bulk of a conductor, whereas Peltier heating occurs strictly at the junction of two dissimilar metals.
(2) Joule heating (H = I2Rt) is completely irreversible and always evolves heat. Peltier heating is reversible; reversing current changes heat evolution into heat absorption.
(3) Joule heat is proportional to I2, whereas Peltier heat is proportional to I.

Q4: Why does thermo-emf decrease and reverse beyond neutral temperature?

Answer: Beyond neutral temperature (Tn), the Thomson effect and temperature-dependent internal contact potentials act in opposition to the initial Seebeck potential gradient. As a result, net thermo-emf decreases, becomes zero at Ti, and reverses direction for T > Ti.
