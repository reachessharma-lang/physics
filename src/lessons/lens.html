17.1 Properties of Spherical Lenses

A lens is a transparent refracting medium bounded by two surfaces, at least one of which is a curved or spherical surface. Depending on its shape, a lens may converge or diverge light.

Spherical lens: A spherical lens is a transparent refracting medium bounded by two spherical surfaces, or by one spherical surface and one plane surface.

Types of Spherical Lenses

Spherical lenses are broadly classified into two types:

  • Convex lens: It is thicker at the centre and thinner at the edges. It generally converges parallel rays of light and is therefore called a converging lens.
  • Concave lens: It is thinner at the centre and thicker at the edges. It generally diverges parallel rays of light and is therefore called a diverging lens.

Important Terms Related to a Spherical Lens

1. Principal Axis

The straight line passing through the centres of curvature of the two spherical surfaces of a lens and the optical centre is called the principal axis of the lens.

2. Optical Centre

The point inside a thin lens through which a ray of light passes approximately without deviation is called the optical centre \(O\).

3. Centres of Curvature

The centres of the spheres of which the two refracting surfaces of the lens form parts are called the centres of curvature \(C_1\) and \(C_2\).

4. Radius of Curvature

The distance between a pole of a spherical refracting surface and its centre of curvature is called the radius of curvature. The two radii are represented by \(R_1\) and \(R_2\).

5. Principal Focus

The point on the principal axis at which rays parallel to the principal axis actually converge, or from which they appear to diverge after refraction, is called the principal focus.

A thin lens has two principal foci, \(F_1\) and \(F_2\), one on either side of the lens.

  • For a convex lens, the principal focus is real.
  • For a concave lens, the principal focus is virtual.

6. Focal Length

The distance between the optical centre \(O\) and the principal focus is called the focal length.

\[ \boxed{f=OF} \]
  • For a convex lens, \(f>0\).
  • For a concave lens, \(f<0\).

7. Focal Plane

The plane passing through the principal focus and perpendicular to the principal axis is called the focal plane.

Position of Important Terms on a Convex Lens

Position of Important Terms on a Concave Lens

Standard Ray Rules for a Convex Lens

  1. A ray parallel to the principal axis, after refraction, passes through the second principal focus \(F_2\).
  2. A ray passing through the first principal focus \(F_1\), after refraction, emerges parallel to the principal axis.
  3. A ray passing through the optical centre \(O\) passes approximately undeviated.

Standard Ray Rules for a Concave Lens

  1. A ray parallel to the principal axis, after refraction, diverges as if it came from the first principal focus \(F_1\).
  2. A ray directed towards the second principal focus \(F_2\), after refraction, emerges parallel to the principal axis.
  3. A ray passing through the optical centre \(O\) passes approximately undeviated.

17.2 Relation Between Object Distance, Image Distance and Focal Length of a Convex Lens

The relation between object distance \(u\), image distance \(v\), and focal length \(f\) of a thin lens is called the lens formula.

Using the real-positive and virtual-negative convention used throughout these notes:

\[ \boxed{\frac{1}{f}=\frac{1}{u}+\frac{1}{v}} \]

This form is valid for convex and concave lenses and for both real and virtual images, provided the signs are used consistently.

Sign Convention Used for Lens Formula

Quantity Sign
Real object \(u>0\)
Virtual object \(u<0\)
Real image \(v>0\)
Virtual image \(v<0\)
Convex lens focal length \(f>0\)
Concave lens focal length \(f<0\)

Examination warning: Do not mix this convention with the Cartesian convention in which a real object distance is written as negative. In these notes, a real object distance is positive and a virtual image distance is negative.

Case I: Convex Lens Forming a Real Image

Consider a convex lens with optical centre \(O\), principal focus \(F_2\), and an object \(AB\) placed beyond the focal point on the left side of the lens.

A ray \(BD\) parallel to the principal axis is refracted through \(F_2\). Another ray \(BO\) passes through the optical centre and continues approximately undeviated. The two refracted rays meet at \(B'\), forming the real inverted image \(A'B'\).

Geometrical Derivation for a Convex Lens Forming a Real Image

Let \(AB\) be the object and \(A'B'\) be the real image. Let \(D\) be a point on the lens at the height of \(B\). Since \(BD\) is parallel to the principal axis, \(OD=AB\).

From similar triangles \(ABO\) and \(A'B'O\),

\[ \frac{AB}{A'B'}=\frac{AO}{A'O} \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\frac{AB}{A'B'}=\frac{u}{v}} \tag{1} \]

Now consider the triangles \(ODF_2\) and \(A'B'F_2\). They are similar.

\[ \frac{OD}{A'B'}=\frac{OF_2}{F_2A'} \]

Since \(OD=AB\), \(OF_2=f\), and

\[ F_2A'=A'O-OF_2=v-f \]

we obtain

\[ \boxed{\frac{AB}{A'B'}=\frac{f}{v-f}} \tag{2} \]

Equating equations (1) and (2):

\[ \frac{u}{v}=\frac{f}{v-f} \]

Cross-multiplying:

\[ u(v-f)=vf \]
\[ uv-uf=vf \]
\[ uv=uf+vf \]

Dividing by \(uvf\):

\[ \boxed{\frac{1}{f}=\frac{1}{u}+\frac{1}{v}} \]

Result for a convex lens forming a real image:

\[ \boxed{\frac{1}{f}=\frac{1}{u}+\frac{1}{v}} \]

Here \(u>0,\;v>0,\;f>0\).

Case II: Convex Lens Forming a Virtual Image

When an object is placed between the optical centre \(O\) and the first focus \(F_1\) of a convex lens, the refracted rays diverge. Their backward extensions meet on the same side of the lens and form a virtual, erect and magnified image.

Geometrical Derivation for a Convex Lens Forming a Virtual Image

Let \(AB\) be the object placed between \(F_1\) and \(O\). The refracted rays diverge and their backward extensions meet at \(B'\), forming the virtual image \(A'B'\).

From similar triangles \(ABO\) and \(A'B'O\),

\[ \frac{AB}{A'B'}=\frac{AO}{A'O} \]

Since the image is virtual, \(v\) is negative. Therefore, the magnitude of the image distance is \(-v\).

\[ \boxed{\frac{AB}{A'B'}=\frac{u}{-v}} \tag{3} \]

Now consider the triangles formed using the refracted ray and its backward extension through \(F_2\). They are similar, giving

\[ \frac{AB}{A'B'}= \frac{OF_2}{F_2A'} \]

Since \(OF_2=f\) and \(A'\) is on the left side of the lens,

\[ F_2A'=f-v \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{ \frac{AB}{A'B'}=\frac{f}{f-v} } \tag{4} \]

Equating equations (3) and (4):

\[ \frac{u}{-v}=\frac{f}{f-v} \]

Cross-multiplying:

\[ u(f-v)=-vf \]
\[ uf-uv=-vf \]
\[ uf=uv-vf \]

Rearranging:

\[ uv=uf+vf \]

Dividing by \(uvf\):

\[ \boxed{\frac{1}{f}=\frac{1}{u}+\frac{1}{v}} \]

Result for a convex lens forming a virtual image:

\[ \boxed{\frac{1}{f}=\frac{1}{u}+\frac{1}{v}} \]

Here \(u>0,\;v<0,\;f>0\).

Case III: Concave Lens Forming a Virtual Image

A concave lens always forms a virtual, erect and diminished image for a real object placed in front of it.

A ray parallel to the principal axis diverges after refraction and appears to originate from the first principal focus \(F_1\). A ray through the optical centre \(O\) passes approximately undeviated. The backward extensions of the refracted rays meet at \(B'\).

Geometrical Derivation for a Concave Lens

Let \(AB\) be a real object placed in front of a concave lens and \(A'B'\) its virtual image.

From similar triangles \(ABO\) and \(A'B'O\),

\[ \frac{AB}{A'B'}=\frac{AO}{A'O} \]

Since \(A'\) is on the object side, the image is virtual and therefore \(v<0\). Hence the magnitude of \(A'O\) is \(-v\).

\[ \boxed{\frac{AB}{A'B'}=\frac{u}{-v}} \tag{5} \]

Now consider the triangles formed by the refracted ray and its backward extension through \(F_1\). These triangles are similar.

\[ \frac{AB}{A'B'}=\frac{CF}{A'F} \]

For a concave lens, the focus is virtual and therefore

\[ f<0 \]

The magnitude of the focal length is \(-f\). Since \(A'\) lies between \(O\) and \(F_1\), the corresponding geometric relation is

\[ \boxed{ \frac{AB}{A'B'}= \frac{-f}{v-f} } \tag{6} \]

Equating equations (5) and (6):

\[ \frac{u}{-v}=\frac{-f}{v-f} \]

Cross-multiplying:

\[ u(v-f)=vf \]
\[ uv-uf=vf \]
\[ uv=uf+vf \]

Dividing by \(uvf\):

\[ \boxed{\frac{1}{f}=\frac{1}{u}+\frac{1}{v}} \]

Result for a concave lens:

\[ \boxed{\frac{1}{f}=\frac{1}{u}+\frac{1}{v}} \]

For a real object and virtual image, \(u>0,\;v<0,\;f<0\).

Why Is the Concave-Lens Image Always Virtual?

A concave lens causes parallel incident rays to diverge after refraction. The refracted rays do not actually meet. Only their backward extensions meet on the same side of the lens as the object.

Therefore, the image is:

  • virtual,
  • erect,
  • diminished, and
  • formed between the optical centre and the principal focus.

Comparison of the Three Lens-Formula Cases

Case Lens \(u\) \(v\) \(f\) Nature of image
Real image Convex Positive Positive Positive Real, inverted
Virtual image Convex Positive Negative Positive Virtual, erect, magnified
Virtual image Concave Positive Negative Negative Virtual, erect, diminished

Most important examination result: Although the geometrical construction is different for the three cases, the final lens formula is the same:

\[ \boxed{\frac{1}{f}=\frac{1}{u}+\frac{1}{v}} \]

Linear Magnification of a Lens

Linear magnification is the ratio of the height of the image to the height of the object.

\[ \boxed{m=\frac{h_i}{h_o}} \]

Using the real-positive and virtual-negative convention:

\[ \boxed{m=-\frac{v}{u}} \]

The negative sign indicates an inverted image, while a positive value indicates an erect image.

  • Real inverted image: \(m<0\).
  • Virtual erect image: \(m>0\).
  • If \(|m|>1\), the image is magnified.
  • If \(|m|<1\), the image is diminished.
  • If \(|m|=1\), the image and object are of the same size.

Worked Example 1: Convex Lens Forming a Real Image

Question: An object is placed \(30\,\text{cm}\) in front of a convex lens of focal length \(20\,\text{cm}\). Find the image distance and magnification.

Using the real-positive convention:

\[ u=30\,\text{cm},\qquad f=20\,\text{cm} \]

From the lens formula:

\[ \frac{1}{f}=\frac{1}{u}+\frac{1}{v} \]
\[ \frac{1}{v} = \frac{1}{20}-\frac{1}{30} = \frac{1}{60} \]
\[ \boxed{v=60\,\text{cm}} \]

Since \(v>0\), the image is real.

Magnification:

\[ m=-\frac{v}{u} =-\frac{60}{30} =-2 \]

Answer: The image is real, inverted and twice the size of the object.

Worked Example 2: Convex Lens Forming a Virtual Image

Question: An object is placed \(10\,\text{cm}\) from a convex lens of focal length \(15\,\text{cm}\). Find the image distance and magnification.

\[ u=10\,\text{cm},\qquad f=15\,\text{cm} \]
\[ \frac{1}{v} = \frac{1}{15}-\frac{1}{10} = -\frac{1}{30} \]
\[ \boxed{v=-30\,\text{cm}} \]

The negative image distance shows that the image is virtual and lies on the object side of the lens.

Magnification:

\[ m=-\frac{v}{u} = -\frac{-30}{10} = 3 \]

Answer: The image is virtual, erect and three times magnified.

Worked Example 3: Concave Lens

Question: A real object is placed \(30\,\text{cm}\) in front of a concave lens of focal length \(15\,\text{cm}\). Find the image distance and magnification.

For a concave lens:

\[ u=30\,\text{cm},\qquad f=-15\,\text{cm} \]

Using the lens formula:

\[ \frac{1}{-15} = \frac{1}{30} + \frac{1}{v} \]
\[ \frac{1}{v} = -\frac{1}{15}-\frac{1}{30} = -\frac{3}{30} = -\frac{1}{10} \]
\[ \boxed{v=-10\,\text{cm}} \]

Therefore, the image is virtual and lies \(10\,\text{cm}\) in front of the lens.

Magnification:

\[ m=-\frac{v}{u} = -\frac{-10}{30} = \frac{1}{3} \]

Answer: The image is virtual, erect and diminished to one-third of the object size.

17.3 Visual Angle and Angular Magnification

Visual Angle

When an object is viewed by the eye, the angle subtended by the object at the eye is called the visual angle.

Visual angle: The angle subtended by an object at the eye is called its visual angle.

If an object of height \(h\) is at a distance \(D\) from the eye, then

\[ \tan\theta=\frac{h}{D} \]

For a small visual angle,

\[ \boxed{\theta\approx\frac{h}{D}} \]

where \(\theta\) is measured in radians.

Angular Magnification

Angular magnification is the ratio of the angle subtended at the eye by the image to the angle subtended by the object when viewed directly from the same position.

\[ \boxed{M=\frac{\beta}{\alpha}} \]

where \(\beta\) is the visual angle subtended by the image and \(\alpha\) is the visual angle subtended by the object when viewed directly.

Angular Magnification of a Simple Microscope

A convex lens used to obtain a magnified virtual image of a small object is called a simple microscope or magnifying glass.

When the final image is formed at the least distance of distinct vision \(D\), the angular magnification is

\[ \boxed{M=1+\frac{D}{f}} \]

For a normal adult eye,

\[ \boxed{D=25\,\text{cm}} \]

Worked Example 4: Angular Magnification

Question: A convex lens of focal length \(5\,\text{cm}\) is used as a magnifying glass. Find its angular magnification when the final image is formed at the least distance of distinct vision. Take \(D=25\,\text{cm}\).

\[ M=1+\frac{D}{f} \]
\[ M=1+\frac{25}{5} \]
\[ \boxed{M=6} \]

Answer: The angular magnification is \(6\).

17.4 Lens Maker's Formula and Determination of Focal Length

The focal length of a lens depends upon the refractive index of the lens material and the radii of curvature of its two refracting surfaces.

Lens maker's formula: The relation between the focal length of a lens, refractive index of its material, and the radii of curvature of its two surfaces is called the lens maker's formula.

For a thin lens in air, using signed radii of curvature consistent with the real-positive and virtual-negative convention:

\[ \boxed{ \frac{1}{f} = (\mu-1) \left( \frac{1}{R_1}-\frac{1}{R_2} \right) } \]

Here \(\mu\) is the refractive index of the lens material with respect to the surrounding medium, and \(R_1\) and \(R_2\) are the signed radii of curvature of the first and second surfaces.

Sign of the Radii of Curvature

Light is assumed to travel from left to right.

  • If the centre of curvature of a surface lies to the right of that surface, its radius is positive.
  • If the centre of curvature lies to the left of that surface, its radius is negative.

Therefore, for a typical biconvex lens:

\[ R_1>0,\qquad R_2<0 \]

and for a typical biconcave lens:

\[ R_1<0,\qquad R_2>0 \]

Geometrical Idea Behind Lens Maker's Formula

A thin lens may be considered as a large number of very small-angle prisms placed together. A ray passing through the central portion of the lens experiences almost no deviation, while rays farther from the principal axis are deviated more strongly.

Derivation of Lens Maker's Formula

Consider a thin convex lens of refractive index \(\mu\) placed in air. Let the radii of curvature of its first and second surfaces be \(R_1\) and \(R_2\).

For a paraxial ray incident parallel to the principal axis at a small height \(h\), the first refracting surface produces a small deviation. Applying the refraction formula at the two spherical surfaces and adding the effects of both surfaces gives the net optical power of the thin lens.

For the first spherical surface, let the refracted intermediate image be formed at distance \(v_1\). The spherical-surface refraction relation gives

\[ \frac{\mu}{v_1}-\frac{1}{u} = \frac{\mu-1}{R_1} \]

For the second surface, the intermediate image acts as a virtual object for the second refracting surface. For a thin lens, the distance between the two surfaces can be neglected.

The refraction relation at the second surface is

\[ \frac{1}{v} - \frac{\mu}{u_2} = \frac{1-\mu}{R_2} \]

Since the intermediate image is a virtual object for the second surface,

\[ u_2=-v_1 \]

Therefore,

\[ \frac{1}{v} + \frac{\mu}{v_1} = \frac{1-\mu}{R_2} \]

From the first surface equation,

\[ \frac{\mu}{v_1} = \frac{1}{u} + \frac{\mu-1}{R_1} \]

Substituting this into the second-surface equation:

\[ \frac{1}{v} + \frac{1}{u} + \frac{\mu-1}{R_1} = \frac{1-\mu}{R_2} \]

Since

\[ 1-\mu=-(\mu-1) \]

we obtain

\[ \frac{1}{u}+\frac{1}{v} = (\mu-1) \left( \frac{1}{R_1}-\frac{1}{R_2} \right) \]

But for a thin lens,

\[ \frac{1}{u}+\frac{1}{v}=\frac{1}{f} \]

Hence,

\[ \boxed{ \frac{1}{f} = (\mu-1) \left( \frac{1}{R_1}-\frac{1}{R_2} \right) } \]

Lens maker's formula in air:

\[ \boxed{ \frac{1}{f} = (\mu-1) \left( \frac{1}{R_1}-\frac{1}{R_2} \right) } \]

Lens Maker's Formula for a Concave Lens

The same formula applies to a concave lens. For a typical biconcave lens,

\[ R_1<0,\qquad R_2>0 \]

Therefore,

\[ \frac{1}{R_1}-\frac{1}{R_2}<0 \]

Since \(\mu-1>0\),

\[ \frac{1}{f}<0 \]

Hence,

\[ \boxed{f<0} \]

This correctly shows that a concave lens has negative focal length under the real-positive and virtual-negative convention.

Determination of Focal Length

From the lens maker's formula, the focal length can be written as

\[ \boxed{ f= \frac{1}{ (\mu-1) \left( \frac{1}{R_1}-\frac{1}{R_2} \right) } } \]

Thus, if the refractive index and radii of curvature are known, the focal length can be calculated.

Worked Example 5: Focal Length of a Biconvex Lens

Question: A biconvex glass lens has refractive index \(1.5\). The radii of curvature of its two surfaces are \(20\,\text{cm}\) and \(20\,\text{cm}\). Find its focal length.

Using the real-positive convention for signed radii:

\[ \mu=1.5,\qquad R_1=+20\,\text{cm},\qquad R_2=-20\,\text{cm} \]

Using the lens maker's formula:

\[ \frac{1}{f} = (1.5-1) \left( \frac{1}{20}-\frac{1}{-20} \right) \]
\[ \frac{1}{f} = 0.5 \left( \frac{2}{20} \right) = \frac{1}{20} \]
\[ \boxed{f=20\,\text{cm}} \]

Answer: The focal length is \(20\,\text{cm}\).

Worked Example 6: Focal Length of a Biconcave Lens

Question: A biconcave lens has refractive index \(1.5\), with radii of curvature \(20\,\text{cm}\) and \(20\,\text{cm}\). Find its focal length.

For a biconcave lens:

\[ R_1=-20\,\text{cm},\qquad R_2=+20\,\text{cm} \]

Therefore,

\[ \frac{1}{f} = 0.5 \left( -\frac{1}{20}-\frac{1}{20} \right) \]
\[ \frac{1}{f} = -\frac{1}{20} \]
\[ \boxed{f=-20\,\text{cm}} \]

Answer: The focal length is \(-20\,\text{cm}\), confirming that the concave lens is a diverging lens.

Lens Maker's Formula in a Medium

If a lens is placed in a medium of refractive index \(\mu_m\), the refractive index of the lens relative to the surrounding medium is \(\mu/\mu_m\).

Therefore, the general lens maker's formula is

\[ \boxed{ \frac{1}{f} = \left( \frac{\mu}{\mu_m}-1 \right) \left( \frac{1}{R_1}-\frac{1}{R_2} \right) } \]

Common Mistakes in Examination

  • Do not draw a concave lens as a converging lens. A concave lens must make a parallel beam diverge.
  • For a concave lens, the backward extension of a refracted parallel ray must pass through the first focus \(F_1\).
  • Do not draw the dashed backward extension as an actual light ray. Actual light travels only in the forward direction.
  • At the optical centre, the ray should be drawn approximately straight and undeviated.
  • For a real object, use \(u>0\) in the convention used here.
  • For a virtual image, use \(v<0\).
  • For a convex lens, \(f>0\).
  • For a concave lens, \(f<0\).
  • For a real inverted image, \(m<0\); for a virtual erect image, \(m>0\).
  • Do not use the Cartesian convention \(u<0\) together with the real-positive convention.
  • In the lens maker's formula, do not forget that the two radii are signed quantities.
  • For a typical biconvex lens, \(R_1>0\) and \(R_2<0\).
  • For a typical biconcave lens, \(R_1<0\) and \(R_2>0\).
  • Remember that the concave lens always forms a virtual, erect and diminished image for a real object.
  • For a convex lens, the image may be real or virtual depending on the position of the object.
