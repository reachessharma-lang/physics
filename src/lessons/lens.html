--- title: Refraction Through Lenses grade: XI chapters: [17] date: 2026-10-08 ---

A lens is one of the most useful optical devices. Spectacles, cameras, microscopes, telescopes, projectors and the human eye all depend on lenses. This chapter explains how a spherical lens bends light, how the positions of object and image are related, how a magnifying glass increases the visual angle, how the focal length depends on the lens material and its curved surfaces, and how two thin lenses in contact combine.

Sign convention used in these notes (real is positive, virtual is negative):

  • Distances are measured from the optical centre \(O\) of the lens.
  • A real object or image has a positive distance. A virtual object or image has a negative distance.
  • Object distance \(u\) is positive for a real object (the usual case).
  • Image distance \(v\) is positive for a real image (opposite side of the lens from the object). \(v\) is negative for a virtual image (same side as the object).
  • Focal length \(f\) is positive for a convex lens and negative for a concave lens.

17.1 Properties of Spherical Lenses

What is a lens?

Definition: A lens is a piece of transparent refracting material (glass or plastic) bounded by two surfaces, of which at least one is spherical.

Types of lenses

  • Convex (converging) lens: thicker at the centre than at the edges. Examples are biconvex, plano-convex and concavo-convex (meniscus).
  • Concave (diverging) lens: thinner at the centre than at the edges. Examples are biconcave, plano-concave and convexo-concave.

Important terms

  • Optical centre (O): the point inside the lens such that a ray passing through it goes straight without deviation (for a thin lens).
  • Principal axis: the straight line through the optical centre and the centres of curvature of both surfaces.
  • Centre of curvature (C): the centre of the sphere of which the lens surface is a part. Radius of curvature (R) is the radius of that sphere.
  • Principal focus (F): for a convex lens, the point on the principal axis where rays parallel to the axis meet after refraction (a real focus). For a concave lens, the point from which such rays appear to diverge (a virtual focus).
  • Focal length (f): the distance from the optical centre to the principal focus.
  • Aperture: the diameter of the lens surface that is exposed to light.
  • Power: \(P = \dfrac{1}{f}\), with \(f\) in metres. The unit is the dioptre (D). A convex lens has positive power and a concave lens has negative power.

Properties of spherical lenses

  1. A convex lens is converging. A concave lens is diverging.
  2. Every lens has two principal foci, one on each side. For a thin lens with the same medium on both sides, the two focal lengths are equal.
  3. A ray passing through the optical centre goes straight without deviation (thin lens).
  4. A ray parallel to the principal axis passes through the focus F′ (convex) or appears to come from the focus F (concave).
  5. A ray passing through the first focus F of a convex lens emerges parallel to the principal axis.
  6. Focal length depends on the refractive index of the lens material, the radii of curvature of its surfaces, the surrounding medium and the colour of the light.
  7. A convex lens can form real or virtual images. A concave lens always forms a virtual, upright, diminished image.

Rules for drawing ray diagrams (convex lens)

Any two of these three rays are enough to locate the image of a point on the object. The image point is where the refracted rays meet. For a virtual image it is where they appear to meet when traced backwards (dashed lines).

  1. A ray from the object parallel to the principal axis passes through F′ after refraction.
  2. A ray through the optical centre O goes straight.
  3. A ray through F emerges parallel to the principal axis.

17.2 Relation between Object Distance, Image Distance and Focal Length

Lens formula:

\[ \frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{u} + \frac{1}{v} \]

Here \(u\) is the object distance, \(v\) the image distance and \(f\) the focal length, all measured from the optical centre. Use the sign convention given at the start (real positive, virtual negative). The same formula works for a concave lens, with \(f\) negative.

Assumptions for all three derivations: the lens is thin, its aperture is small, the object is small and perpendicular to the principal axis, and the rays are close to the axis (paraxial). The geometrical method uses two rays from the top point \(A\) of the object: a ray parallel to the principal axis and a ray through the optical centre \(O\). Because the lens is thin, we treat refraction as happening at a single plane through \(O\), perpendicular to the axis.

(i) Convex lens: when a real image is formed

Setup. Let \(AB\) be a small object of height \(h\), perpendicular to the principal axis of a thin convex lens, at distance \(BO = u\) from the optical centre \(O\). \(F\) and \(F'\) are the principal foci and \(OF' = f\). The real, inverted image is \(A'B'\) of height \(h'\), at distance \(OB' = v\) on the other side of the lens.

Construction. From \(A\), draw ray \(AD\) parallel to the principal axis, meeting the lens plane at \(D\). After refraction it passes through the focus \(F'\) and reaches \(A'\). Draw ray \(AO\) through the optical centre. It goes straight and also reaches \(A'\).

Proof.

Step 1. In triangles \(ABO\) and \(A'B'O\): \(\angle ABO = \angle A'B'O = 90^\circ\), and \(\angle AOB = \angle A'OB'\) (vertically opposite angles, since \(A\), \(O\), \(A'\) lie on one straight line). So the triangles are similar:

\[ \frac{AB}{A'B'} = \frac{BO}{B'O} \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{h}{h'} = \frac{u}{v} \quad \text{... (i)} \]

Step 2. \(AD\) is parallel to \(BO\) and \(AB\) is parallel to \(DO\), so \(ABOD\) is a rectangle. Hence \(DO = AB = h\).

Step 3. In triangles \(DOF'\) and \(A'B'F'\): \(\angle DOF' = \angle A'B'F' = 90^\circ\), and \(\angle DF'O = \angle A'F'B'\) (vertically opposite angles, since \(D\), \(F'\), \(A'\) lie on one straight line). So these triangles are also similar:

\[ \frac{DO}{A'B'} = \frac{OF'}{B'F'} \]

Here \(OF' = f\) and \(B'F' = OB' - OF' = v - f\), so

\[ \frac{h}{h'} = \frac{f}{v-f} \quad \text{... (ii)} \]

Step 4. From (i) and (ii),

\[ \frac{u}{v} = \frac{f}{v-f} \;\Rightarrow\; u(v-f) = fv \;\Rightarrow\; uv - uf = fv \]

Step 5. Divide every term by \(uvf\):

\[ \frac{1}{f} - \frac{1}{v} = \frac{1}{u} \] \[ \boxed{\frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{u} + \frac{1}{v}} \]

This is the lens formula for a real image (\(u\), \(v\), \(f\) all positive). Hence proved.

(ii) Convex lens: when a virtual image is formed

Setup. The object \(AB\) is placed between the optical centre \(O\) and the principal focus \(F\) (\(u < f\)). A virtual, upright, magnified image \(A'B'\) forms on the same side of the lens as the object. Let its distance be \(OB' = v\) (taken as a magnitude for the geometry).

Construction. From \(A\), draw ray \(AD\) parallel to the axis. After refraction it passes through \(F'\) (continuing as a diverging ray). Draw ray \(AO\) through the optical centre; it goes straight. The two refracted rays diverge, so they do not meet. Extend them backwards (dashed lines). They meet at \(A'\). Draw \(A'B'\) perpendicular to the axis.

Proof.

Step 1. In triangles \(ABO\) and \(A'B'O\): \(\angle ABO = \angle A'B'O = 90^\circ\) and the angle at \(O\) is common (\(B\), \(B'\) lie on the same side along the axis, and \(O\), \(A\), \(A'\) are collinear). So the triangles are similar:

\[ \frac{AB}{A'B'} = \frac{BO}{B'O} \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{h}{h'} = \frac{u}{v} \quad \text{... (i)} \]

Step 2. As before, \(ABOD\) is a rectangle, so \(DO = AB = h\).

Step 3. In triangles \(DOF'\) and \(A'B'F'\): \(\angle DOF' = \angle A'B'F' = 90^\circ\), and the angle at \(F'\) is common (\(D\), \(A'\), \(F'\) are collinear). So the triangles are similar:

\[ \frac{DO}{A'B'} = \frac{OF'}{B'F'} \]

The point \(B'\) is on the object side, so \(B'F' = B'O + OF' = v + f\). Therefore

\[ \frac{h}{h'} = \frac{f}{v+f} \quad \text{... (ii)} \]

Step 4. From (i) and (ii),

\[ \frac{u}{v} = \frac{f}{v+f} \;\Rightarrow\; u(v+f) = fv \;\Rightarrow\; uv + uf = fv \]

Step 5. Divide every term by \(uvf\):

\[ \frac{1}{f} + \frac{1}{v} = \frac{1}{u} \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{u} - \frac{1}{v} \quad \text{(with } v \text{ as a magnitude)} \]

Step 6 (apply the sign convention). The image is virtual, so by the convention its distance is negative: replace \(v\) by \(-v\).

\[ \boxed{\frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{u} + \frac{1}{v}} \]

This is the same lens formula. For a virtual image the value of \(v\) comes out negative. Hence proved.

(iii) Concave lens (virtual image)

Setup. Let \(AB\) be a small object of height \(h\) at distance \(BO = u\) from a thin concave lens. \(F\) is the (virtual) principal focus on the object side and \(OF = f\) (magnitude). A concave lens always gives a virtual, upright, diminished image \(A'B'\), between \(O\) and \(F\), on the same side as the object. Let \(OB' = v\) (magnitude).

Construction. From \(A\), draw ray \(AD\) parallel to the axis. After refraction it diverges along \(DE\), which appears to come from the focus \(F\). Draw ray \(AO\) through the optical centre; it goes straight. Extend the diverging ray backwards (dashed line \(DF\)). It meets the line \(AO\) at \(A'\). Draw \(A'B'\) perpendicular to the axis.

Proof.

Step 1. Triangles \(ABO\) and \(A'B'O\) are similar (\(\angle ABO = \angle A'B'O = 90^\circ\), common angle at \(O\)):

\[ \frac{AB}{A'B'} = \frac{BO}{B'O} \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{h}{h'} = \frac{u}{v} \quad \text{... (i)} \]

Step 2. \(ABOD\) is a rectangle, so \(DO = AB = h\).

Step 3. Triangles \(FOD\) and \(FB'A'\) are similar (\(\angle FOD = \angle FB'A' = 90^\circ\), common angle at \(F\), since \(F\), \(A'\), \(D\) are collinear):

\[ \frac{DO}{A'B'} = \frac{FO}{FB'} \]

The image lies between \(O\) and \(F\), so \(FB' = FO - B'O = f - v\). Therefore

\[ \frac{h}{h'} = \frac{f}{f-v} \quad \text{... (ii)} \]

Step 4. From (i) and (ii),

\[ \frac{u}{v} = \frac{f}{f-v} \;\Rightarrow\; u(f-v) = fv \;\Rightarrow\; uf - uv = fv \]

Step 5. Divide every term by \(uvf\):

\[ \frac{1}{v} - \frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{u} \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{v} - \frac{1}{u} \quad \text{(} f \text{ and } v \text{ as magnitudes)} \]

Step 6 (apply the sign convention). The image is virtual, so \(v \to -v\). The focus of a concave lens is virtual, so \(f \to -f\):

\[ \frac{1}{-f} = \frac{1}{-v} - \frac{1}{u} \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{u} + \frac{1}{v} \] \[ \boxed{\frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{u} + \frac{1}{v}} \]

The same formula holds for a concave lens. With the convention, \(f\) and \(v\) come out negative. Hence proved.

Linear (lateral) magnification

Definition: Linear magnification is the ratio of the height of the image to the height of the object.

\[ m = \frac{h'}{h} = \frac{v}{u} \quad (\text{magnitudes}) \]

This comes directly from equation (i) in each derivation above. Combining it with the lens formula gives \( m = \dfrac{f}{u-f} = \dfrac{v-f}{f} \) for a convex lens.

  • If \(v\) is positive (real image), the image is inverted.
  • If \(v\) is negative (virtual image), the image is upright.
  • \(m > 1\) means the image is magnified. \(m < 1\) means it is diminished.

Power of a lens

\[ P = \frac{1}{f\,(\text{in metres})} \ \text{dioptre (D)} \]

A lens of focal length \(+25\ \text{cm}\) has power \(+4\ \text{D}\). A lens of focal length \(-50\ \text{cm}\) has power \(-2\ \text{D}\).

17.3 Visual Angle and Angular Magnification

Visual angle

Definition: The visual angle is the angle subtended at the eye by the object, that is, the angle between the rays from the two extreme points of the object to the eye.

For an object of height \(h\) at distance \(d\) from the eye, with a small angle,

\[ \alpha \approx \tan\alpha = \frac{h}{d} \]

The apparent size of an object depends on its visual angle, not only on its actual size. A bigger visual angle gives a bigger image on the retina, so the object looks larger and more detail is seen. The nearest distance at which a normal eye can see clearly without strain is the near point, also called the least distance of distinct vision, \(D = 25\ \text{cm}\).

Angular magnification (magnifying power)

Definition: Angular magnification is the ratio of the visual angle subtended at the eye by the image formed by the optical instrument (\(\beta\)) to the visual angle subtended by the object at the eye when seen directly at the near point (\(\alpha\)).

\[ M = \frac{\beta}{\alpha} \]

Simple microscope (magnifying glass)

A convex lens used as a magnifier is held very close to the eye, and the object is placed inside the focal length (\(u < f\)). It forms a virtual, upright, magnified image.

Derivation. Without the lens, the object of height \(h\) is placed at the near point \(D\), so

\[ \alpha = \frac{h}{D} \]

With the lens, the object is at distance \(u\) from the lens and the eye is close to the lens. The image subtends the same angle at the lens as the object does:

\[ \beta = \frac{h}{u} \]

So the angular magnification is

\[ M = \frac{\beta}{\alpha} = \frac{D}{u} \]

Case 1: final image at the near point (\(v = -D\)). The image is virtual, so \(v\) is negative. The lens formula gives

\[ \frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{u} + \frac{1}{(-D)} \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{1}{u} = \frac{1}{f} + \frac{1}{D} \] \[ M = \frac{D}{u} = D\left(\frac{1}{f} + \frac{1}{D}\right) = \boxed{1 + \frac{D}{f}} \]

This is the maximum magnification. The eye is most strained here.

Case 2: final image at infinity (\(u = f\)).

\[ M = \frac{D}{u} = \boxed{\frac{D}{f}} \]

This is the "normal adjustment". The eye is relaxed.

A shorter focal length gives a larger magnifying power. Notice that the case 1 value is always exactly 1 more than the case 2 value.

17.4 Lens Maker's Formula

Lens maker's formula: for a thin lens of refractive index \(n\) placed in air,

\[ \frac{1}{f} = (n-1)\left(\frac{1}{R_1} - \frac{1}{R_2}\right) \]

For a biconvex lens, with \(R_1\) and \(R_2\) taken as magnitudes (positive numbers), this becomes

\[ \frac{1}{f} = (n-1)\left(\frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2}\right) \]

Assumptions: the lens is thin, the aperture is small, the rays are close to the principal axis (paraxial), and the object is a point on the axis.

Step 1: refraction at a single spherical surface

Light from a point object \(O\) in a medium of refractive index \(n_1\) strikes a convex spherical surface of vertex \(P\), centre of curvature \(C\) and radius \(R\), and enters a medium of index \(n_2\) (\(n_2 > n_1\)). It forms an image at \(I\). Let the ray \(OM\) strike the surface at a small height \(MN\) above the axis, where \(N\) is the foot of the perpendicular from \(M\). Let \(\alpha\), \(\beta\), \(\gamma\) be the angles that \(OM\), \(IM\) and \(CM\) make with the axis. Let \(i\) and \(r\) be the angles of incidence and refraction at \(M\).

For small angles, \(\tan\theta \approx \theta\), and \(N\) is very close to \(P\), so \(ON \approx OP = u\), \(NI \approx PI = v\) and \(NC \approx PC = R\):

\[ \alpha \approx \frac{MN}{u},\qquad \beta \approx \frac{MN}{v},\qquad \gamma \approx \frac{MN}{R} \]

The exterior angle of a triangle equals the sum of the two opposite interior angles. In triangle \(OMC\): \(i = \alpha + \gamma\). In triangle \(MCI\): \(\gamma = r + \beta\), so \(r = \gamma - \beta\).

Snell's law, \(n_1 \sin i = n_2 \sin r\), becomes \(n_1 i = n_2 r\) for small angles:

\[ n_1(\alpha+\gamma) = n_2(\gamma-\beta) \;\Rightarrow\; n_1\alpha + n_2\beta = (n_2-n_1)\gamma \]

Substitute the angles and cancel \(MN\):

\[ \boxed{\frac{n_1}{u} + \frac{n_2}{v} = \frac{n_2-n_1}{R}} \quad \text{... (A)} \]

In this form \(u\) and \(v\) follow the real-is-positive convention. \(R\) is positive when its centre of curvature lies on the side where the light goes after refraction (the emergent side), that is, for a surface convex towards the incident light.

Step 2: ray diagram 1, refraction at the first surface

Let the thin lens have refractive index \(n\) and be in air. Let a point object \(O\) be on the principal axis at distance \(u\) from the lens. Face 1 has radius \(R_1\) and centre \(C_1\). Consider face 1 alone (ignore face 2 for the moment). It would form an image \(I_1\) at distance \(v_1\) in the glass.

Apply (A) with \(n_1 = 1\), \(n_2 = n\), object distance \(u\), image distance \(v_1\) and radius \(R_1\):

\[ \frac{1}{u} + \frac{n}{v_1} = \frac{n-1}{R_1} \quad \text{... (1)} \]

Step 3: ray diagram 2, refraction at the second surface

The image \(I_1\) lies beyond face 2, so for face 2 it is a virtual object. By the real-positive, virtual-negative convention, its distance is \(-v_1\). The lens is thin, so distances are measured from the same point \(P\). Light now goes from glass (\(n_1 = n\)) to air (\(n_2 = 1\)). Face 2 forms the final real image \(I\) at distance \(v\).

The centre of curvature \(C_2\) of face 2 is on the incident side. In formula (A), where \(R\) is positive for a centre on the emergent side, the radius of face 2 is therefore \(-R_2\) (with \(R_2\) as the magnitude). Apply (A) with \(n_1 = n\), \(n_2 = 1\), object distance \(-v_1\), image distance \(v\):

\[ \frac{n}{-v_1} + \frac{1}{v} = \frac{1-n}{-R_2} \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{1}{v} - \frac{n}{v_1} = \frac{n-1}{R_2} \quad \text{... (2)} \]

Step 4: add the two equations

Adding (1) and (2), the terms with \(v_1\) cancel:

\[ \frac{1}{u} + \frac{1}{v} = (n-1)\left(\frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2}\right) \]

When the object is at infinity (\(u = \infty\)), the image forms at the focus (\(v = f\)):

\[ \boxed{\frac{1}{f} = (n-1)\left(\frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2}\right)} \quad \text{(biconvex, } R_1, R_2 \text{ as magnitudes)} \]

For a general lens, use the sign rule \(R > 0\) if the centre of curvature is on the emergent side. Then \(\dfrac{1}{f} = (n-1)\left(\dfrac{1}{R_1} - \dfrac{1}{R_2}\right)\). Hence proved.

Special cases

  • Equiconvex lens (\(R_1 = R_2 = R\)): \( \dfrac{1}{f} = \dfrac{2(n-1)}{R}\). For \(n = 1.5\), \(f = R\).
  • Plano-convex lens (one face flat, \(R_2 = \infty\)): \( \dfrac{1}{f} = \dfrac{n-1}{R}\). For \(n = 1.5\), \(f = 2R\).
  • Biconcave lens: the centres of curvature are on opposite sides to those of a biconvex lens, so \(f\) comes out negative (diverging).
  • Lens in a medium: replace \(n\) by the relative index \( n_{\text{lens}}/n_{\text{medium}} \). A glass convex lens in water has a longer focal length than in air.

The formula also shows that \(f\) depends on the colour of the light, because \(n\) is slightly larger for violet than for red.

17.5 Two Thin Lenses in Contact

Result: Two thin lenses of focal lengths \(f_1\) and \(f_2\) placed in contact behave like a single thin lens of focal length \(F\), where

\[ \frac{1}{F} = \frac{1}{f_1} + \frac{1}{f_2} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad F = \frac{f_1 f_2}{f_1 + f_2} \]

and the power of the combination is the sum of the individual powers: \(P = P_1 + P_2\).

Setup. Let \(L_1\) and \(L_2\) be two thin lenses in contact on a common principal axis. Because they are thin and touching, both can be taken to have the same optical centre, and distances are measured from it. A point object \(O\) is on the axis at distance \(u\). The lenses are drawn slightly apart in the figure only for clarity.

Derivation.

Step 1. Lens \(L_1\) alone forms an image \(I_1\) of the object \(O\) at distance \(v_1\). By the lens formula,

\[ \frac{1}{f_1} = \frac{1}{u} + \frac{1}{v_1} \quad \text{... (i)} \]

Step 2. The rays are intercepted by \(L_2\) before they reach \(I_1\). So \(I_1\) acts as a virtual object for \(L_2\). By the real-positive, virtual-negative convention, its object distance for \(L_2\) is \(-v_1\). \(L_2\) forms the final image \(I\) at distance \(v\):

\[ \frac{1}{f_2} = \frac{1}{-v_1} + \frac{1}{v} = -\frac{1}{v_1} + \frac{1}{v} \quad \text{... (ii)} \]

Step 3. Add (i) and (ii). The terms in \(v_1\) cancel:

\[ \frac{1}{f_1} + \frac{1}{f_2} = \frac{1}{u} + \frac{1}{v} \quad \text{... (iii)} \]

Step 4. Suppose the pair is replaced by a single thin lens of focal length \(F\) that forms the final image of the same object at the same place. Then, by the lens formula,

\[ \frac{1}{F} = \frac{1}{u} + \frac{1}{v} \quad \text{... (iv)} \]

Step 5. Compare (iii) and (iv):

\[ \boxed{\frac{1}{F} = \frac{1}{f_1} + \frac{1}{f_2}} \quad\Rightarrow\quad F = \frac{f_1 f_2}{f_1 + f_2} \]

Since power is \(P = 1/f\), we get

\[ \boxed{P = P_1 + P_2} \]

Hence proved.

Remarks

  • The result holds for any combination if \(f\) is given its sign: positive for a convex lens, negative for a concave lens.
  • For \(n\) thin lenses in contact, \( \dfrac{1}{F} = \dfrac{1}{f_1} + \dfrac{1}{f_2} + \dots + \dfrac{1}{f_n}\) and \(P = P_1 + P_2 + \dots + P_n\).
  • The magnification of the combination is the product of the individual magnifications: \( m = m_1 \times m_2 = \dfrac{v_1}{u} \times \dfrac{v}{v_1} = \dfrac{v}{u} \).
  • A convex lens in contact with a weaker concave lens gives a weaker convex combination. This is how a lens of the required power is built from two lenses.

Worked Examples

Example 1: convex lens, real image

An object is placed 20 cm from a convex lens of focal length 15 cm. Find the position, nature and magnification of the image.

Solution. \(u = 20\ \text{cm}\), \(f = +15\ \text{cm}\).

\[ \frac{1}{v} = \frac{1}{f} - \frac{1}{u} = \frac{1}{15} - \frac{1}{20} = \frac{4-3}{60} = \frac{1}{60} \]

So \(v = +60\ \text{cm}\). The image is real, inverted, and on the opposite side of the lens from the object.

\[ m = \frac{v}{u} = \frac{60}{20} = 3 \]

The image is magnified 3 times. This matches the table, since \(f < u < 2f\) (15 cm < 20 cm < 30 cm).

Example 2: convex lens, virtual image

An object is placed 10 cm from a convex lens of focal length 15 cm. Find the image distance and nature.

Solution. \(u = 10\ \text{cm}\), \(f = +15\ \text{cm}\).

\[ \frac{1}{v} = \frac{1}{15} - \frac{1}{10} = \frac{2-3}{30} = -\frac{1}{30} \]

So \(v = -30\ \text{cm}\). The negative sign means the image is virtual, on the same side as the object, 30 cm from the lens.

\[ m = \frac{|v|}{u} = \frac{30}{10} = 3 \]

The image is virtual, upright and magnified 3 times. This is the magnifying-glass situation, since \(u < f\).

Example 3: concave lens

A concave lens has focal length 20 cm. An object is 30 cm from it. Find the image position and magnification.

Solution. \(u = 30\ \text{cm}\), \(f = -20\ \text{cm}\).

\[ \frac{1}{v} = \frac{1}{f} - \frac{1}{u} = -\frac{1}{20} - \frac{1}{30} = -\frac{3+2}{60} = -\frac{5}{60} = -\frac{1}{12} \]

So \(v = -12\ \text{cm}\). The image is virtual, upright, on the object side, 12 cm from the lens.

\[ m = \frac{|v|}{u} = \frac{12}{30} = 0.4 \]

The image is diminished to 0.4 times the object size.

Example 4: lens maker's formula

A biconvex lens is made of glass of refractive index 1.5. Its faces have radii of curvature 10 cm and 20 cm. Find its focal length and power.

Solution. \(n = 1.5\), \(R_1 = 10\ \text{cm}\), \(R_2 = 20\ \text{cm}\).

\[ \frac{1}{f} = (1.5-1)\left(\frac{1}{10} + \frac{1}{20}\right) = 0.5 \times \frac{3}{20} = \frac{3}{40} \]

So \(f = \dfrac{40}{3} = 13.3\ \text{cm}\), or \(0.133\ \text{m}\).

\[ P = \frac{1}{0.133} = 7.5\ \text{D} \]

Example 5: plano-convex lens

A plano-convex lens of refractive index 1.5 has a curved surface of radius 15 cm. Find its focal length.

Solution. One face is flat (\(R_2 = \infty\)), so \(\dfrac{1}{R_2} = 0\).

\[ \frac{1}{f} = (1.5-1)\left(\frac{1}{15}\right) = \frac{0.5}{15} = \frac{1}{30} \;\Rightarrow\; f = 30\ \text{cm} \]

This agrees with the special case \(f = 2R\) for \(n = 1.5\).

Example 6: equiconvex lens, find the radius

An equiconvex lens of refractive index 1.5 has focal length 20 cm. Find the radius of curvature of each surface.

Solution.

\[ \frac{1}{20} = (1.5-1)\left(\frac{2}{R}\right) = \frac{1}{R} \;\Rightarrow\; R = 20\ \text{cm} \]

Example 7: simple microscope

A convex lens of focal length 5 cm is used as a magnifying glass. Find the angular magnification (a) when the final image is at the near point (\(D = 25\ \text{cm}\)) and (b) when it is at infinity.

Solution.

(a) \( M = 1 + \dfrac{D}{f} = 1 + \dfrac{25}{5} = 6 \)

The object distance for this case is found from \( \dfrac{1}{u} = \dfrac{1}{5} + \dfrac{1}{25} = \dfrac{6}{25} \), which gives \(u = 4.17\ \text{cm}\). Check: \( M = \dfrac{D}{u} = \dfrac{25}{4.17} = 6 \). ✓

(b) \( M = \dfrac{D}{f} = \dfrac{25}{5} = 5 \)

Example 8: two convex lenses in contact

Two thin convex lenses of focal lengths 20 cm and 30 cm are placed in contact. Find the focal length and power of the combination.

Solution. \(f_1 = +20\ \text{cm}\), \(f_2 = +30\ \text{cm}\).

\[ \frac{1}{F} = \frac{1}{20} + \frac{1}{30} = \frac{3+2}{60} = \frac{5}{60} = \frac{1}{12} \;\Rightarrow\; F = +12\ \text{cm} \]

Power: \( P = \dfrac{1}{0.12} = 8.33\ \text{D} \). Check: \(P_1 = 5\ \text{D}\), \(P_2 = 3.33\ \text{D}\), and \(5 + 3.33 = 8.33\ \text{D}\). ✓

Example 9: convex and concave lens in contact

A convex lens of focal length 25 cm is placed in contact with a concave lens of focal length 40 cm. Find the focal length and nature of the combination.

Solution. \(f_1 = +25\ \text{cm}\), \(f_2 = -40\ \text{cm}\).

\[ \frac{1}{F} = \frac{1}{25} - \frac{1}{40} = \frac{8-5}{200} = \frac{3}{200} \;\Rightarrow\; F = +66.7\ \text{cm} \]

\(F\) is positive, so the combination is convex (converging). Power: \(P = +4 - 2.5 = +1.5\ \text{D}\). ✓

Example 10: finding the second lens

A convex lens of power +4 D is combined with a second lens to give a combination of power +2 D. Find the power and focal length of the second lens.

Solution. \(P = P_1 + P_2\), so

\[ P_2 = P - P_1 = 2 - 4 = -2\ \text{D} \] \[ f_2 = \frac{1}{P_2} = \frac{1}{-2} = -0.5\ \text{m} = -50\ \text{cm} \]

The second lens is concave, with focal length 50 cm.

Common Mistakes

  • Forgetting that a virtual image has negative \(v\). If your answer for \(v\) is negative, the image is on the same side as the object and is virtual.
  • Using a positive \(f\) for a concave lens. A concave lens has \(f\) negative, so power is also negative.
  • Mixing units. Keep \(u\), \(v\), \(f\) in the same unit (cm or m). Convert \(f\) to metres only for power in dioptres.
  • In the derivation for a virtual image, writing \(B'F' = v - f\) instead of \(v + f\) (convex lens), or \(FB' = v - f\) instead of \(f - v\) (concave lens). Look at where \(B'\) lies relative to \(F\) before writing the distance.
  • Stopping the derivation at the magnitude form. For virtual images, always state the final step where \(v \to -v\) (and \(f \to -f\) for a concave lens) to reach \(\dfrac{1}{f} = \dfrac{1}{u} + \dfrac{1}{v}\).
  • Confusing linear magnification \(m = v/u\) with angular magnification \(M = \beta/\alpha\). They are equal only for a simple magnifier with the image at the near point.
  • Writing \(M = 1 + D/f\) when the image is at infinity. Use \(M = D/f\) for image at infinity and \(M = 1 + D/f\) for image at the near point.
  • Using the lens maker's formula with the wrong signs for \(R_1\) and \(R_2\). For a biconvex lens, add the two reciprocals. For a plano-convex lens, \(1/R_2 = 0\).
  • Forgetting that \(I_1\) is a virtual object (distance \(-v_1\)) for the second surface or second lens. This is why the \(v_1\) terms cancel when the equations are added.
  • For lenses in contact, adding the focal lengths. It is the powers (or the reciprocals of the focal lengths) that add.
  • In ray diagrams: forgetting arrowheads on the rays, drawing real rays dashed (only virtual extensions are dashed), or having refracted rays that do not pass through F′ (ray parallel to the axis) or go straight through O.
  • Saying a virtual image can be caught on a screen. Only a real image can.

Key Points Summary

  • A convex lens is converging (thicker in the middle, \(f > 0\)). A concave lens is diverging (thinner in the middle, \(f < 0\)).
  • Sign convention: real is positive, virtual is negative. The same convention is used for \(u\), \(v\) and \(f\).
  • Three standard rays: parallel to the axis then through F′; through O undeviated; through F then parallel to the axis.
  • Lens formula: \( \dfrac{1}{f} = \dfrac{1}{u} + \dfrac{1}{v} \). It is derived from two pairs of similar triangles: \(\dfrac{h}{h'} = \dfrac{u}{v}\) (rays through \(O\)) and \(\dfrac{h}{h'} = \dfrac{f}{v-f}\) (convex, real), \(\dfrac{f}{v+f}\) (convex, virtual) or \(\dfrac{f}{f-v}\) (concave).
  • Linear magnification: \( m = \dfrac{h'}{h} = \dfrac{v}{u} \). A real image is inverted. A virtual image is upright.
  • A convex lens gives a virtual, upright, magnified image only when \(u < f\). A concave lens always gives a virtual, upright, diminished image.
  • Power: \( P = \dfrac{1}{f(\text{m})} \) in dioptres.
  • Visual angle: \( \alpha \approx h/d \), the angle subtended by the object at the eye. The near point is \(D = 25\ \text{cm}\).
  • Angular magnification: \( M = \beta/\alpha \). For a simple microscope, \( M = 1 + \dfrac{D}{f} \) (image at near point) and \( M = \dfrac{D}{f} \) (image at infinity).
  • Lens maker's formula: \( \dfrac{1}{f} = (n-1)\left(\dfrac{1}{R_1} + \dfrac{1}{R_2}\right) \) for a biconvex lens in air. For an equiconvex lens with \(n = 1.5\), \(f = R\). For a plano-convex lens with \(n = 1.5\), \(f = 2R\).
  • The lens maker's formula comes from refraction at a single spherical surface, \( \dfrac{n_1}{u} + \dfrac{n_2}{v} = \dfrac{n_2-n_1}{R} \), applied twice. The first image \(I_1\) acts as a virtual object for the second surface.
  • Two thin lenses in contact: \( \dfrac{1}{F} = \dfrac{1}{f_1} + \dfrac{1}{f_2} \), \( F = \dfrac{f_1 f_2}{f_1 + f_2} \) and \( P = P_1 + P_2 \). The image of the first lens is a virtual object for the second. Total magnification is \(m = m_1 m_2\).
