17.1 Properties of Spherical Lenses

A spherical lens is a transparent refracting medium bounded by two surfaces, at least one of which is spherical. The surfaces may be convex or concave.

Lens: A transparent optical medium bounded by two refracting surfaces, at least one of which is spherical, is called a lens.

Types of Spherical Lenses

Spherical lenses are mainly of two types:

  • Convex lens: It is thicker at the centre than at the edges and generally converges a parallel beam of light. It is therefore called a converging lens.
  • Concave lens: It is thinner at the centre than at the edges and generally diverges a parallel beam of light. It is therefore called a diverging lens.

Important Terms Related to a Spherical Lens

1. Principal Axis

The straight line passing through the optical centre and the centres of curvature of the two spherical surfaces is called the principal axis of the lens.

2. Optical Centre

The point inside a thin lens through which a ray of light passes approximately without deviation is called the optical centre \(O\).

3. Centres of Curvature

The centres of the spheres of which the two refracting surfaces of a spherical lens form parts are called the centres of curvature \(C_1\) and \(C_2\).

4. Radii of Curvature

The distances from the poles of the refracting surfaces to their respective centres of curvature are called the radii of curvature, represented by \(R_1\) and \(R_2\).

5. Principal Focus

The principal focus is the point on the principal axis at which rays parallel to the principal axis converge, or from which they appear to diverge, after refraction through the lens.

A thin lens has two principal foci, \(F_1\) and \(F_2\), situated on opposite sides of the lens.

  • For a convex lens, parallel rays actually converge at the principal focus.
  • For a concave lens, parallel rays appear to diverge from the principal focus.

6. Focal Length

The distance between the optical centre \(O\) and either principal focus is called the focal length of the lens.

\[ \boxed{f=OF} \]

For a converging convex lens, \(f\) is positive. For a diverging concave lens, \(f\) is negative under the real-positive, virtual-negative convention used in these notes.

Ray Rules for a Thin Convex Lens

The following standard rays are used to construct images formed by a convex lens.

  1. A ray parallel to the principal axis passes through the second principal focus \(F_2\) after refraction.
  2. A ray passing through the first principal focus \(F_1\) emerges parallel to the principal axis.
  3. A ray passing through the optical centre \(O\) passes approximately undeviated.

17.2 Relation Between Object Distance, Image Distance and Focal Length of a Convex Lens

For a thin convex lens, the relation between object distance, image distance and focal length is called the lens formula.

Lens formula using the real-positive, virtual-negative convention:

\[ \boxed{\frac{1}{f}=\frac{1}{u}+\frac{1}{v}} \]

where \(u\) is the object distance, \(v\) is the image distance and \(f\) is the focal length.

Sign Convention Used in These Notes

For consistency with the requested NEB-style real-positive, virtual-negative convention:

  • Distance of a real object from the lens: \(u>0\).
  • Distance of a virtual object: \(u<0\).
  • Distance of a real image from the lens: \(v>0\).
  • Distance of a virtual image: \(v<0\).
  • Focal length of a convex lens: \(f>0\).
  • Focal length of a concave lens: \(f<0\).
  • For a convex lens, the radius of curvature of the first surface is positive and that of the second surface is negative when light travels from left to right.

Important: Do not mix this real-positive convention with the Cartesian sign convention in which a real object distance is negative. The numerical signs and the form of the lens formula must be used consistently.

Formation of a Real Image by a Convex Lens

When a real object is placed beyond the principal focus of a convex lens, the refracted rays actually meet on the opposite side of the lens and form a real, inverted image.

For this case:

\[ u>0,\qquad v>0,\qquad f>0 \]

Therefore,

\[ \frac{1}{f}=\frac{1}{u}+\frac{1}{v} \]

Formation of a Virtual Image by a Convex Lens

When the object is placed between the optical centre and the principal focus of a convex lens, the refracted rays diverge. Their backward extensions meet on the object side, producing a virtual, erect and magnified image.

For this case:

\[ u>0,\qquad v<0,\qquad f>0 \]

and the lens formula remains

\[ \boxed{\frac{1}{f}=\frac{1}{u}+\frac{1}{v}} \]

Useful Rearrangements

From

\[ \frac{1}{f}=\frac{1}{u}+\frac{1}{v} \]

we obtain

\[ \boxed{f=\frac{uv}{u+v}} \]

For finding the image distance:

\[ \boxed{v=\frac{uf}{u-f}} \]

For finding the object distance:

\[ \boxed{u=\frac{vf}{v-f}} \]

Magnification of a Lens

The linear magnification \(m\) is defined as the ratio of the height of the image to the height of the object.

\[ \boxed{m=\frac{h_i}{h_o}} \]

With the real-positive, virtual-negative convention, the corresponding relation is

\[ \boxed{m=-\frac{v}{u}} \]

The negative sign indicates an inverted image, while a positive magnification indicates an erect image.

Worked Example 1: Finding Image Distance

Question: An object is placed \(30\,\text{cm}\) from a convex lens of focal length \(20\,\text{cm}\). Find the image distance and state the nature of the image.

Given:

\[ u=30\,\text{cm},\qquad f=20\,\text{cm} \]

Using the lens formula,

\[ \frac{1}{f}=\frac{1}{u}+\frac{1}{v} \]
\[ \frac{1}{v}=\frac{1}{20}-\frac{1}{30} \]
\[ \frac{1}{v}=\frac{3-2}{60}=\frac{1}{60} \]
\[ \boxed{v=60\,\text{cm}} \]

Since \(v>0\), the image is real. Its magnification is

\[ m=-\frac{v}{u}=-\frac{60}{30}=-2 \]

Therefore, the image is real, inverted and twice the size of the object.

Worked Example 2: Virtual Image by a Convex Lens

Question: An object is placed \(10\,\text{cm}\) from a convex lens of focal length \(15\,\text{cm}\). Find the image distance and magnification.

Given:

\[ u=10\,\text{cm},\qquad f=15\,\text{cm} \]

Using

\[ \frac{1}{f}=\frac{1}{u}+\frac{1}{v} \]
\[ \frac{1}{v}=\frac{1}{15}-\frac{1}{10} \]
\[ \frac{1}{v}=\frac{2-3}{30}=-\frac{1}{30} \]
\[ \boxed{v=-30\,\text{cm}} \]

The negative value of \(v\) shows that the image is virtual.

Magnification:

\[ m=-\frac{v}{u} =-\frac{-30}{10} =3 \]

Answer: The image is \(30\,\text{cm}\) in front of the lens, virtual, erect and three times magnified.

17.3 Visual Angle and Angular Magnification

Visual Angle

The visual angle is the angle subtended by an object at the eye when the object is viewed from a particular position.

If an object of height \(h\) is at a distance \(D\) from the eye, the visual angle \(\theta\) is given by

\[ \boxed{\theta=\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{h}{D}\right)} \]

For a small object viewed at a relatively large distance, \(\theta\) is small and

\[ \boxed{\theta\approx\frac{h}{D}} \]

where \(\theta\) is measured in radians.

Angular Magnification

Angular magnification is the ratio of the angle subtended at the eye by the image to the angle subtended by the object when the object is viewed directly, both being considered from the same eye position.

Angular magnification: It is the ratio of the angle subtended by the image at the eye to the angle subtended by the object at the eye when viewed directly.

\[ \boxed{M=\frac{\theta'}{\theta}} \]

where \(\theta'\) is the angle subtended by the image at the eye and \(\theta\) is the angle subtended by the object when viewed directly.

Angular magnification is dimensionless.

Angular Magnification of a Simple Magnifying Glass

A convex lens used to produce an enlarged virtual image of a small object is called a simple microscope or magnifying glass.

When the final image is formed at the least distance of distinct vision \(D\), the angular magnification is

\[ \boxed{M=1+\frac{D}{f}} \]

where \(D\) is the least distance of distinct vision and \(f\) is the focal length of the convex lens.

For a normal adult eye,

\[ \boxed{D\approx25\,\text{cm}} \]

The value \(D=25\,\text{cm}\) is conventionally used for a normal adult eye in NEB numerical problems unless another value is specified.

Worked Example 3: Angular Magnification

Question: A convex lens of focal length \(5\,\text{cm}\) is used as a magnifying glass. Find its angular magnification when the final image is formed at the least distance of distinct vision. Take \(D=25\,\text{cm}\).

For a magnifying glass,

\[ M=1+\frac{D}{f} \]
\[ M=1+\frac{25}{5} \]
\[ \boxed{M=6} \]

Answer: The angular magnification is \(6\).

17.4 Lens Maker's Formula

The focal length of a lens depends on the refractive index of the lens material and the radii of curvature of its two refracting surfaces. The relation connecting these quantities is called the lens maker's formula.

Lens maker's formula for a thin lens in air:

\[ \boxed{ \frac{1}{f} = (\mu-1) \left( \frac{1}{R_1}-\frac{1}{R_2} \right) } \]

Here, \(\mu\) is the refractive index of the lens material relative to air, \(R_1\) and \(R_2\) are the radii of curvature of the first and second surfaces, and \(f\) is the focal length.

Sign Convention for Radii of Curvature

Light is assumed to travel from left to right.

  • For the first surface of a typical biconvex lens, \(R_1>0\).
  • For the second surface of a typical biconvex lens, \(R_2<0\).
  • For a typical biconcave lens, \(R_1<0\) and \(R_2>0\).

Derivation of Lens Maker's Formula

Consider a thin lens of refractive index \(\mu\) placed in air. Let the radii of curvature of the first and second refracting surfaces be \(R_1\) and \(R_2\).

Let the first surface form an intermediate image at distance \(v_1\).

Refraction at the First Surface

For refraction at a spherical surface, using the real-positive and virtual-negative convention:

\[ \frac{\mu}{v_1}-\frac{1}{u} = \frac{\mu-1}{R_1} \]

For the complete lens, let the final image be formed at distance \(v\). Since the lens is thin, the intermediate image formed by the first surface acts as a virtual object for the second surface. Therefore, the object distance for the second surface is

\[ u_2=-v_1 \]

Refraction at the Second Surface

Applying the spherical refracting-surface relation at the second surface:

\[ \frac{1}{v} - \frac{\mu}{u_2} = \frac{1-\mu}{R_2} \]

Since \(u_2=-v_1\),

\[ \frac{1}{v} + \frac{\mu}{v_1} = \frac{1-\mu}{R_2} \]

From the first surface equation,

\[ \frac{\mu}{v_1} = \frac{1}{u} + \frac{\mu-1}{R_1} \]

Substituting this into the equation for the second surface:

\[ \frac{1}{v} + \frac{1}{u} + \frac{\mu-1}{R_1} = \frac{1-\mu}{R_2} \]

Since \(1-\mu=-(\mu-1)\),

\[ \frac{1}{u}+\frac{1}{v} = (\mu-1) \left( \frac{1}{R_1}-\frac{1}{R_2} \right) \]

For a thin lens in air, the lens formula is

\[ \frac{1}{u}+\frac{1}{v}=\frac{1}{f} \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \frac{1}{f} = (\mu-1) \left( \frac{1}{R_1}-\frac{1}{R_2} \right) } \]

Lens Maker's Formula:

\[ \boxed{ \frac{1}{f} = (\mu-1) \left( \frac{1}{R_1}-\frac{1}{R_2} \right) } \]

Physical Meaning of Lens Maker's Formula

The formula shows that the focal length of a lens depends on:

  • the refractive index \(\mu\) of the lens material,
  • the curvature of the first surface represented by \(R_1\), and
  • the curvature of the second surface represented by \(R_2\).

A lens with a smaller magnitude of focal length has greater converging or diverging power.

\[ \boxed{P=\frac{1}{f}} \]

when \(f\) is expressed in metres and \(P\) is expressed in dioptres.

Worked Example 4: Finding Focal Length Using Lens Maker's Formula

Question: A biconvex lens has refractive index \(1.5\). Its radii of curvature are \(20\,\text{cm}\) and \(20\,\text{cm}\). Find its focal length in air.

For a biconvex lens, using the real-positive convention:

\[ \mu=1.5,\qquad R_1=+20\,\text{cm},\qquad R_2=-20\,\text{cm} \]

Using the lens maker's formula:

\[ \frac{1}{f} = (\mu-1) \left( \frac{1}{R_1}-\frac{1}{R_2} \right) \]
\[ \frac{1}{f} = (1.5-1) \left( \frac{1}{20}-\frac{1}{-20} \right) \]
\[ \frac{1}{f} = 0.5 \left( \frac{2}{20} \right) = \frac{1}{20} \]
\[ \boxed{f=20\,\text{cm}} \]

Answer: The focal length of the lens is \(20\,\text{cm}\).

Worked Example 5: Finding a Radius of Curvature

Question: A symmetric biconvex glass lens has refractive index \(1.5\) and focal length \(20\,\text{cm}\). Find the magnitude of the radius of curvature of each surface.

For a symmetric biconvex lens:

\[ R_1=R,\qquad R_2=-R \]

Using the lens maker's formula:

\[ \frac{1}{f} = (\mu-1) \left( \frac{1}{R}-\frac{1}{-R} \right) \]
\[ \frac{1}{20} = 0.5\left(\frac{2}{R}\right) \]
\[ \frac{1}{20}=\frac{1}{R} \]
\[ \boxed{R=20\,\text{cm}} \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{R_1=+20\,\text{cm},\qquad R_2=-20\,\text{cm}} \]

Worked Example 6: Effect of Refractive Index

Question: A thin symmetric biconvex lens has \(R_1=+20\,\text{cm}\), \(R_2=-20\,\text{cm}\), and refractive index \(1.6\). Find its focal length in air.

\[ \frac{1}{f} = (1.6-1) \left( \frac{1}{20}-\frac{1}{-20} \right) \]
\[ \frac{1}{f} = 0.6\left(\frac{2}{20}\right) = \frac{0.06}{1} \]
\[ f=\frac{1}{0.06} \approx16.67\,\text{cm} \]

Answer:

\[ \boxed{f\approx16.7\,\text{cm}} \]

The higher refractive index produces a shorter focal length for the same surface curvatures.

Lens Maker's Formula in a Medium

If a lens is placed in a medium whose refractive index is \(\mu_m\), the relative refractive index of the lens material is \(\mu/\mu_m\).

The general lens maker's formula becomes

\[ \boxed{ \frac{1}{f} = \left( \frac{\mu}{\mu_m}-1 \right) \left( \frac{1}{R_1}-\frac{1}{R_2} \right) } \]

For a lens in air, \(\mu_m\approx1\), giving

\[ \boxed{ \frac{1}{f} = (\mu-1) \left( \frac{1}{R_1}-\frac{1}{R_2} \right) } \]

Common Mistakes

  • Do not confuse a convex lens with a concave lens: a convex lens is generally converging and a concave lens is generally diverging.
  • Do not measure the focal length from the lens surface; for a thin lens it is measured from the optical centre.
  • Do not mix sign conventions. These notes use real positive and virtual negative.
  • For a real object, use \(u>0\); for a virtual object, use \(u<0\).
  • For a real image, use \(v>0\); for a virtual image, use \(v<0\).
  • For a convex lens, \(f>0\); for a concave lens, \(f<0\).
  • For a typical biconvex lens with light travelling from left to right, \(R_1>0\) and \(R_2<0\).
  • Do not use \(R_1=R_2\) for a symmetric biconvex lens; the correct signed values are \(R_1=+R\) and \(R_2=-R\).
  • In the lens maker's formula, the second radius appears as \(-R_2\): \(\frac{1}{R_1}-\frac{1}{R_2}\).
  • In a virtual-image problem with a convex lens, \(v\) is negative and the lens equation must still be used consistently.
  • The small-angle approximation \(\tan\theta\approx\theta\) is valid only when \(\theta\) is small and measured in radians.

Key Points Summary

  • A spherical lens has at least one spherical refracting surface.
  • A convex lens is thicker at the centre and is generally converging.
  • A concave lens is thinner at the centre and is generally diverging.
  • The optical centre is the point through which a ray passes approximately without deviation.
  • The focal length is the distance between the optical centre and principal focus.
  • Using the real-positive, virtual-negative convention, the lens formula is \(\displaystyle \frac{1}{f}=\frac{1}{u}+\frac{1}{v}\).
  • For a real object, \(u>0\); for a virtual object, \(u<0\).
  • For a real image, \(v>0\); for a virtual image, \(v<0\).
  • For a convex lens, \(f>0\); for a concave lens, \(f<0\).
  • The linear magnification of a lens is \(\displaystyle m=-\frac{v}{u}\).
  • Visual angle is the angle subtended by an object at the eye.
  • Angular magnification is \(\displaystyle M=\frac{\theta'}{\theta}\).
  • For a simple magnifying glass with the final image at the least distance of distinct vision, \(\displaystyle M=1+\frac{D}{f}\).
  • The lens maker's formula in air is \(\displaystyle \frac{1}{f}=(\mu-1)\left(\frac{1}{R_1}-\frac{1}{R_2}\right)\).
  • For a typical biconvex lens, \(R_1>0\) and \(R_2<0\).
  • The focal length can be determined from the refractive index and the radii of curvature of the lens surfaces.
  • For a lens immersed in another medium, \(\displaystyle \frac{1}{f}=\left(\frac{\mu}{\mu_m}-1\right)\left(\frac{1}{R_1}-\frac{1}{R_2}\right)\).
