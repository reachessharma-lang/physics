24.1 Meaning of Radioactivity

In 1896, Henri Becquerel discovered that uranium salts emit invisible radiations that can pass through opaque materials and affect photographic plates. This spontaneous phenomenon was later named radioactivity by Marie Curie. Radioactivity is the spontaneous emission of high-energy radiation from the unstable nucleus of an atom. Elements that undergo this process are called radioactive elements.

Definition Radioactivity is the spontaneous and random disintegration of an unstable atomic nucleus with the emission of energetic particles or electromagnetic radiation.

Radioactivity is strictly a nuclear phenomenon. It depends entirely on the composition of the nucleus and is unaffected by external physical conditions (like temperature and pressure) or chemical states.

Natural and Artificial Radioactivity

Based on the origin of the instability, radioactivity is classified into two types:

  1. Natural Radioactivity: The spontaneous disintegration of heavy, naturally occurring unstable nuclei (typically elements with atomic number $Z > 82$, such as Uranium, Radium, Thorium, and Polonium) is called natural radioactivity. It occurs without any external intervention.
  2. Artificial (Induced) Radioactivity: When a stable nucleus is bombarded with high-energy particles (like alpha particles, neutrons, or protons), it transforms into an unstable, radioactive isotope. This induced instability is called artificial radioactivity. It was first discovered by Irène Joliot-Curie and Frédéric Joliot in 1934.
    Example: Bombarding Aluminum-27 with alpha particles produces radioactive Phosphorus-30:
    $^{27}_{13}\text{Al} + ^{4}_{2}\text{He} \rightarrow ^{30}_{15}\text{P} + ^{1}_{0}\text{n}$
    The produced $^{30}_{15}\text{P}$ is radioactive and decays by emitting a positron ($\beta^+$).

24.2 Types of Radiations and Their Properties

Ernest Rutherford identified that the radiations emitted by radioactive substances are of three distinct types when passed through a strong electric or magnetic field. They are named alpha ($\alpha$), beta ($\beta$), and gamma ($\gamma$) rays.

1. Alpha ($\alpha$) Particles

  • Nature: Doubly ionized helium nuclei ($^{4}_{2}\text{He}^{2+}$).
  • Charge and Mass: Charge is $+2e$ ($+3.2 \times 10^{-19}$ C) and mass is $4 \text{ u}$ ($6.64 \times 10^{-27}$ kg).
  • Speed: Emitted with speeds around $10^7 \text{ m/s}$ (about 5-10% of the speed of light).
  • Ionizing Power: Very high. They strongly interact with matter, creating thousands of ion pairs per centimeter.
  • Penetrating Power: Very low. They can be stopped by a thin sheet of paper or a few centimeters of air.
  • Deflection: Deflected by electric and magnetic fields, but less than beta particles due to their high mass.

2. Beta ($\beta$) Particles

  • Nature: High-speed electrons ($\beta^-$) or positrons ($\beta^+$) emitted from the nucleus.
  • Charge and Mass: Charge is $-e$ or $+e$ ($\pm 1.6 \times 10^{-19}$ C) and mass is $\frac{1}{1836} \text{ u}$ ($9.1 \times 10^{-31}$ kg).
  • Speed: Emitted with speeds up to $0.9c$ (90% of the speed of light).
  • Ionizing Power: Moderate (about 1/100th of alpha particles).
  • Penetrating Power: Moderate. They can pass through paper but are stopped by a few millimeters of aluminum or plastic.
  • Deflection: Deflected strongly by electric and magnetic fields in the opposite direction to alpha particles (for $\beta^-$).

3. Gamma ($\gamma$) Rays

  • Nature: High-energy electromagnetic waves (photons), similar to X-rays but with higher frequency and shorter wavelength.
  • Charge and Mass: No charge (neutral) and no rest mass.
  • Speed: Travel at the speed of light ($c = 3 \times 10^8 \text{ m/s}$).
  • Ionizing Power: Very low (about 1/10,000th of alpha particles).
  • Penetrating Power: Very high. They require thick lead shielding or several meters of concrete to be significantly attenuated.
  • Deflection: Not deflected by electric or magnetic fields.

24.3 Radioactive Disintegration Law

The radioactive disintegration law, also known as the Rutherford-Soddy Law, describes the mathematical relationship governing the decay of radioactive nuclei.

Statement of the Law The rate of disintegration (decay) of a radioactive substance at any instant is directly proportional to the number of undecayed active nuclei present at that time.

Mathematical Derivation:

Let $N$ be the number of undecayed nuclei at time $t$. According to the law:

$$ \frac{dN}{dt} \propto N $$ $$ \frac{dN}{dt} = -\lambda N $$

Here, $\lambda$ is the constant of proportionality called the decay constant (or disintegration constant). The negative sign indicates that the number of nuclei $N$ is decreasing with time.

Rearranging the equation to integrate:

$$ \frac{dN}{N} = -\lambda \, dt $$

Integrating both sides from $t = 0$ (where $N = N_0$, the initial number of nuclei) to time $t$ (where $N$ is the remaining number of nuclei):

$$ \int_{N_0}^{N} \frac{dN}{N} = -\lambda \int_{0}^{t} dt $$ $$ \ln(N) - \ln(N_0) = -\lambda t $$ $$ \ln\left(\frac{N}{N_0}\right) = -\lambda t $$

Taking the exponential of both sides:

$$ \frac{N}{N_0} = e^{-\lambda t} $$ $$ N(t) = N_0 e^{-\lambda t} $$
Figure 24.1: Exponential Decay Curve of Active Nuclei over Time
Note This equation $N = N_0 e^{-\lambda t}$ is the fundamental equation of radioactive decay. It shows that the number of undecayed nuclei decreases exponentially with time, rather than linearly.

24.4 Half-life, Decay Constant, and Mean Life

1. Decay Constant ($\lambda$)

From the equation $\frac{dN}{dt} = -\lambda N$, we can write:

$$ \lambda = -\frac{dN/dt}{N} = -\frac{1}{N}\frac{dN}{dt} $$

If $N = 1$, then $\lambda = -\frac{dN}{dt}$. Thus, the decay constant is defined as the ratio of the number of nuclei decaying per unit time to the total number of radioactive nuclei present. Its unit is $\text{s}^{-1}$, $\text{min}^{-1}$, $\text{day}^{-1}$, or $\text{year}^{-1}$. A higher $\lambda$ indicates a faster disintegration rate and a shorter half-life.

2. Half-life ($T_{1/2}$)

Definition The half-life of a radioactive substance is the time required for half of the original number of radioactive nuclei to decay.

Derivation:

At $t = T_{1/2}$, the number of undecayed nuclei is $N = \frac{N_0}{2}$. Substituting this into the decay equation:

$$ \frac{N_0}{2} = N_0 e^{-\lambda T_{1/2}} $$ $$ \frac{1}{2} = e^{-\lambda T_{1/2}} $$

Taking the natural logarithm of both sides:

$$ \ln\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) = -\lambda T_{1/2} $$ $$ -\ln(2) = -\lambda T_{1/2} $$ $$ T_{1/2} = \frac{\ln(2)}{\lambda} = \frac{0.693}{\lambda} $$

This shows that the half-life is inversely proportional to the decay constant. A larger $\lambda$ means a faster decay and a shorter half-life.

3. Mean Life ($\tau$)

Definition The mean life (or average life) of a radioactive nucleus is the average time a nucleus exists before it decays. It is the sum of the lifetimes of all individual nuclei divided by the total number of initial nuclei.

Derivation:

Let $dN$ nuclei decay between time $t$ and $t + dt$. The lifetime of each of these nuclei is $t$. The total lifetime of these $dN$ nuclei is $t \cdot |dN|$. Since $dN = -\lambda N dt$, the magnitude is $|dN| = \lambda N dt = \lambda N_0 e^{-\lambda t} dt$.

The total lifetime of all $N_0$ nuclei is:

$$ \text{Total time} = \int_{0}^{\infty} t (\lambda N_0 e^{-\lambda t}) dt = \lambda N_0 \int_{0}^{\infty} t e^{-\lambda t} dt $$

Using integration by parts, $\int_{0}^{\infty} t e^{-\lambda t} dt = \frac{1}{\lambda^2}$. Therefore:

$$ \text{Total time} = \lambda N_0 \left( \frac{1}{\lambda^2} \right) = \frac{N_0}{\lambda} $$

The mean life $\tau$ is the total time divided by the total number of nuclei $N_0$:

$$ \tau = \frac{N_0 / \lambda}{N_0} = \frac{1}{\lambda} $$

Relating mean life to half-life:

$$ \tau = \frac{1}{\lambda} = \frac{T_{1/2}}{0.693} \approx 1.44 \, T_{1/2} $$

24.5 Working of Geiger-Muller Tube

The Geiger-Muller (GM) tube is a widely used instrument for detecting and measuring ionizing radiation (alpha, beta, and gamma rays). It operates on the principle of gas ionization and avalanche multiplication.

Figure 24.2: Line Diagram of Geiger-Müller (G-M) Counter System

Construction

  • It consists of a cylindrical metal tube (usually copper or brass) which acts as the cathode.
  • A thin tungsten wire runs coaxially along the axis of the cylinder, insulated from it, acting as the anode.
  • The tube is filled with an inert gas, typically argon, at a low pressure of about $10 \text{ cm of Hg}$.
  • A small amount of quenching gas (such as ethyl alcohol vapor or a halogen like bromine) is added to the argon.
  • One end of the tube has a thin mica window to allow radiation to enter.
  • A high potential difference (typically $400\text{ V}$ to $500\text{ V}$) is applied between the anode and cathode.

Working Principle

  1. When an ionizing particle (e.g., an alpha or beta particle) enters through the mica window, it collides with the argon atoms and ionizes them, producing electron-ion pairs.
  2. The strong electric field accelerates the freed electrons towards the central anode wire. As they accelerate, they gain enough kinetic energy to ionize other argon atoms they encounter.
  3. This creates a chain reaction known as a Townsend avalanche, resulting in a massive multiplication of electrons (up to $10^{10}$ electrons per initial ionization).
  4. These electrons reach the anode, and the positive ions move slowly towards the cathode, creating a brief pulse of electric current.
  5. The pulse is amplified and counted by an external electronic circuit (scaler or ratemeter), producing a "click" sound or a digital readout.
  6. Role of Quenching Gas: During the avalanche, excited argon atoms emit ultraviolet (UV) photons. If these UV photons hit the cathode, they can eject more electrons, causing a continuous, unwanted discharge. The quenching gas molecules absorb these UV photons (or collide with positive argon ions to neutralize them without ejecting electrons), thereby "quenching" the discharge and ensuring each radiation particle produces only one distinct pulse.

24.6 Radiocarbon Dating ($^{14}\text{C}$ Dating)

Radiocarbon dating is a radiometric age-determination method used to estimate the age of carbon-bearing organic materials up to approximately $50,000 \text{ years}$ old.

Principle

  1. High-energy cosmic rays bombard nitrogen atoms in the upper atmosphere to continuously produce radioactive Carbon-14 ($^{14}\text{C}$):
    $$ ^{14}_{7}\text{N} + ^{1}_{0}\text{n} \rightarrow ^{14}_{6}\text{C} + ^{1}_{1}\text{H} $$
  2. $^{14}\text{C}$ rapidly oxidizes into $^{14}\text{CO}_2$ and mixes into the global atmosphere alongside stable, abundant Carbon-12 ($^{12}\text{CO}_2$).
  3. Living organisms continually exchange carbon with the environment through photosynthesis or food intake, maintaining a constant equilibrium ratio of $\frac{^{14}\text{C}}{^{12}\text{C}}$ in their tissues.
  4. Upon death, the organism ceases carbon exchange. The fixed amount of radioactive $^{14}\text{C}$ decays steadily via $\beta^-$ emission with a half-life of $T_{1/2} \approx 5730 \text{ years}$, whereas stable $^{12}\text{C}$ remains unchanged:
    $$ ^{14}_{6}\text{C} \rightarrow ^{14}_{7}\text{N} + e^- + \bar{\nu}_e $$

Mathematical Derivation of Sample Age

The specific activity $A(t)$ (disintegrations per minute per gram of carbon) of $^{14}\text{C}$ at time $t$ after death follows the decay law:

$$ A(t) = A_0 e^{-\lambda t} $$

Taking the natural logarithm of both sides:

$$ \ln\left(\frac{A}{A_0}\right) = -\lambda t \implies t = \frac{1}{\lambda} \ln\left(\frac{A_0}{A}\right) $$

Substituting the relationship between decay constant and half-life ($\lambda = \frac{\ln 2}{T_{1/2}} = \frac{0.693}{T_{1/2}}$):

$$ t = \frac{T_{1/2}}{\ln 2} \ln\left(\frac{A_0}{A}\right) = \frac{T_{1/2}}{0.693} \ln\left(\frac{A_0}{A}\right) $$

where:

  • $A_0$ = Specific activity of $^{14}\text{C}$ in living organic tissue ($\approx 15.3 \text{ disintegrations/min/g of carbon}$).
  • $A$ = Measured specific activity of $^{14}\text{C}$ in the ancient sample.
  • $T_{1/2}$ = Half-life of Carbon-14 ($5730 \text{ years}$).
  • $t$ = Age of the sample in years.
Detailed Mathematical Example

Problem: An ancient wooden artifact recovered from an archaeological digging site exhibits a Carbon-14 activity of $3.825 \text{ disintegrations/min per gram of carbon}$. Freshly harvested wood shows an activity of $15.3 \text{ disintegrations/min per gram}$. Given the half-life of Carbon-14 is $5730 \text{ years}$, calculate the age of the artifact.

Solution:

Given Data:

  • Initial Activity ($A_0$) = $15.3 \text{ dpm/g}$
  • Measured Activity ($A$) = $3.825 \text{ dpm/g}$
  • Half-life ($T_{1/2}$) = $5730 \text{ years}$

Step 1: Calculate the Decay Constant ($\lambda$)

$$ \lambda = \frac{\ln 2}{T_{1/2}} = \frac{0.69315}{5730 \text{ years}} \approx 1.2097 \times 10^{-4} \text{ year}^{-1} $$

Step 2: Calculate the Age ($t$)

$$ \frac{A}{A_0} = \frac{3.825}{15.3} = 0.25 $$ $$ 0.25 = e^{-\lambda t} \implies \ln(0.25) = -\lambda t $$ $$ -1.3863 = -(1.2097 \times 10^{-4} \text{ year}^{-1}) \times t $$ $$ t = \frac{1.3863}{1.2097 \times 10^{-4}} \approx 11,460 \text{ years} $$

Alternative shortcut verification: Since $\frac{A}{A_0} = 0.25 = \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^2$, exactly 2 half-lives have passed:

$$ t = 2 \times T_{1/2} = 2 \times 5730 = 11,460 \text{ years} $$

Answer: The wooden artifact is approximately 11,460 years old.

24.7 Medical Uses and Health Hazards of Nuclear Radiation

Medical Uses

Nuclear radiation has revolutionized modern medicine in both diagnostics and therapeutics:

  • Radiotherapy (Treatment): High-energy gamma rays from isotopes like Cobalt-60 ($^{60}\text{Co}$) or linear accelerators are directed at cancerous tumors. The radiation damages the DNA of rapidly dividing cancer cells, destroying them while sparing surrounding healthy tissue as much as possible.
  • Diagnostic Radiotracers: Short-lived radioactive isotopes are introduced into the body to track biological processes. For example, Iodine-131 ($^{131}\text{I}$) is used to diagnose and treat thyroid disorders; Technetium-99m ($^{99m}\text{Tc}$) is widely used for bone, heart, and brain imaging.
  • PET Scans: Positron Emission Tomography uses positron-emitting isotopes (like Fluorine-18) to detect metabolic activity, highly effective in identifying cancer metastasis.
  • Sterilization: Gamma rays are used to sterilize surgical instruments, syringes, and bandages that would be damaged by heat sterilization (autoclaving).

Health Hazards

While beneficial in controlled doses, ionizing radiation poses severe health risks due to its ability to ionize atoms in biological tissues:

  • Cellular and DNA Damage: Radiation can break chemical bonds in DNA molecules. If the cell fails to repair this damage, it can lead to mutations, uncontrolled cell division (cancer), or cell death.
  • Acute Radiation Syndrome (ARS): Exposure to very high doses of radiation over a short period causes ARS, characterized by nausea, vomiting, skin burns, hair loss, destruction of bone marrow, and potentially death within days or weeks.
  • Genetic Effects: Damage to the DNA of reproductive cells (sperm or egg) can cause genetic mutations that are passed on to future generations, leading to birth defects or hereditary diseases.
  • Protection Principles: To minimize hazards, radiation workers follow the ALARA principle (As Low As Reasonably Achievable) using Time (minimize exposure time), Distance (maximize distance from the source), and Shielding (use lead aprons, concrete walls, or glass shields).

24.8 Numerical Problems

Example 1

Problem: The half-life of Radon is 3.8 days. After how many days will only $\frac{1}{16}$th of the original amount of Radon remain undecayed?

Solution:

Given: Half-life $T_{1/2} = 3.8 \text{ days}$.

Let the initial amount be $N_0$. The remaining amount is $N = \frac{N_0}{16}$.

We know that the remaining amount after $n$ half-lives is given by:

$$ N = N_0 \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^n $$ $$ \frac{N_0}{16} = N_0 \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^n \implies \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^4 = \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^n $$

Therefore, the number of half-lives $n = 4$.

Total time $t = n \times T_{1/2} = 4 \times 3.8 = 15.2 \text{ days}$.

Answer: $15.2 \text{ days}$.

Example 2

Problem: The decay constant of a radioactive isotope is $1.5 \times 10^{-3} \text{ s}^{-1}$. Calculate its half-life and mean life.

Solution:

Given: Decay constant $\lambda = 1.5 \times 10^{-3} \text{ s}^{-1}$.

Half-life ($T_{1/2}$):

$$ T_{1/2} = \frac{0.693}{\lambda} = \frac{0.693}{1.5 \times 10^{-3}} = 462 \text{ s} $$

Mean life ($\tau$):

$$ \tau = \frac{1}{\lambda} = \frac{1}{1.5 \times 10^{-3}} = 666.67 \text{ s} $$

Answer: Half-life = $462 \text{ s}$, Mean life = $666.67 \text{ s}$.

Example 3

Problem: A radioactive sample has an initial activity of $8 \times 10^6 \text{ Bq}$. If its half-life is 8 days, what will be its activity after 24 days?

Solution:

Given: Initial activity $A_0 = 8 \times 10^6 \text{ Bq}$, $T_{1/2} = 8 \text{ days}$, $t = 24 \text{ days}$.

Number of half-lives $n = \frac{t}{T_{1/2}} = \frac{24}{8} = 3$.

Activity after time $t$ is given by:

$$ A = A_0 \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^n = 8 \times 10^6 \times \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^3 $$ $$ A = 8 \times 10^6 \times \frac{1}{8} = 1 \times 10^6 \text{ Bq} $$

Answer: $1 \times 10^6 \text{ Bq}$.

24.9 Conceptual Questions

Q1: Why is radioactivity considered a nuclear phenomenon and not an atomic one?

Answer: Radioactivity involves the spontaneous emission of particles ($\alpha$, $\beta$) or photons ($\gamma$) originating strictly from the unstable nucleus. The process alters the number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus, changing the element itself. Furthermore, the rate of decay is completely unaffected by external physical conditions (temperature, pressure) or chemical bonding, which only affect the extranuclear electrons. Hence, it is a purely nuclear phenomenon.

Q2: Can a single radioactive nucleus emit an alpha particle and a beta particle simultaneously?

Answer: No. Radioactive decay is a random, independent quantum event for an individual nucleus. A single unstable nucleus will undergo one specific decay mode (e.g., alpha decay OR beta decay) at a time to transition to a more stable state. It cannot split its decay process to emit multiple different primary particles simultaneously.

Q3: Why are gamma rays not deflected by electric and magnetic fields?

Answer: Gamma rays are high-energy electromagnetic waves (photons) and carry absolutely no electric charge. The Lorentz force exerted by electric and magnetic fields on a particle is given by $\vec{F} = q(\vec{E} + \vec{v} \times \vec{B})$. Since the charge $q = 0$ for gamma rays, the force is zero, meaning they travel in straight lines without any deflection.

Q4: What is the specific role of the quenching gas in a Geiger-Muller tube?

Answer: During the Townsend avalanche in a GM tube, excited argon atoms emit ultraviolet (UV) photons. If these UV photons strike the cathode, they can eject secondary electrons via the photoelectric effect, triggering a continuous, spurious discharge. The quenching gas (like alcohol or halogen) has a lower ionization potential than argon; it absorbs these UV photons or undergoes inelastic collisions with positive argon ions, neutralizing them without ejecting electrons. This "quenches" the discharge, ensuring each incident radiation particle produces only one distinct, countable pulse.

Q5: If the half-life of a radioactive substance is 1 hour, does it mean that after 2 hours, all of the substance will have decayed?

Answer: No. Radioactive decay follows an exponential law, not a linear one. After the first hour (one half-life), half ($50\%$) of the original substance remains. During the second hour, half of that remaining amount decays. Therefore, after 2 hours, one-fourth ($25\%$) of the original substance will still remain undecayed. It will theoretically take an infinite amount of time for the entire sample to decay completely.

Q6: Why is the penetrating power of alpha particles much lower than that of beta particles, even though alpha particles have higher kinetic energy?

Answer: Penetrating power depends inversely on ionizing power. Alpha particles have a relatively large mass and a double positive charge ($+2e$). As they pass through matter, their strong charge exerts a significant electromagnetic force on the electrons of the medium, causing intense ionization. They lose their kinetic energy very rapidly over a short distance. Beta particles, being much lighter and having only a single charge ($\pm e$), interact less frequently and ionize less intensely, allowing them to travel much further before losing all their energy.
