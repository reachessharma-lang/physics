5.1 Second Law of Thermodynamics

The first law of thermodynamics is based on the conservation of energy. It tells us that heat supplied to a system is used partly to increase its internal energy and partly to do external work. However, the first law does not tell us the direction in which a thermodynamic process occurs or whether a particular process is possible.

The second law of thermodynamics gives the direction of natural thermodynamic processes and establishes limitations on the conversion of heat into work.

Second law of thermodynamics: The second law states that heat cannot spontaneously flow from a colder body to a hotter body, and no heat engine can convert all the heat supplied to it completely into work.

Kelvin–Planck Statement

Kelvin–Planck statement: It is impossible to construct a heat engine operating in a cycle whose sole effect is to absorb heat from a single reservoir and convert it completely into an equivalent amount of work.

In other words, a heat engine cannot have an efficiency of \(100\%\). Some amount of heat must always be rejected to a low-temperature reservoir.

Clausius Statement

Clausius statement: It is impossible to construct a cyclic device whose sole effect is to transfer heat from a colder body to a hotter body without supplying external work.

Thus, heat naturally flows from a body at higher temperature to a body at lower temperature. To transfer heat from a cold reservoir to a hot reservoir, external work must be supplied.

Equivalence of Kelvin–Planck and Clausius Statements

The Kelvin–Planck and Clausius statements are two equivalent forms of the second law. Violation of either statement would lead to violation of the other.

Example 1: Conceptual question

Why can no heat engine have an efficiency of \(100\%\)?

Show solution

According to the Kelvin–Planck statement, it is impossible for a cyclic heat engine to convert all the heat absorbed from a single reservoir completely into work. Therefore, some heat must always be rejected to a sink. Hence, the efficiency of a practical heat engine is always less than \(100\%\).

5.2 Comparison of First and Second Laws of Thermodynamics

First law Second law
Based on conservation of energy. Determines the direction of thermodynamic processes.
States that energy cannot be created or destroyed. States that natural heat transfer occurs from hot to cold.
Does not specify the direction of heat flow. Specifies the natural direction of heat flow.
Allows conversion of heat into work in principle. Shows that complete conversion of heat into work is impossible.
Does not impose a maximum efficiency on a heat engine. Shows that the efficiency of a heat engine must be less than \(100\%\).
For a cyclic process, \(\Delta U=0\). For a reversible cycle, entropy change of the universe is zero.
Important: The first law answers “How much energy is conserved?”, whereas the second law answers “In which direction can the process occur?” and “How much of the supplied heat can be converted into useful work?”

Direction of Heat Flow

According to the second law, heat flows spontaneously from a body at higher temperature to a body at lower temperature.

Natural process

Hot body \(\rightarrow\) Cold body

No external work is required.

Reversed process

Cold body \(\rightarrow\) Hot body

External work is required.

5.3 Heat Engine

Heat engine: A heat engine is a device that converts part of the heat supplied to it into mechanical work by operating through a cyclic process.

A heat engine operates between two reservoirs:

  • Source: high-temperature reservoir at temperature \(T_1\).
  • Working substance: the substance that undergoes the thermodynamic cycle.
  • Sink: low-temperature reservoir at temperature \(T_2\).

During one complete cycle, the engine absorbs heat \(Q_1\) from the source, does work \(W\), and rejects heat \(Q_2\) to the sink.

\[ Q_1=W+Q_2 \] where \(Q_1\) is heat absorbed from the source, \(Q_2\) is heat rejected to the sink, and \(W\) is work done by the engine.

Efficiency of a Heat Engine

The efficiency of a heat engine is defined as the ratio of useful work obtained to the heat supplied to the engine.

\[ \eta=\frac{W}{Q_1} \] where \(\eta\) is efficiency, \(W\) is useful work output, and \(Q_1\) is heat absorbed from the source.

Since \(W=Q_1-Q_2\),

\[ \eta=\frac{Q_1-Q_2}{Q_1} \] where \(Q_1\) and \(Q_2\) are the heat absorbed and rejected, respectively.
\[ \boxed{\eta=1-\frac{Q_2}{Q_1}} \] where \(\eta\) is the efficiency of the heat engine.

For a real heat engine, \(Q_2\neq0\), so

\[ \eta<1 \] where \(\eta=1\) would correspond to \(100\%\) efficiency.
No heat engine can have \(100\%\) efficiency. A sink is necessary because some heat must be rejected according to the second law.

Work Done in a Heat Engine

\[ \boxed{W=Q_1-Q_2} \] where \(W\) is the net work obtained in one cycle.
Example 2

A heat engine absorbs \(2000\,\text{J}\) of heat from a source and rejects \(800\,\text{J}\) to the sink. Find the work done and efficiency.

Show solution

Given:

\(Q_1=2000\,\text{J}\), \(Q_2=800\,\text{J}\)

Work done:

\[ W=Q_1-Q_2=2000-800=1200\,\text{J} \]

Efficiency:

\[ \eta=\frac{W}{Q_1} =\frac{1200}{2000}=0.6 \]
\[ \boxed{\eta=60\%} \]

5.4 Carnot Cycle

Carnot cycle: A Carnot cycle is an ideal reversible thermodynamic cycle consisting of two isothermal processes and two adiabatic processes.

The Carnot engine operates between a hot reservoir at absolute temperature \(T_1\) and a cold reservoir at absolute temperature \(T_2\), where \(T_1>T_2\).

Four Processes of the Carnot Cycle

  1. Isothermal expansion
  2. Adiabatic expansion
  3. Isothermal compression
  4. Adiabatic compression

Process AB: Isothermal Expansion

The working substance is kept in contact with the hot reservoir at temperature \(T_1\). It expands slowly and absorbs heat \(Q_1\) from the source.

For an ideal gas undergoing isothermal expansion:

\[ PV=\text{constant} \] where \(P\) is pressure and \(V\) is volume.

Since temperature is constant, the change in internal energy is zero:

\[ \Delta U=0 \]

From the first law, \(Q=W\). Therefore, the heat supplied is completely converted into work during this step.

Work done from \(V_A\) to \(V_B\) is

\[ W_1=\int_{V_A}^{V_B}P\,dV \]

For an ideal gas, \(P=\frac{nRT_1}{V}\). Therefore,

\[ W_1=nRT_1\ln\left(\frac{V_B}{V_A}\right) \] where \(n\) is the number of moles, \(R\) is the gas constant, and \(T_1\) is the source temperature.

Hence,

\[ \boxed{Q_1=W_1=nRT_1\ln\left(\frac{V_B}{V_A}\right)} \]

Process BC: Adiabatic Expansion

The working substance is thermally insulated from the surroundings and expands without exchange of heat.

\[ Q=0 \]

For an adiabatic process:

\[ PV^\gamma=\text{constant} \] where \(\gamma=C_P/C_V\) is the ratio of specific heats.

From the first law,

\[ \Delta U=-W \]

Thus, the gas does work at the expense of its internal energy and its temperature decreases from \(T_1\) to \(T_2\).

The work done during adiabatic expansion is

\[ W_2=\frac{P_BV_B-P_CV_C}{\gamma-1} \] where \(P_B,V_B\) and \(P_C,V_C\) are the pressure and volume at the initial and final states of the adiabatic expansion.

Using \(PV=nRT\),

\[ \boxed{W_2=\frac{nR(T_1-T_2)}{\gamma-1}} \]

Process CD: Isothermal Compression

The working substance is maintained at the low temperature \(T_2\) and is compressed slowly while remaining in contact with the cold reservoir.

Heat \(Q_2\) is rejected to the sink.

\[ W_3=nRT_2\ln\left(\frac{V_D}{V_C}\right) \] where \(V_C\) and \(V_D\) are the initial and final volumes during isothermal compression.

\[ Q=0 \]

Condition for Carnot Cycle

For the two adiabatic processes,

\[ T_1V_B^{\gamma-1}=T_2V_C^{\gamma-1} \]
\[ T_1V_A^{\gamma-1}=T_2V_D^{\gamma-1} \]

Dividing the two equations gives

\[ \frac{V_B}{V_A}=\frac{V_C}{V_D} \]

Efficiency of Carnot Engine

For one complete cycle, the net work is

\[ W=Q_1-Q_2 \]

Therefore, efficiency is

\[ \eta=\frac{W}{Q_1} =\frac{Q_1-Q_2}{Q_1} \]
\[ \eta=1-\frac{Q_2}{Q_1} \]

For a reversible Carnot cycle,

\[ \frac{Q_1}{T_1}=\frac{Q_2}{T_2} \]

Hence,

\[ \frac{Q_2}{Q_1}=\frac{T_2}{T_1} \]

Therefore, the efficiency of a Carnot engine is

\[ \boxed{\eta=1-\frac{T_2}{T_1}} \] where \(T_1\) and \(T_2\) are the absolute temperatures of the source and sink, respectively.
Important: Temperatures in the Carnot efficiency formula must always be expressed in kelvin, not in degree Celsius.
Example 3: Carnot engine

A Carnot engine operates between \(500\,\text{K}\) and \(300\,\text{K}\). Calculate its efficiency.

Show solution
\[ T_1=500\,\text{K},\qquad T_2=300\,\text{K} \]
\[ \eta=1-\frac{T_2}{T_1} =1-\frac{300}{500} =0.4 \]
\[ \boxed{\eta=40\%} \]
Example 4: Finding sink temperature

A Carnot engine has an efficiency of \(40\%\) and operates with a source temperature of \(500\,\text{K}\). Find the temperature of the sink.

Show solution
\[ \eta=1-\frac{T_2}{T_1} \]
\[ 0.40=1-\frac{T_2}{500} \]
\[ \frac{T_2}{500}=0.60 \]
\[ \boxed{T_2=300\,\text{K}} \]

5.5 Internal Combustion Engines: Otto and Diesel Engines

Internal combustion engine: An internal combustion engine is a heat engine in which the fuel burns inside the engine cylinder and the heat released by combustion is used to produce mechanical work.

In internal combustion engines, the fuel and air mixture burns inside the cylinder. The high-pressure gases produced by combustion push the piston and produce mechanical work.

Major Types

  • Otto engine: ideal cycle for spark-ignition petrol engines.
  • Diesel engine: ideal cycle for compression-ignition diesel engines.

Otto Cycle

The ideal Otto cycle consists of four processes:

  1. Adiabatic compression
  2. Constant-volume heat addition
  3. Adiabatic expansion
  4. Constant-volume heat rejection

Efficiency of Otto Engine

The efficiency of an ideal Otto engine is

\[ \boxed{\eta_O=1-\frac{1}{r^{\gamma-1}}} \] where \(r=V_{\max}/V_{\min}\) is the compression ratio and \(\gamma=C_P/C_V\).

Thus, the efficiency of the Otto cycle increases as the compression ratio increases.

Diesel Cycle

The ideal Diesel cycle consists of:

  1. Adiabatic compression
  2. Constant-pressure heat addition
  3. Adiabatic expansion
  4. Constant-volume heat rejection

Efficiency of Diesel Engine

\[ \boxed{\eta_D =1-\frac{1}{r^{\gamma-1}} \left(\frac{\rho^\gamma-1}{\gamma(\rho-1)}\right)} \] where \(r=V_1/V_2\) is the compression ratio and \(\rho=V_3/V_2\) is the cut-off ratio.

Otto and Diesel Engine Comparison

Otto cycle Diesel cycle
Heat is supplied at constant volume. Heat is supplied at constant pressure.
Used as the ideal cycle for petrol engines. Used as the ideal cycle for diesel engines.
Ignition is generally represented by spark ignition. Ignition occurs due to high compression and temperature.
Efficiency depends mainly on compression ratio and \(\gamma\). Efficiency depends on compression ratio, \(\gamma\), and cut-off ratio.
For the same compression ratio, its ideal efficiency is greater than that of the Diesel cycle. For the same compression ratio, its ideal efficiency is lower than that of the Otto cycle.
Exam point: For equal compression ratio and the same working substance, the ideal Otto cycle has greater efficiency than the ideal Diesel cycle.

5.6 Refrigerator as a Heat Engine Working in Reverse

Refrigerator: A refrigerator is a device that transfers heat from a low-temperature region to a high-temperature region by using external work.

A refrigerator operates on the reverse principle of a heat engine.

A heat engine transfers heat naturally from hot to cold and produces work. A refrigerator transfers heat from cold to hot, but external work must be supplied.

For one complete cycle,

\[ Q_1=Q_2+W \] where \(Q_1\) is heat rejected to the surroundings, \(Q_2\) is heat removed from the cold region, and \(W\) is work supplied.

Coefficient of Performance

The performance of a refrigerator is measured by its coefficient of performance (COP), not by ordinary efficiency.

\[ \boxed{\text{COP}_{R}=\frac{Q_2}{W}} \] where \(Q_2\) is the heat removed from the cold reservoir and \(W\) is the work supplied.

Since \(W=Q_1-Q_2\),

\[ \boxed{\text{COP}_{R}=\frac{Q_2}{Q_1-Q_2}} \]

COP of a Carnot Refrigerator

For a reversible Carnot refrigerator,

\[ \frac{Q_1}{Q_2}=\frac{T_1}{T_2} \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\text{COP}_{R}=\frac{T_2}{T_1-T_2}} \] where \(T_1\) is the temperature of the hot reservoir and \(T_2\) is the temperature of the cold reservoir.
Example 5: Refrigerator

A refrigerator removes \(600\,\text{J}\) of heat from its cold chamber when \(200\,\text{J}\) of work is supplied. Find its COP.

Show solution
\[ Q_2=600\,\text{J},\qquad W=200\,\text{J} \]
\[ \text{COP}_{R}=\frac{Q_2}{W} =\frac{600}{200} \]
\[ \boxed{\text{COP}_{R}=3} \]

5.7 Entropy and Its Role in Thermodynamic Processes

Entropy: Entropy is a thermodynamic state function that provides a measure of the degree of disorder or randomness of a system and indicates the direction of spontaneous thermodynamic processes.

Entropy is represented by the symbol \(S\).

For a reversible process, the infinitesimal change in entropy is defined as

\[ \boxed{dS=\frac{dQ_{\text{rev}}}{T}} \] where \(dS\) is the change in entropy, \(dQ_{\text{rev}}\) is the reversible heat supplied, and \(T\) is the absolute temperature.

For a finite reversible process,

\[ \boxed{\Delta S=\int\frac{dQ_{\text{rev}}}{T}} \]

If the temperature remains constant,

\[ \boxed{\Delta S=\frac{Q_{\text{rev}}}{T}} \] where \(Q_{\text{rev}}\) is reversible heat transfer at constant absolute temperature \(T\).

Unit of Entropy

\[ [S]=\text{J K}^{-1} \]

The molar entropy has unit \(\text{J mol}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1}\).

Entropy Change in an Isothermal Process

For a reversible isothermal process at temperature \(T\),

\[ \Delta S=\frac{Q_{\text{rev}}}{T} \]

For an ideal gas undergoing reversible isothermal expansion:

\[ Q_{\text{rev}}=W=nRT\ln\left(\frac{V_2}{V_1}\right) \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\Delta S=nR\ln\left(\frac{V_2}{V_1}\right)} \] where \(n\) is the number of moles and \(V_1,V_2\) are the initial and final volumes.

Entropy Change in an Adiabatic Reversible Process

For a reversible adiabatic process,

\[ dQ_{\text{rev}}=0 \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\Delta S=0} \]

Such a process is called isentropic.

Entropy and the Second Law

The second law can be expressed in terms of entropy. For an isolated system, the entropy never decreases.

\[ \boxed{\Delta S_{\text{universe}}\geq0} \]
  • For a reversible process: \(\Delta S_{\text{universe}}=0\).
  • For an irreversible process: \(\Delta S_{\text{universe}}>0\).
Entropy and disorder: In general, a more disordered or random state has greater entropy. For example, a gas distributed throughout a container has greater entropy than the same gas confined to a small portion of the container.

Entropy and Direction of Heat Flow

Suppose heat \(Q\) flows from a hot reservoir at temperature \(T_1\) to a cold reservoir at \(T_2\), where \(T_1>T_2\).

The entropy change of the hot reservoir is

\[ \Delta S_h=-\frac{Q}{T_1} \]

The entropy change of the cold reservoir is

\[ \Delta S_c=\frac{Q}{T_2} \]

Therefore,

\[ \Delta S_{\text{universe}} =Q\left(\frac{1}{T_2}-\frac{1}{T_1}\right) \]

Since \(T_1>T_2\),

\[ \boxed{\Delta S_{\text{universe}}>0} \]

This explains why heat naturally flows from a hot body to a cold body.

Entropy–Temperature Graph

\[ \boxed{dQ_{\text{rev}}=T\,dS} \]

5.8 Mathematical Problems Related to Heat Engines

Useful Formulae

  • \(W=Q_1-Q_2\)
  • \(\eta=W/Q_1\)
  • \(\eta=1-Q_2/Q_1\)
  • For Carnot engine: \(\eta=1-T_2/T_1\)
  • For Carnot engine: \(Q_1/T_1=Q_2/T_2\)
  • For refrigerator: \(\text{COP}_R=Q_2/W\)
  • For Carnot refrigerator: \(\text{COP}_R=T_2/(T_1-T_2)\)
  • For Otto engine: \(\eta_O=1-1/r^{\gamma-1}\)
  • For Diesel engine: \(\eta_D=1-\frac{1}{r^{\gamma-1}}\left(\frac{\rho^\gamma-1}{\gamma(\rho-1)}\right)\)
  • For reversible process: \(dS=dQ_{\text{rev}}/T\)
  • For isothermal ideal-gas process: \(\Delta S=nR\ln(V_2/V_1)\)
Example 6: Heat engine efficiency

A heat engine absorbs \(5000\,\text{J}\) of heat and rejects \(3000\,\text{J}\) to the sink. Find its efficiency.

Show solution

Given:

\[ Q_1=5000\,\text{J},\qquad Q_2=3000\,\text{J} \]

Work obtained:

\[ W=Q_1-Q_2=5000-3000=2000\,\text{J} \]

Efficiency:

\[ \eta=\frac{W}{Q_1} =\frac{2000}{5000} =0.4 \]
\[ \boxed{\eta=40\%} \]
Example 7: Heat rejected by an engine

A heat engine works with an efficiency of \(25\%\). If it absorbs \(4000\,\text{J}\) of heat per cycle, calculate the work done and heat rejected.

Show solution

Given:

\[ \eta=0.25,\qquad Q_1=4000\,\text{J} \]

Using \(\eta=W/Q_1\):

\[ W=\eta Q_1 =0.25\times4000 =1000\,\text{J} \]

Using \(W=Q_1-Q_2\):

\[ Q_2=Q_1-W =4000-1000 =3000\,\text{J} \]
\[ \boxed{W=1000\,\text{J}} \]
\[ \boxed{Q_2=3000\,\text{J}} \]
Example 8: Carnot engine

A Carnot engine takes heat from a reservoir at \(600\,\text{K}\) and rejects heat to a sink at \(300\,\text{K}\). If it absorbs \(2400\,\text{J}\) of heat per cycle, calculate its efficiency, work output and heat rejected.

Show solution

Given:

\[ T_1=600\,\text{K},\qquad T_2=300\,\text{K},\qquad Q_1=2400\,\text{J} \]

Efficiency:

\[ \eta=1-\frac{T_2}{T_1} =1-\frac{300}{600} =0.5 \]
\[ \boxed{\eta=50\%} \]

Work output:

\[ W=\eta Q_1 =0.5\times2400 =1200\,\text{J} \]

Heat rejected:

\[ Q_2=Q_1-W =2400-1200 =1200\,\text{J} \]
\[ \boxed{W=1200\,\text{J},\qquad Q_2=1200\,\text{J}} \]
Example 9: Finding source temperature

A Carnot engine has an efficiency of \(60\%\) and its sink is maintained at \(300\,\text{K}\). Find the temperature of the source.

Show solution
\[ \eta=1-\frac{T_2}{T_1} \]
\[ 0.60=1-\frac{300}{T_1} \]
\[ \frac{300}{T_1}=0.40 \]
\[ T_1=\frac{300}{0.40} \]
\[ \boxed{T_1=750\,\text{K}} \]
Example 10: Carnot heat quantities

A Carnot engine operates between \(800\,\text{K}\) and \(400\,\text{K}\). If the heat rejected per cycle is \(1000\,\text{J}\), calculate the heat absorbed and work output.

Show solution

For a Carnot engine:

\[ \frac{Q_1}{T_1}=\frac{Q_2}{T_2} \]
\[ Q_1=Q_2\frac{T_1}{T_2} =1000\times\frac{800}{400} =2000\,\text{J} \]

Work output:

\[ W=Q_1-Q_2 =2000-1000 =1000\,\text{J} \]
\[ \boxed{Q_1=2000\,\text{J},\qquad W=1000\,\text{J}} \]
Example 11: Carnot refrigerator

A Carnot refrigerator operates between \(270\,\text{K}\) and \(300\,\text{K}\). Find its coefficient of performance.

Show solution
\[ T_2=270\,\text{K},\qquad T_1=300\,\text{K} \]
\[ \text{COP}_R=\frac{T_2}{T_1-T_2} \]
\[ \text{COP}_R=\frac{270}{300-270} =\frac{270}{30} =9 \]
\[ \boxed{\text{COP}_R=9} \]
Example 12: Entropy change

A system absorbs \(600\,\text{J}\) of heat reversibly at a constant temperature of \(300\,\text{K}\). Calculate the entropy change.

Show solution
\[ \Delta S=\frac{Q_{\text{rev}}}{T} \]
\[ \Delta S=\frac{600}{300} =2\,\text{J K}^{-1} \]
\[ \boxed{\Delta S=2\,\text{J K}^{-1}} \]

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Do not use Celsius temperatures directly in \(\eta=1-T_2/T_1\). Convert them to kelvin first.
  • Do not write \(W=Q_1+Q_2\) for a heat engine. The correct relation is \(W=Q_1-Q_2\).
  • Do not confuse the heat absorbed \(Q_1\) with the useful work \(W\).
  • Remember that a heat engine rejects heat to the sink, whereas a refrigerator removes heat from the cold region.
  • Use efficiency for a heat engine and COP for a refrigerator.
  • Do not interchange \(T_1\) and \(T_2\): normally \(T_1>T_2\), where \(T_1\) is source temperature and \(T_2\) is sink temperature.
  • For an adiabatic process, \(Q=0\), but work need not be zero.
  • For a reversible adiabatic process, entropy remains constant.
  • For an isothermal ideal-gas process, internal energy remains constant because temperature remains constant.
  • In the Carnot cycle, heat is supplied during isothermal expansion and rejected during isothermal compression.
