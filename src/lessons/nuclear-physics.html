Nuclear physics explores the constituents and behaviour of atomic nuclei. Understanding the nucleus is crucial for explaining radioactivity, nuclear energy, and the fundamental forces that hold matter together. This chapter bridges classical mechanics with modern physics through Einstein's mass-energy equivalence and the concept of binding energy.

24.1 Discovery of the Nucleus

The existence of the nucleus was discovered by Ernest Rutherford in 1911 through the alpha-particle scattering experiment (Geiger-Marsden experiment).

The Experiment

  • A narrow beam of alpha particles (\( \alpha \), helium nuclei) was directed at a very thin gold foil.
  • A fluorescent screen surrounded the foil to detect the scattered alpha particles.

Observations

  1. Most alpha particles passed straight through the foil with little or no deflection.
  2. A small fraction were deflected through small angles.
  3. A very tiny fraction (about 1 in 8000) bounced back (deflected by \( > 90^\circ \)).

Conclusions

  • Most of the atom is empty space.
  • The entire positive charge and almost all the mass of the atom are concentrated in a tiny, dense central region called the nucleus.
  • Electrons revolve around the nucleus in circular orbits, balancing the electrostatic attraction with centrifugal force.

24.2 Mass Number and Atomic Number

The nucleus is represented by the notation \( _Z^A X \), where \( X \) is the chemical symbol of the element.

  • Atomic Number (\( Z \)): The number of protons in the nucleus. It determines the chemical identity of the element and the number of electrons in a neutral atom.
  • Mass Number (\( A \)): The total number of nucleons (protons + neutrons) in the nucleus. \( A = Z + N \), where \( N \) is the number of neutrons.
  • Neutron Number (\( N \)): \( N = A - Z \).

Example: For \( _{6}^{12}\text{C} \), \( Z = 6 \) (6 protons), \( A = 12 \) (12 nucleons), so \( N = 12 - 6 = 6 \) neutrons.

24.3 Expression for Nuclear Density

Experimental evidence shows that the radius \( R \) of a nucleus is related to its mass number \( A \) by the empirical formula:

\[ R = R_0 A^{1/3} \]

where \( R_0 \approx 1.2 \times 10^{-15} \text{ m} \) (or 1.2 fm) is a constant.

Derivation of Nuclear Density

  1. Volume of the nucleus: Assuming the nucleus is spherical, \[ V = \frac{4}{3} \pi R^3 = \frac{4}{3} \pi (R_0 A^{1/3})^3 = \frac{4}{3} \pi R_0^3 A \]
  2. Mass of the nucleus: Let \( m \) be the average mass of a nucleon (proton or neutron, \( m \approx 1.67 \times 10^{-27} \text{ kg} \)). The total mass is: \[ M \approx A \cdot m \]
  3. Density (\( \rho \)): \[ \rho = \frac{M}{V} = \frac{A \cdot m}{\frac{4}{3} \pi R_0^3 A} \]
  4. Canceling \( A \) from numerator and denominator: \[ \rho = \frac{3m}{4\pi R_0^3} \]

Since \( m \) and \( R_0 \) are constants, nuclear density is independent of the mass number \( A \). It is approximately \( 2.3 \times 10^{17} \text{ kg m}^{-3} \), which is enormously high compared to ordinary matter.

24.4 Isotopes

Isotopes are atoms of the same element that have the same atomic number (\( Z \)) but different mass numbers (\( A \)). This means they have the same number of protons but a different number of neutrons.

Examples

  • Hydrogen: Protium (\( _1^1\text{H} \), 0 neutrons), Deuterium (\( _1^2\text{H} \), 1 neutron), Tritium (\( _1^3\text{H} \), 2 neutrons).
  • Carbon: Carbon-12 (\( _6^{12}\text{C} \)) and Carbon-14 (\( _6^{14}\text{C} \)).

Isotopes have identical chemical properties (determined by electrons) but different physical properties like mass and nuclear stability.

24.5 Einstein’s Mass-Energy Relation

Albert Einstein proposed that mass and energy are two forms of the same entity and can be converted into each other. This is the main theme of special relativity.

The relation is given by the famous equation:

\[ E = mc^2 \]

where \( E \) is energy, \( m \) is mass, and \( c \) is the speed of light in vacuum (\( 3 \times 10^8 \text{ m s}^{-1} \)). A small amount of mass corresponds to a tremendous amount of energy.

In nuclear physics, it is convenient to express mass in atomic mass units (u) and energy in mega-electron volts (MeV):

\[ 1 \text{ u} = 1.6605 \times 10^{-27} \text{ kg} \equiv 931.5 \text{ MeV}/c^2 \]

24.6 Mass Defect and Its Cause

Mass defect (\( \Delta m \)) is the difference between the sum of the masses of the individual constituent nucleons (protons and neutrons) and the actual measured mass of the nucleus.

\[ \Delta m = [Z m_p + (A - Z) m_n] - M_{\text{nucleus}} \]

where \( m_p \) is the mass of a proton, \( m_n \) is the mass of a neutron, and \( M_{\text{nucleus}} \) is the actual mass of the nucleus.

Cause of Mass Defect

The "missing" mass is not lost; it is converted into energy (according to \( E = \Delta m c^2 \)) and released when the nucleus is formed. This energy is called the binding energy, and it is responsible for holding the nucleons together against the electrostatic repulsion between protons.

24.7 Pair Creation and Annihilation

These phenomena are direct experimental proofs of Einstein's mass-energy equivalence.

Pair Creation (Production)

A high-energy gamma-ray photon (\( \gamma \)) disappears in the vicinity of a heavy nucleus, and its energy is converted into an electron (\( e^- \)) and a positron (\( e^+ \)).

\[ \gamma \rightarrow e^- + e^+ \]

The minimum energy required for this is the rest mass energy of both particles: \( E_{\text{min}} = 2 m_e c^2 = 2 \times 0.511 \text{ MeV} = 1.022 \text{ MeV} \).

Pair Annihilation

When an electron and a positron collide, they destroy each other, and their mass is completely converted into energy in the form of two gamma-ray photons (to conserve momentum).

\[ e^- + e^+ \rightarrow 2\gamma \]

Energy released = \( 2 m_e c^2 = 1.022 \text{ MeV} \).

24.8 Binding Energy and Binding Energy per Nucleon

Binding Energy (BE) is the minimum energy required to completely separate a nucleus into its constituent protons and neutrons. It is equivalent to the energy released when the nucleus is formed.

\[ \text{BE} = \Delta m \cdot c^2 \]

If \( \Delta m \) is in atomic mass units (u), then:

\[ \text{BE} = \Delta m \times 931.5 \text{ MeV} \]

Binding Energy per Nucleon (\( \text{BE}/A \))

This is the average energy required to remove a single nucleon from the nucleus.

\[ \frac{\text{BE}}{A} = \frac{\Delta m \cdot c^2}{A} \]

Significance: It is a measure of nuclear stability. A higher \( \text{BE}/A \) means the nucleus is more tightly bound and more stable.

24.9 Graph of BE per Nucleon vs Mass Number

The plot of \( \text{BE}/A \) against mass number \( A \) reveals crucial insights into nuclear stability and energy release.

Features of the Graph

  1. Light nuclei (\( A < 30 \)): \( \text{BE}/A \) rises rapidly with \( A \), with sharp peaks at \( _2^4\text{He} \), \( _6^{12}\text{C} \), and \( _8^{16}\text{O} \), indicating they are exceptionally stable.
  2. Middle-mass nuclei (\( 30 < A < 170 \)): The curve is relatively flat, peaking at Iron-56 (\( _{26}^{56}\text{Fe} \)) with a maximum \( \text{BE}/A \approx 8.8 \text{ MeV} \). These are the most stable nuclei.
  3. Heavy nuclei (\( A > 170 \)): \( \text{BE}/A \) gradually decreases (e.g., Uranium-238 has \( \text{BE}/A \approx 7.6 \text{ MeV} \)) due to increasing Coulomb repulsion between protons.

24.10 Nuclear Fusion and Fission

Nuclear Fission

Nuclear fission is the process in which a heavy, unstable nucleus splits into two or more lighter nuclei of comparable mass, accompanied by the release of a large amount of energy and a few neutrons.

  • Mechanism of energy release: The parent heavy nucleus has a lower \( \text{BE}/A \) than the daughter nuclei. When it splits, the total binding energy of the products is greater than that of the parent. This difference in binding energy is released as kinetic energy of the fragments and radiation.
  • Example: \( _{92}^{235}\text{U} + _0^1\text{n} \rightarrow _{56}^{141}\text{Ba} + _{36}^{92}\text{Kr} + 3 _0^1\text{n} + \text{Energy} \)

Nuclear Fusion

Nuclear fusion is the process in which two or more light nuclei combine to form a single, heavier nucleus, releasing a tremendous amount of energy.

  • Mechanism of energy release: Light nuclei have low \( \text{BE}/A \). When they fuse, the resulting heavier nucleus has a higher \( \text{BE}/A \). The increase in binding energy per nucleon is released as energy.
  • Example: \( _1^2\text{H} + _1^3\text{H} \rightarrow _2^4\text{He} + _0^1\text{n} + 17.6 \text{ MeV} \)
  • Fusion requires extremely high temperatures (\( \sim 10^7 \text{ K} \)) to overcome the electrostatic repulsion between the positively charged nuclei (thermonuclear reaction).

Worked Examples

Example 1. Calculate the binding energy and binding energy per nucleon of an alpha particle (\( _2^4\text{He} \)). Given: mass of proton \( m_p = 1.007276 \text{ u} \), mass of neutron \( m_n = 1.008665 \text{ u} \), mass of \( _2^4\text{He} \) nucleus \( = 4.001506 \text{ u} \), and \( 1 \text{ u} = 931.5 \text{ MeV}/c^2 \).

Solution.

For \( _2^4\text{He} \), \( Z = 2 \), \( A = 4 \), so \( N = 2 \).

Mass of constituent nucleons \( = 2 m_p + 2 m_n \)

\[ = 2(1.007276) + 2(1.008665) = 2.014552 + 2.017330 = 4.031882 \text{ u} \]

Mass defect \( \Delta m = 4.031882 - 4.001506 = 0.030376 \text{ u} \)

Binding Energy \( \text{BE} = \Delta m \times 931.5 = 0.030376 \times 931.5 \approx 28.3 \text{ MeV} \)

Binding Energy per nucleon \( \text{BE}/A = \frac{28.3}{4} \approx 7.07 \text{ MeV/nucleon} \).

Answer: \( \text{BE} = 28.3 \text{ MeV} \), \( \text{BE}/A = 7.07 \text{ MeV/nucleon} \).

Example 2. In a pair annihilation event, an electron and a positron, both essentially at rest, annihilate each other. Calculate the total energy released and the energy of each resulting gamma-ray photon.

Solution.

Rest mass of electron (or positron) \( m_e = 9.1 \times 10^{-31} \text{ kg} \), or equivalently, rest mass energy \( = 0.511 \text{ MeV} \).

Total energy released \( E = 2 m_e c^2 = 2 \times 0.511 \text{ MeV} = 1.022 \text{ MeV} \).

To conserve momentum, two photons are produced, each carrying half the total energy.

Energy of each photon \( = \frac{1.022}{2} = 0.511 \text{ MeV} \).

Answer: Total energy = \( 1.022 \text{ MeV} \), Energy per photon = \( 0.511 \text{ MeV} \).

Example 3. Calculate the energy released in the fission of one \( _{92}^{235}\text{U} \) nucleus if the binding energy per nucleon of \( _{92}^{235}\text{U} \) is \( 7.6 \text{ MeV} \) and that of the fission fragments (average) is \( 8.5 \text{ MeV} \).

Solution.

Total binding energy of parent \( _{92}^{235}\text{U} \) nucleus:

\[ \text{BE}_{\text{initial}} = 235 \times 7.6 = 1786 \text{ MeV} \]

Total binding energy of the fission fragments (total nucleons remain 235):

\[ \text{BE}_{\text{final}} = 235 \times 8.5 = 1997.5 \text{ MeV} \]

Energy released \( Q = \text{BE}_{\text{final}} - \text{BE}_{\text{initial}} \)

\[ Q = 1997.5 - 1786 = 211.5 \text{ MeV} \]

Answer: Energy released is approximately \( 211.5 \text{ MeV} \).

Common Mistakes

  • Using atomic mass instead of nuclear mass: If the problem gives the atomic mass, you must subtract the mass of \( Z \) electrons to find the nuclear mass before calculating mass defect (or ensure electron masses cancel out on both sides of the equation).
  • Forgetting the \( c^2 \) factor: Mass defect is in kg or u; to get energy, you must multiply by \( c^2 \) or use the conversion \( 1 \text{ u} = 931.5 \text{ MeV} \).
  • Confusing fission and fusion: Fission is splitting heavy nuclei (moving right-to-left toward the peak on the BE/A graph); fusion is combining light nuclei (moving left-to-right toward the peak).
  • Miscalculating neutron number: Remember \( N = A - Z \), not just \( A \). A common error is using \( A \) instead of \( (A-Z) \) for the number of neutrons in the mass defect formula.
  • Assuming nuclear density varies with size: Nuclear density is constant for all nuclei; it does not depend on \( A \).

Key Points Summary

  • Rutherford's alpha scattering experiment proved the existence of a tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus.
  • Nuclear radius \( R = R_0 A^{1/3} \), leading to a constant nuclear density of \( \sim 10^{17} \text{ kg m}^{-3} \), independent of \( A \).
  • Isotopes have the same \( Z \) but different \( A \) (different number of neutrons).
  • Einstein's relation \( E = mc^2 \) explains the interchangeability of mass and energy (\( 1 \text{ u} = 931.5 \text{ MeV}/c^2 \)).
  • Mass defect \( \Delta m \) is the difference between the mass of constituent nucleons and the actual nuclear mass.
  • Binding Energy \( \text{BE} = \Delta m \cdot c^2 \). Higher \( \text{BE}/A \) means greater nuclear stability.
  • The \( \text{BE}/A \) curve peaks at Iron-56 (\( \sim 8.8 \text{ MeV} \)), explaining why both fission (heavy nuclei splitting) and fusion (light nuclei joining) release energy.
  • Pair production converts energy to mass (\( \gamma \rightarrow e^- + e^+ \)), while pair annihilation converts mass to energy (\( e^- + e^+ \rightarrow 2\gamma \)).
