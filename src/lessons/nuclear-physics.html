24.1 Discovery of the Nucleus

The nucleus is the small, dense, positively charged central part of an atom. It contains almost all of the mass of the atom and consists of protons and neutrons, collectively called nucleons.

Rutherford's Alpha-Particle Scattering Experiment

The existence of the nucleus was established by Ernest Rutherford and his collaborators through the alpha-particle scattering experiment performed around 1909.

Observations

  • Most of the alpha particles passed through the gold foil without any appreciable deflection.
  • Some alpha particles were deflected through small angles.
  • A very small number of alpha particles were deflected through large angles.
  • In extremely rare cases, alpha particles were scattered almost backward.

Conclusions

  1. Most of the atom is empty space.
  2. Almost the entire positive charge of the atom is concentrated in a very small region called the nucleus.
  3. Almost the entire mass of the atom is concentrated in the nucleus.
  4. The nucleus is extremely small compared with the size of the atom.
Nucleus: The tiny, dense, positively charged central region of an atom containing protons and neutrons is called the nucleus.
The atomic radius is of the order of \(10^{-10}\,\mathrm{m}\), whereas the nuclear radius is of the order of \(10^{-15}\,\mathrm{m}\).

24.2 Mass Number and Atomic Number

A nucleus is represented in the form

\[ {}^{A}_{Z}X \] where \(X\) is the chemical symbol, \(Z\) is the atomic number and \(A\) is the mass number.

Atomic Number

Atomic number \(Z\): The number of protons present in the nucleus of an atom is called its atomic number.

For a neutral atom, the number of electrons is also equal to \(Z\).

\[ Z=N_p=N_e \] for a neutral atom.

Mass Number

Mass number \(A\): The total number of protons and neutrons present in the nucleus is called its mass number.
\[ A=Z+N \] where \(N\) is the number of neutrons.

Therefore,

\[ N=A-Z \] where \(N\) is the number of neutrons.
Example 1: Find the number of protons, neutrons and electrons in \({}^{23}_{11}\mathrm{Na}\).
Show solution

Here, \(A=23\) and \(Z=11\).

Number of protons \(=Z=11\).

For a neutral atom, number of electrons \(=11\).

Number of neutrons \(=A-Z=23-11=12\).

Answer: Protons \(=11\), electrons \(=11\), neutrons \(=12\).

24.3 Nuclear Density

The radius of a nucleus is approximately related to its mass number by

\[ R=R_0A^{1/3} \] where \(R_0\approx1.2\times10^{-15}\,\mathrm{m}\).

The volume of the nucleus is

\[ V=\frac{4}{3}\pi R^3 \]

Since \(R=R_0A^{1/3}\),

\[ V=\frac{4}{3}\pi R_0^3A \]

If the mass of each nucleon is approximately \(m_N\), the mass of the nucleus is approximately

\[ M\approx Am_N \]

Therefore, nuclear density is

\[ \rho=\frac{M}{V} =\frac{Am_N}{\frac{4}{3}\pi R_0^3A} \]

Hence,

\[ \boxed{\rho=\frac{3m_N}{4\pi R_0^3}} \]

The mass number \(A\) cancels out. Therefore, nuclear density is approximately the same for all nuclei.

Nuclear matter has an extremely high density, of the order of \(10^{17}\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\).
Example 2: Show that the radius of a nucleus containing \(A=64\) nucleons is approximately \(4.8\times10^{-15}\,\mathrm{m}\), taking \(R_0=1.2\times10^{-15}\,\mathrm{m}\).
Show solution
\[ R=R_0A^{1/3} \]

Thus,

\[ R=(1.2\times10^{-15})(64)^{1/3} \]
\[ R=(1.2\times10^{-15})(4) =4.8\times10^{-15}\,\mathrm{m} \]

Answer: \(R=4.8\times10^{-15}\,\mathrm{m}\).

24.4 Isotopes

Isotopes: Atoms of the same element having the same atomic number but different mass numbers are called isotopes.

Since isotopes have the same atomic number, they contain the same number of protons and, in neutral atoms, the same number of electrons. However, they contain different numbers of neutrons.

\[ Z_1=Z_2,\qquad A_1\ne A_2 \]

Example of Hydrogen Isotopes

Isotope Symbol Protons Neutrons
Protium \({}^{1}_{1}\mathrm{H}\) 1 0
Deuterium \({}^{2}_{1}\mathrm{H}\) 1 1
Tritium \({}^{3}_{1}\mathrm{H}\) 1 2
Isotopes have very similar chemical properties because chemical properties mainly depend on electronic configuration. Their physical and nuclear properties can be different because their masses and neutron numbers are different.

24.5 Einstein's Mass-Energy Relation

According to Einstein's special theory of relativity, mass and energy are equivalent. A body possesses energy simply because it has mass.

Mass-energy relation: The energy equivalent of a mass \(m\) is
\[ \boxed{E=mc^2} \] where \(E\) is energy, \(m\) is mass and \(c\) is the speed of light in vacuum.

Since

\[ c=3\times10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}, \]

a very small amount of mass corresponds to a very large amount of energy.

Mass-Energy Relation in Nuclear Physics

If the mass of a nuclear system changes by \(\Delta m\), the corresponding energy change is

\[ \boxed{\Delta E=\Delta mc^2} \]

This relation explains why enormous amounts of energy can be released during nuclear fission and fusion.

Useful Conversion

\[ 1\,\mathrm{u}\approx931.5\,\mathrm{MeV}/c^2 \]

Therefore, if mass defect is expressed in atomic mass unit, the corresponding energy is

\[ \boxed{E(\mathrm{MeV})=\Delta m(\mathrm{u})\times931.5} \]
Example 3: Calculate the energy equivalent of \(1\,\mathrm{u}\).
Show solution

Using \(E=mc^2\),

\[ E=(1.6605\times10^{-27})(3\times10^8)^2 \]
\[ E\approx1.494\times10^{-10}\,\mathrm{J} \]

Converting into electron volts gives approximately

\[ E\approx931.5\,\mathrm{MeV}. \]

Answer: \(1\,\mathrm{u}\) corresponds to approximately \(931.5\,\mathrm{MeV}/c^2\).

24.6 Mass Defect and Its Cause

Mass defect: The difference between the sum of the masses of the individual free nucleons and the actual mass of the nucleus is called the mass defect.

For a nucleus containing \(Z\) protons and \(N\) neutrons, using nuclear masses,

\[ \boxed{\Delta m=Zm_p+Nm_n-M} \] where \(m_p\) is the proton mass, \(m_n\) is the neutron mass and \(M\) is the actual nuclear mass.

Since \(A=Z+N\), the equation can also be written as

\[ \Delta m=Zm_p+(A-Z)m_n-M \]

Cause of Mass Defect

When free nucleons combine to form a nucleus, energy is released. The released energy corresponds to a decrease in the mass of the system.

\[ E_b=\Delta mc^2 \]

Thus, the mass of a bound nucleus is less than the sum of the masses of its separated nucleons. This difference is called mass defect.

Do not confuse mass defect with a loss of nucleons. The number of nucleons remains unchanged; the decrease in mass is associated with the binding energy of the nucleus.

24.7 Creation and Annihilation

Creation

Creation: The process in which energy is converted into a particle-antiparticle pair is called pair creation or creation.

For example, a high-energy photon can produce an electron and a positron under suitable conditions:

\[ \gamma\rightarrow e^-+e^+ \]

Because momentum must be conserved, pair production normally occurs in the presence of a nearby nucleus or another particle that can take up recoil momentum.

The minimum energy required for electron-positron pair production is

\[ E_{\min}=2m_ec^2 \]

For an electron-positron pair, this is approximately \(1.022\,\mathrm{MeV}\).

Annihilation

Annihilation: The process in which a particle and its antiparticle disappear and their rest mass is converted into energy is called annihilation.

For electron-positron annihilation,

\[ e^-+e^+\rightarrow2\gamma \]

The energy is carried away by the photons, along with the appropriate momentum.

Creation converts energy into particle mass, whereas annihilation converts particle rest mass into other forms of energy, commonly photons.

24.8 Binding Energy and Binding Energy per Nucleon

Binding energy: The minimum energy required to separate a nucleus completely into its constituent free protons and neutrons is called the binding energy of the nucleus.

The same energy is released when the free nucleons combine to form the nucleus.

From mass defect,

\[ \boxed{BE=\Delta mc^2} \]

Using the nuclear mass expression,

\[ BE=\left[Zm_p+Nm_n-M\right]c^2 \]

Since \(N=A-Z\),

\[ \boxed{BE=\left[Zm_p+(A-Z)m_n-M\right]c^2} \]

Using Atomic Masses

When atomic masses are used, the electron masses cancel. The mass defect can be written as

\[ \boxed{\Delta m=Zm_H+Nm_n-M_{\mathrm{atom}}} \] where \(m_H\) is the mass of a hydrogen atom and \(M_{\mathrm{atom}}\) is the atomic mass of the isotope.

Hence,

\[ \boxed{BE=\left[Zm_H+(A-Z)m_n-M_{\mathrm{atom}}\right]c^2} \]

Binding Energy in MeV

If \(\Delta m\) is expressed in atomic mass unit,

\[ \boxed{BE(\mathrm{MeV})=\Delta m(\mathrm{u})\times931.5} \]

Binding Energy per Nucleon

Binding energy per nucleon: The average binding energy associated with each nucleon in a nucleus is called binding energy per nucleon.
\[ \boxed{\frac{BE}{A}} \]

A larger value of \(BE/A\) generally indicates greater nuclear stability.

Example 4: The mass defect of a nucleus is \(0.030\,\mathrm{u}\). Calculate its binding energy.
Show solution
\[ BE=\Delta m\times931.5 \]
\[ BE=0.030\times931.5 =27.945\,\mathrm{MeV} \]

Answer: \(BE\approx27.95\,\mathrm{MeV}\).

Example 5: A nucleus has a binding energy of \(56\,\mathrm{MeV}\) and mass number \(A=14\). Find its binding energy per nucleon.
Show solution
\[ \frac{BE}{A}=\frac{56}{14}=4\,\mathrm{MeV} \]

Answer: Binding energy per nucleon \(=4\,\mathrm{MeV}\).

24.9 Binding Energy per Nucleon Curve

The binding energy per nucleon varies with mass number. A graph of \(BE/A\) against mass number \(A\) is called the binding-energy curve.

Features of the Binding-Energy Curve

  • For very light nuclei, \(BE/A\) is comparatively small.
  • \(BE/A\) rises rapidly as mass number increases from very small values.
  • It reaches a maximum in the region of iron and nickel, around \(A\approx56\) to \(62\).
  • For very heavy nuclei, \(BE/A\) gradually decreases.
  • Nuclei near the maximum are among the most tightly bound and stable nuclei.

Energy Release from the Curve

Nuclear energy can be released by two important processes:

  • Fusion: Light nuclei combine to form a heavier nucleus with a higher \(BE/A\).
  • Fission: A heavy nucleus splits into medium-mass nuclei with higher \(BE/A\).

In both cases, the final products are more tightly bound. The increase in total binding energy appears as released energy.

24.10 Nuclear Fusion and Fission

Nuclear Fusion

Nuclear fusion: The process in which two or more light nuclei combine to form a heavier nucleus with the release of a large amount of energy is called nuclear fusion.

A typical example is the fusion of hydrogen isotopes in which several light nuclei ultimately produce helium.

\[ {}^{2}_{1}\mathrm{H}+{}^{3}_{1}\mathrm{H} \rightarrow {}^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He}+{}^{1}_{0}\mathrm{n}+17.6\,\mathrm{MeV} \]

Mechanism of Energy Release in Fusion

  1. Light nuclei approach one another at extremely high temperature.
  2. They must overcome the electrostatic repulsion between their positively charged nuclei.
  3. At sufficiently small separation, the strong nuclear force becomes dominant.
  4. The nuclei combine to form a more tightly bound nucleus.
  5. The mass of the products is less than the total initial mass.
  6. The mass difference is converted into energy according to \(E=\Delta mc^2\).
Fusion powers stars. The extremely high temperature and pressure inside stars allow light nuclei to undergo fusion.

Nuclear Fission

Nuclear fission: The process in which a heavy nucleus splits into two medium-mass nuclei, usually accompanied by the emission of neutrons and a large amount of energy, is called nuclear fission.

A common example is the fission of uranium-235 after absorbing a neutron:

\[ {}^{235}_{92}\mathrm{U}+{}^{1}_{0}\mathrm{n} \rightarrow {}^{141}_{56}\mathrm{Ba} + {}^{92}_{36}\mathrm{Kr} + 3{}^{1}_{0}\mathrm{n} +\text{energy} \]

The exact fission products can vary. The important features are conservation of mass number, atomic number and energy.

Mechanism of Energy Release in Fission

  1. A heavy nucleus such as uranium-235 absorbs a neutron.
  2. The nucleus becomes excited and unstable.
  3. It deforms and splits into two smaller nuclei.
  4. Additional neutrons are released.
  5. The products have greater binding energy per nucleon than the original heavy nucleus.
  6. The total mass of the products is slightly less than the initial mass.
  7. The mass defect is converted into energy through \(E=\Delta mc^2\).

Chain Reaction

The neutrons released during one fission reaction can cause further fission reactions. This process is called a chain reaction.

  • Controlled chain reaction: The rate of fission is controlled, as in a nuclear reactor.
  • Uncontrolled chain reaction: A very rapid multiplication of fission reactions can occur when conditions allow, producing an enormous release of energy.

Fusion vs Fission

Fusion Fission
Light nuclei combine. Heavy nucleus splits.
Produces a heavier nucleus. Produces two or more medium-mass nuclei.
Requires extremely high temperature and suitable pressure. Can be initiated by neutron absorption in suitable fissile nuclei.
Occurs naturally in stars. Used in nuclear reactors.
Energy is released because the products have greater binding energy per nucleon. Energy is released because the fission products have greater binding energy per nucleon.

Numerical Problems Related to Nuclear Physics

Problem 1: Number of Neutrons

Question: Find the number of neutrons in \({}^{238}_{92}\mathrm{U}\).
Show solution
\[ N=A-Z \]
\[ N=238-92=146 \]

Answer: The nucleus contains \(146\) neutrons.

Problem 2: Nuclear Radius

Question: Calculate the radius of a nucleus having mass number \(A=125\). Take \(R_0=1.2\times10^{-15}\,\mathrm{m}\).
Show solution
\[ R=R_0A^{1/3} \]
\[ R=(1.2\times10^{-15})(125)^{1/3} \]
\[ R=(1.2\times10^{-15})(5) =6.0\times10^{-15}\,\mathrm{m} \]

Answer: \(R=6.0\times10^{-15}\,\mathrm{m}\).

Problem 3: Mass Defect and Binding Energy

Question: The mass defect of a nucleus is \(0.010\,\mathrm{u}\). Calculate its binding energy in MeV and joules.
Show solution

In MeV,

\[ BE=0.010\times931.5=9.315\,\mathrm{MeV} \]

Since \(1\,\mathrm{eV}=1.602\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}\),

\[ 9.315\,\mathrm{MeV} =9.315\times10^6\times1.602\times10^{-19} \]
\[ BE\approx1.492\times10^{-12}\,\mathrm{J} \]

Answer: \(BE\approx9.315\,\mathrm{MeV}\approx1.49\times10^{-12}\,\mathrm{J}\).

Problem 4: Binding Energy per Nucleon

Question: A nucleus of mass number \(A=56\) has a binding energy of \(492\,\mathrm{MeV}\). Calculate its binding energy per nucleon.
Show solution
\[ \frac{BE}{A}=\frac{492}{56} \]
\[ \frac{BE}{A}\approx8.79\,\mathrm{MeV} \]

Answer: \(BE/A\approx8.79\,\mathrm{MeV\,nucleon^{-1}}\).

Problem 5: Energy Released from Mass Defect

Question: A nuclear reaction produces a mass defect of \(0.20\,\mathrm{u}\). Calculate the energy released.
Show solution
\[ E=\Delta m c^2 \]

Using \(1\,\mathrm{u}\approx931.5\,\mathrm{MeV}/c^2\),

\[ E=0.20\times931.5 \]
\[ \boxed{E=186.3\,\mathrm{MeV}} \]

In joules,

\[ E=186.3\times10^6\times1.602\times10^{-19} \]
\[ E\approx2.985\times10^{-11}\,\mathrm{J} \]

Answer: \(186.3\,\mathrm{MeV}\approx2.99\times10^{-11}\,\mathrm{J}\).

Problem 6: Binding Energy Using Atomic Masses

Question: For a nucleus with atomic number \(Z\), mass number \(A\), atomic mass \(M\), hydrogen-atom mass \(m_H\), and neutron mass \(m_n\), derive the expression for its binding energy using atomic masses.
Show solution

The nucleus contains \(Z\) protons and \(A-Z\) neutrons.

Using atomic masses, the electron masses cancel when the mass of \(Z\) hydrogen atoms is compared with the neutral atom.

\[ \Delta m=Zm_H+(A-Z)m_n-M \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{BE=\left[Zm_H+(A-Z)m_n-M\right]c^2} \]

If the masses are given in atomic mass unit,

\[ \boxed{BE(\mathrm{MeV})= \left[Zm_H+(A-Z)m_n-M\right]\times931.5} \]

Common Mistakes

  • Do not confuse mass number \(A\) with atomic number \(Z\).
  • Remember \(A=Z+N\), so \(N=A-Z\).
  • Do not use \(A\) as the number of protons; \(Z\) is the number of protons.
  • Use \(R=R_0A^{1/3}\), not \(R=R_0A\).
  • Nuclear density is approximately independent of mass number because both nuclear mass and volume are proportional to \(A\).
  • Mass defect is converted into binding energy through \(E=\Delta mc^2\).
  • When \(\Delta m\) is in atomic mass unit, use \(931.5\,\mathrm{MeV}\) per atomic mass unit.
  • Binding energy per nucleon is \(BE/A\), not \(BE\times A\).
  • Do not say that the nucleus with the largest total binding energy is necessarily the most stable. For comparison of nuclei of different sizes, \(BE/A\) is more useful.
  • Fusion involves light nuclei combining, whereas fission involves a heavy nucleus splitting.
  • Both fission and fusion release energy when the products have greater total binding energy than the initial nuclei.
  • In nuclear equations, conserve both mass number and atomic number.

Key Points

  • The nucleus was discovered through Rutherford's alpha-particle scattering experiment.
  • The nucleus contains protons and neutrons, collectively called nucleons.
  • Atomic number \(Z\) is the number of protons.
  • Mass number \(A\) is the total number of protons and neutrons.
  • Number of neutrons \(N=A-Z\).
  • Nuclear radius is \(R=R_0A^{1/3}\).
  • Nuclear density is approximately independent of mass number.
  • Isotopes have the same atomic number but different mass numbers.
  • Einstein's mass-energy relation is \(E=mc^2\).
  • Mass defect is the difference between the combined mass of free nucleons and the actual nuclear mass.
  • Binding energy is \(BE=\Delta mc^2\).
  • Binding energy per nucleon is \(BE/A\).
  • The binding-energy curve reaches a maximum near the iron/nickel region.
  • Fusion combines light nuclei; fission splits heavy nuclei.
  • Energy released in nuclear reactions is associated with the mass defect and increase in binding energy.
  • For mass defect in atomic mass unit, \(BE(\mathrm{MeV})=\Delta m(\mathrm{u})\times931.5\).
