22. Semiconductor Devices

Semiconductor devices are electronic devices made from semiconductor materials such as silicon and germanium. Their electrical properties can be controlled by doping and by applying suitable external voltages. Important semiconductor devices include PN junction diodes, Zener diodes, rectifiers and logic gates.

22.1 Formation of PN Junction and Semiconductor Diode

Semiconductor

A semiconductor is a material whose electrical conductivity lies between that of a conductor and an insulator. The most commonly used semiconductor materials are silicon (Si) and germanium (Ge).

Intrinsic Semiconductor

A pure semiconductor is called an intrinsic semiconductor. In an intrinsic semiconductor, the number of free electrons is equal to the number of holes.

Extrinsic Semiconductor

A semiconductor whose conductivity is increased by adding a small amount of suitable impurity is called an extrinsic semiconductor. The process of adding impurities is called doping.

n-Type Semiconductor

An n-type semiconductor is obtained by doping a pure semiconductor with a pentavalent impurity such as phosphorus, arsenic or antimony.

Four electrons of the impurity atom participate in covalent bonding, while the fifth electron is weakly bound and becomes a free electron.

  • Majority carriers: electrons
  • Minority carriers: holes

p-Type Semiconductor

A p-type semiconductor is obtained by doping a pure semiconductor with a trivalent impurity such as boron, aluminium or gallium.

Because the impurity atom has only three valence electrons, one covalent bond remains incomplete. This produces a hole.

  • Majority carriers: holes
  • Minority carriers: electrons

Formation of PN Junction

When a p-type semiconductor is joined with an n-type semiconductor, a PN junction is formed.

Immediately after the junction is formed, the concentration of holes is high on the p-side and the concentration of electrons is high on the n-side. Therefore, holes diffuse from the p-side towards the n-side, while electrons diffuse from the n-side towards the p-side.

Near the junction, electrons and holes recombine. This leaves behind immobile positively charged donor ions on the n-side and negatively charged acceptor ions on the p-side.

The region around the junction depleted of mobile charge carriers is called the depletion region or depletion layer.

Potential Barrier

The immobile ions in the depletion region produce an electric field directed from the positively charged n-side towards the negatively charged p-side. This electric field opposes further diffusion of majority carriers.

The potential difference developed across the depletion region is called the potential barrier or barrier potential.

At room temperature, the barrier potential is approximately \(0.7\,\text{V}\) for silicon and \(0.3\,\text{V}\) for germanium.

Depletion region: The region around a PN junction containing almost no mobile charge carriers because electrons and holes have recombined near the junction.
Potential barrier: The potential difference developed across the depletion region that opposes the diffusion of majority charge carriers across the junction.

Semiconductor Diode

A semiconductor diode is a two-terminal semiconductor device consisting essentially of a single PN junction. Its two terminals are called the anode and cathode.

Biasing of a PN Junction

Applying an external voltage across a PN junction is called biasing. There are two types of biasing:

  1. Forward bias
  2. Reverse bias

Forward Bias

A PN junction is said to be forward biased when the p-side is connected to the positive terminal and the n-side to the negative terminal of a battery.

Forward bias reduces the width of the depletion region and lowers the potential barrier. Majority carriers can therefore cross the junction more easily, producing a significant current.

Reverse Bias

A PN junction is said to be reverse biased when the p-side is connected to the negative terminal and the n-side to the positive terminal of the battery.

Reverse bias increases the width of the depletion region and increases the potential barrier. Only a very small current due to minority carriers flows through the junction. This current is called reverse saturation current.

22.2 Forward and Reverse Characteristics of a Semiconductor Diode

The relationship between the current through a diode and the voltage applied across it is called the V-I characteristic of the diode.

Forward Characteristic

In forward bias, initially the current is very small because the applied voltage is insufficient to overcome the potential barrier. As the applied voltage approaches the knee voltage, the current begins to increase rapidly.

The knee voltage is approximately \(0.7\,\text{V}\) for silicon and \(0.3\,\text{V}\) for germanium.

Reverse Characteristic

In reverse bias, only a small reverse saturation current flows. As the reverse voltage is increased to a sufficiently high value, the current increases sharply. This voltage is called the breakdown voltage.

Breakdown

At breakdown, a small increase in reverse voltage produces a very large increase in reverse current. If the current is not properly limited, an ordinary diode may be damaged.

Zener Diode

A Zener diode is a specially designed semiconductor diode intended to operate safely in the reverse-breakdown region.

It is heavily doped compared with an ordinary junction diode, giving it a thin depletion layer and a well-defined breakdown voltage called the Zener voltage, \(V_Z\).

Use of Zener Diode as a Voltage Regulator

A Zener diode can maintain an approximately constant voltage across a load when operated in its breakdown region. Therefore, it is commonly used as a voltage regulator.

Important distinction: An ordinary diode is generally operated in forward bias for rectification, whereas a Zener diode is specially designed to operate in reverse breakdown for voltage regulation.

22.3 Rectifier

Rectifier: A rectifier is an electronic device or circuit that converts alternating current (AC) into unidirectional or pulsating direct current (DC).

Since a semiconductor diode conducts mainly in one direction, it can be used for rectification.

Types of Rectification

  • Half-wave rectification: only one half-cycle of the AC input is used.
  • Full-wave rectification: both half-cycles of the AC input are converted into pulsating DC.
Exam point: Full-wave rectification is more efficient than half-wave rectification because both half-cycles of the AC input contribute to the output.

22.4 Full-Wave Rectification Using Semiconductor Diodes

A full-wave rectifier is a rectifier circuit that converts both the positive and negative half-cycles of an AC input into output current flowing in the same direction through the load.

A common full-wave rectifier uses four diodes in a bridge configuration. It is called a bridge rectifier.

Bridge Full-Wave Rectifier

Working During the Positive Half-Cycle

During the positive half-cycle of the AC input, one pair of diodes becomes forward biased and conducts current through the load. The other pair remains reverse biased.

The current through the load flows in a particular direction.

Working During the Negative Half-Cycle

During the negative half-cycle, the other pair of diodes becomes forward biased. The current through the load nevertheless flows in the same direction as during the positive half-cycle.

Thus both half-cycles of the AC input produce output current through the load in the same direction.

Output of a Full-Wave Rectifier

Advantages of Full-Wave Rectifier

  • Both half-cycles of AC are utilized.
  • Higher average output current than a half-wave rectifier.
  • Higher rectification efficiency.
  • Lower ripple compared with half-wave rectification.
  • Output frequency is twice the input AC frequency.

22.5 Logic Gates

Definition of Logic Gate

Logic gate: A logic gate is an electronic circuit that performs a logical operation on one or more binary inputs and produces a single binary output.

Digital electronic systems use two logical states:

  • Logic 0: LOW or false
  • Logic 1: HIGH or true

Logic gates are represented using symbols and can also be described using Boolean algebra and truth tables.

Basic Logic Gates

The important gates in this syllabus are:

  1. OR gate
  2. AND gate
  3. NOT gate
  4. NAND gate
  5. NOR gate

OR Gate

An OR gate gives an output of 1 if at least one of its inputs is 1. Its output is 0 only when all inputs are 0.

Universal Gates

NAND and NOR are called universal gates because either of them alone can be used to construct the basic gates and, consequently, any Boolean logic circuit.

Important: The small circle (bubble) at the output of an AND or OR symbol indicates inversion. Thus, AND + NOT gives NAND, while OR + NOT gives NOR.

Key Points Summary

  • A semiconductor has conductivity between that of a conductor and an insulator.
  • Doping produces extrinsic semiconductors.
  • n-type semiconductors have electrons as majority carriers.
  • p-type semiconductors have holes as majority carriers.
  • A PN junction contains a depletion region and a potential barrier.
  • Forward bias reduces the depletion region and allows significant current to flow.
  • Reverse bias widens the depletion region and produces only a small reverse current until breakdown.
  • The V-I characteristic shows the relationship between diode current and applied voltage.
  • A Zener diode is designed to operate in the reverse-breakdown region.
  • A Zener diode can be used as a voltage regulator.
  • A rectifier converts AC into unidirectional or pulsating DC.
  • A full-wave rectifier uses both half-cycles of the AC input.
  • In a bridge rectifier, four diodes are used.
  • The output frequency of a full-wave rectifier is twice the input frequency.
  • A logic gate performs a Boolean operation on binary inputs.
  • OR operation is represented by \(+\).
  • AND operation is represented by \(\cdot\).
  • NOT operation gives the complement of the input.
  • NAND is NOT-AND.
  • NOR is NOT-OR.
  • NAND and NOR are universal logic gates.
