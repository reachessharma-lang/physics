Polarization is an important phenomenon of wave optics that provides strong evidence for the transverse nature of light. A potentiometer is a sensitive electrical measuring instrument used to compare potential differences and determine the internal resistance of cells without drawing appreciable current from the source being measured.

11.1 Polarization of Light

Polarization: Polarization is the phenomenon of restricting the vibrations of light waves to a particular direction perpendicular to the direction of propagation.

Ordinary light emitted by sources such as the Sun, lamps, and candles consists of vibrations in all directions perpendicular to the direction of propagation. Such light is called unpolarized light.

When the vibrations of light are restricted to one particular direction perpendicular to the direction of propagation, the light is called plane-polarized or linearly polarized light.

Unpolarized and Plane-Polarized Light

Polarizer

A device that converts unpolarized light into plane-polarized light is called a polarizer.

Examples of polarizing materials include Polaroids and certain crystals such as tourmaline.

Analyzer

A device used to determine whether light is polarized and to investigate the direction of polarization is called an analyzer.

A Polaroid can act either as a polarizer or as an analyzer depending on its position in the optical arrangement.

Transmission of Light Through Two Polaroids

Suppose unpolarized light is incident on a polarizer. The emerging light becomes plane-polarized. If this light is then passed through a second Polaroid called the analyzer, the transmitted intensity depends on the angle between the transmission axes of the two Polaroids.

If the angle between the transmission axes of the polarizer and analyzer is \( \theta \), Malus' law gives

\[ I=I_0\cos^2\theta \] where \(I_0\) is the intensity of plane-polarized light incident on the analyzer and \(I\) is the transmitted intensity.

For initially unpolarized light of intensity \(I\), the intensity after passing through an ideal polarizer is \(I/2\). Therefore, after the analyzer,

\[ I'=\frac{I}{2}\cos^2\theta \] where \(\theta\) is the angle between the transmission axes of the polarizer and analyzer.
Important: When the axes of the polarizer and analyzer are parallel, \(\theta=0^\circ\), so maximum intensity is transmitted. When they are perpendicular, \(\theta=90^\circ\), and ideally no light is transmitted.

Polarization by Reflection

When unpolarized light is incident on a transparent non-metallic surface such as glass or water, the reflected light becomes partially or completely polarized for a particular angle of incidence.

At a particular angle of incidence, the reflected light is completely plane-polarized. This angle is called the polarizing angle or Brewster angle.

11.2 Polarization and the Transverse Nature of Light

Polarization provides direct evidence that light is a transverse wave.

In a transverse wave, the particles or field quantities vibrate perpendicular to the direction of propagation. Therefore, the vibrations can be restricted to one particular direction perpendicular to propagation.

In a longitudinal wave, the vibrations are parallel to the direction of propagation. Such vibrations cannot be restricted to one particular transverse direction by an ordinary polarizer.

Why polarization proves the transverse nature of light:
  • Polarization restricts the vibrations of light to a particular direction.
  • The restricted direction is perpendicular to the direction of propagation.
  • Only transverse waves possess vibrations that can be restricted in this manner.
  • Therefore, polarization establishes that light is a transverse wave.
Example 1: Intensity through an analyzer

Plane-polarized light of intensity \(100\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}\) is incident on an analyzer whose transmission axis makes an angle of \(60^\circ\) with the polarization direction. Find the transmitted intensity.

Show solution

Using Malus' law,

\[ I=I_0\cos^2\theta \]

Here,

\[ I_0=100\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}},\qquad \theta=60^\circ \]

Therefore,

\[ I=100\cos^2 60^\circ \]
\[ I=100\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^2 =25\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}} \]

Answer: \(25\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}\)

11.3 Brewster's Law

When unpolarized light is incident on a transparent surface, the reflected light becomes completely plane-polarized at a particular angle of incidence. This angle is called the Brewster angle or polarizing angle.

Brewster's law: The refractive index of a transparent medium is equal to the tangent of its polarizing angle.
\[ \mu=\tan i_p \] where \(\mu\) is the refractive index of the transparent medium and \(i_p\) is the polarizing or Brewster angle.

Derivation of Brewster's Law

At the polarizing angle \(i_p\), the reflected and refracted rays are mutually perpendicular.

Therefore,

\[ i_p+r=90^\circ \]

From Snell's law,

\[ \mu=\frac{\sin i_p}{\sin r} \]

Since \(r=90^\circ-i_p\),

\[ \mu=\frac{\sin i_p}{\sin(90^\circ-i_p)} \]

Using \(\sin(90^\circ-i_p)=\cos i_p\),

\[ \boxed{\mu=\tan i_p} \]
Example 2: Finding refractive index using Brewster's law

The polarizing angle for a transparent medium is \(60^\circ\). Find its refractive index.

Show solution

According to Brewster's law,

\[ \mu=\tan i_p \]

Given \(i_p=60^\circ\),

\[ \mu=\tan60^\circ=\sqrt{3} \]
\[ \boxed{\mu\approx1.73} \]

Answer: The refractive index is approximately \(1.73\).

Example 3: Finding Brewster angle

The refractive index of glass is \(1.5\). Find its polarizing angle.

Show solution

Using Brewster's law,

\[ \mu=\tan i_p \]

Therefore,

\[ i_p=\tan^{-1}(1.5) \]
\[ \boxed{i_p\approx56.3^\circ} \]

Answer: The polarizing angle is approximately \(56.3^\circ\).

Potentiometer

A potentiometer is an electrical instrument used for the accurate measurement of potential difference. It works on the principle that the potential drop across a uniform wire carrying a steady current is directly proportional to the length of the wire.

Potentiometer: A potentiometer is a device consisting of a long uniform resistance wire through which a steady current is passed and which is used to measure potential differences by comparing them with the potential drop along a known length of the wire.

Principle of Potentiometer

Consider a uniform potentiometer wire of length \(L\), cross-sectional area \(A\), and resistance \(R\), carrying a steady current \(I\).

The resistance of a length \(l\) of the wire is

\[ R_l=\rho\frac{l}{A} \]

The potential difference across this length is

\[ V_l=IR_l \]

Therefore,

\[ V_l=I\rho\frac{l}{A} \]

Since \(I\), \(\rho\), and \(A\) are constant,

\[ V_l\propto l \]

Hence,

\[ \boxed{V=kl} \] where \(k\) is the potential gradient of the potentiometer wire.
Potential gradient: The potential drop per unit length of the potentiometer wire is called the potential gradient.
\[ \boxed{k=\frac{V}{L}} \] where \(V\) is the potential difference across the length \(L\).

Construction of a Potentiometer

A practical potentiometer consists mainly of:

  • A long uniform resistance wire, usually several metres long.
  • A battery or driver cell to provide a steady current through the wire.
  • A key or switch.
  • A rheostat to control the current.
  • A galvanometer to detect the null point.
  • A jockey to make electrical contact with different points of the potentiometer wire.
  • A cell whose emf or internal resistance is to be measured.

Working Principle

When a steady current is passed through the uniform potentiometer wire, there is a uniform potential drop along its length. Therefore, the potential difference between two points is proportional to the distance between them.

\[ V=kl \]

The jockey is moved along the wire until the galvanometer shows zero deflection. This position is called the null point or balance point.

At the null point, no current flows through the galvanometer branch. Hence the potentiometer method is a null method and provides highly accurate measurements.

Comparing the EMFs of Two Cells Using a Potentiometer

Suppose two cells having emfs \(E_1\) and \(E_2\) are compared using the same potentiometer wire.

Let their respective balance lengths be \(l_1\) and \(l_2\).

At balance,

\[ E_1=kl_1 \]

and

\[ E_2=kl_2 \]

Dividing these equations,

\[ \boxed{\frac{E_1}{E_2}=\frac{l_1}{l_2}} \]

Thus, the ratio of the emfs of two cells is equal to the ratio of their corresponding balance lengths.

Example 4: Comparing two emfs

The balance lengths for two cells are \(80\,\mathrm{cm}\) and \(100\,\mathrm{cm}\). If the emf of the first cell is \(1.2\,\mathrm{V}\), find the emf of the second cell.

Show solution

For comparison of emfs,

\[ \frac{E_1}{E_2}=\frac{l_1}{l_2} \]

Therefore,

\[ E_2=E_1\frac{l_2}{l_1} \]
\[ E_2=1.2\times\frac{100}{80} \]
\[ \boxed{E_2=1.5\,\mathrm{V}} \]

Answer: The emf of the second cell is \(1.5\,\mathrm{V}\).

Measurement of Internal Resistance of a Cell Using a Potentiometer

The potentiometer can be used to determine the internal resistance of a cell by comparing its emf with its terminal potential difference when an external resistance is connected across it.

Let

  • \(E\) = emf of the cell
  • \(V\) = terminal potential difference when the cell supplies current
  • \(r\) = internal resistance of the cell
  • \(R\) = external resistance connected across the cell

First, when the external circuit is open, the balance length is \(l_1\). Therefore,

\[ E=kl_1 \]

Now a known resistance \(R\) is connected across the cell. The terminal potential difference is measured using the potentiometer, and the new balance length is \(l_2\).

\[ V=kl_2 \]

Therefore,

\[ \frac{E}{V}=\frac{l_1}{l_2} \]

For a cell of internal resistance \(r\) connected to external resistance \(R\),

\[ V=\frac{ER}{R+r} \]

Hence,

\[ \frac{E}{V}=\frac{R+r}{R} \]

Thus,

\[ \frac{l_1}{l_2}=\frac{R+r}{R} \]

Solving for \(r\),

\[ \boxed{r=R\left(\frac{l_1}{l_2}-1\right)} \]

Therefore, the internal resistance of the cell can be determined from the known external resistance and the two balance lengths.

Example 5: Internal resistance of a cell

A cell gives a balance length of \(100\,\mathrm{cm}\) when its circuit is open. When a resistance of \(5\,\Omega\) is connected across the cell, the balance length becomes \(80\,\mathrm{cm}\). Find the internal resistance of the cell.

Show solution

Using

\[ r=R\left(\frac{l_1}{l_2}-1\right) \]

Given

\[ R=5\,\Omega,\qquad l_1=100\,\mathrm{cm},\qquad l_2=80\,\mathrm{cm} \]

Therefore,

\[ r=5\left(\frac{100}{80}-1\right) \]
\[ r=5(1.25-1) \]
\[ \boxed{r=1.25\,\Omega} \]

Answer: The internal resistance of the cell is \(1.25\,\Omega\).

Uses of a Potentiometer

  • To compare the emfs of two cells.
  • To determine the internal resistance of a cell.
  • To measure an unknown potential difference accurately.
  • To determine the potential gradient of a potentiometer wire.
  • To calibrate voltmeters and ammeters.

Advantages of a Potentiometer

  • It is a null method and hence provides accurate measurements.
  • At the balance point, no current is drawn from the test cell through the galvanometer branch.
  • It can compare the emfs of cells accurately.
  • It can measure small potential differences.
  • Its sensitivity can be increased by decreasing the potential gradient.

Common Mistakes

  • Do not use Celsius temperature in optical or thermal formulas where absolute temperature is required; use kelvin where applicable.
  • Do not confuse the polarizing angle \(i_p\) with the angle of refraction \(r\).
  • At Brewster angle, the reflected and refracted rays are perpendicular: \(i_p+r=90^\circ\).
  • Brewster's law is \(\mu=\tan i_p\), not \(\mu=\sin i_p\).
  • Polarization proves the transverse nature of light because only transverse waves can have vibrations restricted to a particular direction perpendicular to propagation.
  • For a potentiometer, the balance condition is based on \(V=kl\).
  • For comparing emfs, use \(\frac{E_1}{E_2}=\frac{l_1}{l_2}\).
  • For internal resistance, the open-circuit balance length must be greater than the loaded balance length.
  • Do not confuse emf \(E\) with terminal potential difference \(V\).

Key Points Summary

  • Polarization is the restriction of light vibrations to a particular direction perpendicular to propagation.
  • Unpolarized light vibrates in many directions perpendicular to its direction of propagation.
  • Plane-polarized light has vibrations restricted to one direction.
  • Polarization proves that light is a transverse wave.
  • Malus' law is \(I=I_0\cos^2\theta\).
  • Brewster's law is \(\mu=\tan i_p\).
  • At Brewster angle, the reflected and refracted rays are perpendicular.
  • A potentiometer works on the principle \(V\propto l\).
  • The potential gradient is \(k=V/L\).
  • For comparing two emfs, \(\frac{E_1}{E_2}=\frac{l_1}{l_2}\).
  • For measuring internal resistance, \(r=R\left(\frac{l_1}{l_2}-1\right)\).
  • A potentiometer is more accurate than an ordinary voltmeter for comparing potential differences because it uses a null method.
