Chapter 21: Potential, Potential Difference and Potential Energy

Electric potential is one of the most important concepts in electrostatics. While electric field describes the force experienced by a charge, electric potential describes the work required to bring a unit positive charge from infinity to a particular point in an electric field. Potential is a scalar quantity, so it can be added algebraically.

This chapter develops the concepts of electric potential, potential difference, electric potential energy, electron volt, potential due to a point charge, potential gradient, equipotential surfaces and the relation between electric field and potential difference in a uniform electric field.

  • Electric potential is work done per unit positive test charge.
  • The SI unit of electric potential is volt (V).
  • Potential is a scalar quantity.
  • For a point charge, \(V=\dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\dfrac{Q}{r}\).
  • Electric field strength is related to potential gradient by \(E=-\dfrac{dV}{dx}\).
  • No work is done in moving a charge along an equipotential surface.
  • For a uniform electric field, \(E=\dfrac{\Delta V}{\Delta x}\) in magnitude.

21.1 Electric Potential at a Point

Definition: Electric potential at a point is the work done per unit positive test charge in bringing a small positive test charge from infinity to that point, without producing acceleration of the test charge.

Suppose a small positive test charge \(q_0\) is brought from infinity to a point \(P\) in an electric field. If the work done by the external agent is \(W\), then the electric potential at \(P\) is

\[ V=\frac{W}{q_0} \] where \(V\) is electric potential, \(W\) is work done, and \(q_0\) is the positive test charge.

SI Unit of Potential

From

\[ V=\frac{W}{q_0} \] where work is measured in joule and charge in coulomb.

Therefore,

\[ 1\ \mathrm{volt}=1\ \mathrm{joule\ per\ coulomb} \] \(1\ \mathrm{V}=1\ \mathrm{J\,C^{-1}}\).
Important: Electric potential is a scalar quantity. It has magnitude and sign but no direction.

Reference Potential

Electric potential is defined relative to a reference point. For isolated charges, infinity is generally chosen as the reference point and its potential is taken as zero:

\[ V_{\infty}=0 \]

Potential Due to a Positive and Negative Charge

For a positive source charge, the potential at a point is positive when infinity is taken as zero. For a negative source charge, the potential is negative.

\[ V=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{Q}{r} \] \(Q\) is the source charge and \(r\) is the distance of the point from the charge.

Work Done and Potential Difference

If a positive test charge \(q_0\) is moved from point \(A\) to point \(B\), the work done by the electric field is related to the potential difference by

\[ W_{\mathrm{field}}=q_0(V_A-V_B) \] \(V_A\) and \(V_B\) are the potentials at \(A\) and \(B\).

Therefore, if \(V_B>V_A\), the electric field does negative work on a positive charge when it is moved from \(A\) to \(B\).

21.2 Electron Volt as a Unit of Electric Potential Energy

Electron volt: One electron volt is the amount of energy gained by an electron when it moves through a potential difference of one volt in the direction of increasing electric potential energy.

The magnitude of charge on an electron is

\[ e=1.602\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{C} \]

When a charge \(e\) moves through a potential difference of \(1\ \mathrm{V}\), the energy change in magnitude is

\[ 1\ \mathrm{eV}=e\times1\ \mathrm{V} \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{1\ \mathrm{eV}=1.602\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{J}} \]

Conversion Between Electron Volt and Joule

\[ 1\ \mathrm{J}=\frac{1}{1.602\times10^{-19}}\ \mathrm{eV} \]
\[ 1\ \mathrm{J}\approx6.242\times10^{18}\ \mathrm{eV} \]

Electron volt is particularly convenient for expressing very small energies involved in atomic, molecular and nuclear physics.

Relation Between Potential Energy and Potential

If a charge \(q\) is placed at a point having electric potential \(V\), its electric potential energy is

\[ U=qV \] \(U\) is electric potential energy, \(q\) is the charge and \(V\) is electric potential.

For an electron, \(q=-e\), so

\[ U=-eV \]
Worked Example 1: Conversion of electron volt to joule

Problem: Convert \(5\ \mathrm{eV}\) into joules.

Show solution

We know that

\[ 1\ \mathrm{eV}=1.602\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{J} \]

Therefore,

\[ 5\ \mathrm{eV}=5(1.602\times10^{-19}) \]
\[ \boxed{5\ \mathrm{eV}=8.01\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{J}} \]

21.3 Potential in the Field of a Point Charge

Consider an isolated point charge \(Q\). Let \(P\) be a point at distance \(r\) from the charge. The electric field at distance \(x\) from the point charge is

\[ E=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{Q}{x^2} \]

The small work required to move a positive test charge \(q_0\) through a small distance \(dx\) towards the charge is related to the potential difference. Taking infinity as the reference point, the potential at \(P\) is obtained by integration.

Derivation of Potential Due to a Point Charge

The electric field due to \(Q\) at distance \(x\) is

\[ E=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{Q}{x^2} \]

The work done by an external agent in moving a positive test charge through a small distance \(dx\) against the electric field is

\[ dW=q_0E\,dx \]

Hence, the total work done in bringing \(q_0\) from infinity to a point at distance \(r\) is

\[ W=q_0\int_{\infty}^{r}(-E)\,dx \]

Equivalently, using the potential definition and integrating the field contribution from \(r\) to infinity,

\[ V=-\int_{\infty}^{r}\vec E\cdot d\vec r \]

For the point charge,

\[ V=-\int_{\infty}^{r}\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{Q}{x^2}\,dx \]

Taking the constant outside the integral,

\[ V=-\frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int_{\infty}^{r}\frac{dx}{x^2} \]

Since

\[ \int\frac{dx}{x^2}=-\frac{1}{x} \]

we obtain

\[ V=-\frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \left[-\frac{1}{x}\right]_{\infty}^{r} \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{V=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{Q}{r}} \] where \(V\) is potential at distance \(r\) from point charge \(Q\).
Potential due to a point charge:
\[ V=\frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r} \]

In air or vacuum, \(\dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\approx9\times10^9\ \mathrm{N\,m^2\,C^{-2}}\), so

\[ \boxed{V=\frac{9\times10^9Q}{r}} \]

Potential Due to Several Point Charges

Since potential is a scalar quantity, the potential due to several point charges is the algebraic sum of their individual potentials.

\[ V=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \left( \frac{Q_1}{r_1}+ \frac{Q_2}{r_2}+ \frac{Q_3}{r_3}+\cdots \right) \] \(r_1,r_2,r_3,\ldots\) are distances of the point from the corresponding charges.
Important: Unlike electric field, potential does not require vector addition. Positive and negative potentials are simply added algebraically.
Worked Example 2: Potential due to a point charge

Problem: Calculate the electric potential at a point \(0.20\ \mathrm{m}\) from a point charge of \(4\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{C}\). Take \(k=9\times10^9\ \mathrm{N\,m^2\,C^{-2}}\).

Show solution

Given:

  • \(Q=4\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{C}\)
  • \(r=0.20\ \mathrm{m}\)
  • \(k=9\times10^9\ \mathrm{N\,m^2\,C^{-2}}\)

Using

\[ V=\frac{kQ}{r} \]

Therefore,

\[ V=\frac{(9\times10^9)(4\times10^{-8})}{0.20} \]
\[ V=\frac{360}{0.20}=1800\ \mathrm{V} \]

Answer:

\[ \boxed{V=1.8\times10^3\ \mathrm{V}} \]

21.4 Variation of Potential with Distance and Potential Gradient

Variation of Potential Due to a Point Charge

For a point charge \(Q\),

\[ V=\frac{kQ}{r} \]

Therefore, for a fixed source charge,

\[ V\propto\frac{1}{r} \]

Thus, the potential decreases inversely with distance from the source charge. It does not decrease as \(1/r^2\); that relation applies to electric field strength.

For a negative point charge, the potential is negative and approaches zero from below as the distance increases.

Potential Gradient

The rate of change of potential with distance along a particular direction is called the potential gradient.

Potential gradient: The potential gradient at a point is the rate of decrease of electric potential per unit distance in the direction of the electric field.

Consider a small displacement \(dx\) in the direction of the electric field. The potential difference is related to the electric field by

\[ dV=-E\,dx \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{E=-\frac{dV}{dx}} \] \(E\) is electric field strength and \(\dfrac{dV}{dx}\) is the potential gradient.
Relation between electric field and potential gradient:
\[ \boxed{E=-\frac{dV}{dx}} \]

The negative sign indicates that the electric field is directed from higher potential towards lower potential.

Derivation of \(E=-dV/dx\)

Consider a positive test charge \(q\) moved through a small distance \(dx\) in an electric field \(E\).

The work done by the electric field is

\[ dW=-q\,dV \]

But work done by the electric field for a small displacement in its direction is

\[ dW=qE\,dx \]

Equating the two expressions,

\[ qE\,dx=-q\,dV \]

Dividing by \(q\,dx\),

\[ \boxed{E=-\frac{dV}{dx}} \]

Physical Meaning of the Negative Sign

  • Electric field points in the direction in which potential decreases.
  • Potential decreases in the direction of the electric field.
  • A positive charge naturally moves from higher potential to lower potential.
  • The magnitude of electric field is large where potential changes rapidly with distance.
Worked Example 3: Electric field from potential gradient

Problem: The potential along the \(x\)-axis is given by \(V=100-20x\), where \(V\) is in volts and \(x\) is in metres. Find the electric field strength.

Show solution

Given

\[ V=100-20x \]

Electric field is

\[ E=-\frac{dV}{dx} \]

Therefore,

\[ \frac{dV}{dx}=-20\ \mathrm{V\,m^{-1}} \]

Hence,

\[ E=-(-20)=20\ \mathrm{V\,m^{-1}} \]

Answer:

\[ \boxed{E=20\ \mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}} \]

The field is directed in the positive \(x\)-direction.

21.5 Equipotential Lines and Surfaces

Equipotential surface: A surface on which every point has the same electric potential is called an equipotential surface.
Equipotential line: In a two-dimensional representation, a line joining points having the same electric potential is called an equipotential line.

Potential Difference Between Two Points on an Equipotential

If points \(A\) and \(B\) lie on the same equipotential surface,

\[ V_A=V_B \]

Therefore, the potential difference is

\[ \Delta V=V_B-V_A=0 \]

The work done in moving a charge \(q\) from \(A\) to \(B\) is

\[ W=q\Delta V \]

Since \(\Delta V=0\),

\[ \boxed{W=0} \]
Important property: No work is done in moving a charge along an equipotential surface.

Equipotential Surfaces Around a Point Charge

For an isolated point charge, all points at the same distance from the charge have the same potential. Therefore, the equipotential surfaces are concentric spherical surfaces centered on the charge.

Properties of Equipotential Surfaces

  • Every point on an equipotential surface has the same potential.
  • No work is done in moving a charge along an equipotential surface.
  • Electric field is always perpendicular to an equipotential surface.
  • Two equipotential surfaces never intersect.
  • For a point charge, equipotential surfaces are concentric spheres.
  • For a uniform electric field, equipotential surfaces are parallel planes perpendicular to the field.
  • Closer equipotential surfaces indicate a stronger electric field because potential changes more rapidly with distance.

Why is Electric Field Perpendicular to an Equipotential Surface?

Suppose a component of electric field existed along an equipotential surface. A charge moved along that component would experience a force and work would be done. But the potential difference along an equipotential surface is zero, so no work can be done.

Therefore, the electric field can have no component along an equipotential surface and must be perpendicular to it.

21.6 Electric Field Between Charged Parallel Plates

Consider two large, oppositely charged parallel conducting plates separated by a distance \(d\). Neglecting edge effects, the electric field between the plates is approximately uniform.

Relation Between Electric Field and Potential Difference

Let the potential difference between two parallel plates be \(\Delta V\), and let their separation be \(\Delta x\). For a uniform field, the potential changes uniformly with distance.

From the potential-gradient relation,

\[ E=-\frac{\Delta V}{\Delta x} \]

When only the magnitude of field strength is required,

\[ \boxed{E=\frac{\Delta V}{\Delta x}} \] \(E\) is the magnitude of electric field strength, \(\Delta V\) is the potential difference and \(\Delta x\) is the separation.

If the plates are separated by distance \(d\) and the potential difference between them is \(V\), then

\[ \boxed{E=\frac{V}{d}} \]

Derivation of \(E=\Delta V/\Delta x\)

For a uniform electric field, the potential gradient is constant. Therefore,

\[ E=-\frac{dV}{dx} \]

For a finite separation, integration gives

\[ \int E\,dx=-\int dV \]

Since \(E\) is constant,

\[ E\Delta x=V_A-V_B \]

Thus, in magnitude,

\[ \boxed{E=\frac{\Delta V}{\Delta x}} \]
Direction: The electric field is directed from the plate at higher potential to the plate at lower potential. The magnitude relation \(E=\Delta V/\Delta x\) does not show direction; the vector relation contains the negative sign.

Potential Difference Across Parallel Plates

Rearranging

\[ E=\frac{V}{d} \]

gives

\[ \boxed{V=Ed} \]

Force on a Charge Between Parallel Plates

A charge \(q\) placed between the plates experiences an electric force

\[ F=qE \]

Since \(E=V/d\),

\[ \boxed{F=\frac{qV}{d}} \]

The direction of the force depends on the sign of the charge. A positive charge experiences force in the direction of the electric field, whereas a negative charge experiences force opposite to the electric field.

Worked Example 4: Field between parallel plates

Problem: Two parallel plates are separated by \(5\ \mathrm{cm}\) and maintained at a potential difference of \(500\ \mathrm{V}\). Find the electric field strength between the plates.

Show solution

Given:

  • \(V=500\ \mathrm{V}\)
  • \(d=5\ \mathrm{cm}=0.05\ \mathrm{m}\)

For a uniform electric field,

\[ E=\frac{V}{d} \]

Therefore,

\[ E=\frac{500}{0.05} \]
\[ \boxed{E=1.0\times10^4\ \mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}} \]

Since \(1\ \mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}=1\ \mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}\),

\[ \boxed{E=1.0\times10^4\ \mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}} \]

Potential Variation in a Uniform Electric Field

In a uniform electric field, the potential decreases linearly with distance in the direction of the electric field.

Potential Gradient from the Graph

For a graph of \(V\) against \(x\), the gradient is

\[ \text{gradient}=\frac{\Delta V}{\Delta x} \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{E=-\text{gradient of the }V-x\text{ graph}} \]
Worked Example 5: Potential difference and field strength

Problem: The potential decreases from \(1200\ \mathrm{V}\) to \(300\ \mathrm{V}\) over a distance of \(0.15\ \mathrm{m}\) in a uniform electric field. Find the magnitude of the electric field.

Show solution

Potential difference in magnitude:

\[ \Delta V=1200-300=900\ \mathrm{V} \]

Distance:

\[ \Delta x=0.15\ \mathrm{m} \]

Hence,

\[ E=\frac{\Delta V}{\Delta x} \]
\[ E=\frac{900}{0.15}=6000\ \mathrm{V\,m^{-1}} \]

Answer:

\[ \boxed{E=6.0\times10^3\ \mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}} \]

The field is directed from the \(1200\ \mathrm{V}\) point towards the \(300\ \mathrm{V}\) point.

Electric Potential Energy

When a charge is placed in an electric field, it possesses electric potential energy because of its position in the field.

Electric potential energy: Electric potential energy of a charge at a point is the work required to bring the charge from the chosen reference point to that point without changing its kinetic energy.

For a charge \(q\) at a point having potential \(V\),

\[ \boxed{U=qV} \]

For two point charges \(q_1\) and \(q_2\), separated by distance \(r\),

\[ \boxed{U=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q_1q_2}{r}} \]

If \(q_1q_2>0\), the potential energy is positive. If \(q_1q_2<0\), the potential energy is negative, when zero potential energy is taken at infinite separation.

Relation Between Potential Energy and Work

The change in electric potential energy is equal to the negative of the work done by the electric field:

\[ \boxed{\Delta U=-W_{\mathrm{field}}} \]

For a charge \(q\) moved from \(A\) to \(B\),

\[ \Delta U=q(V_B-V_A) \]

Therefore,

\[ W_{\mathrm{field}}=q(V_A-V_B) \]

Important Comparison: Electric Field and Electric Potential

Electric Field Electric Potential
Vector quantity Scalar quantity
Unit: \(\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}\) or \(\mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}\) Unit: volt (V)
Related to force per unit positive charge Related to work per unit positive charge
For a point charge, \(E\propto1/r^2\) For a point charge, \(V\propto1/r\)
Field points from high potential to low potential Potential decreases in the direction of the electric field
Field lines represent direction and relative strength Equipotential surfaces represent equal potential

Common Mistakes

  • Do not confuse electric potential \(V\) with potential energy \(U\). Potential is energy per unit charge, while potential energy is the actual energy of a charge.
  • Do not write \(V=\dfrac{kQ}{r^2}\). The correct expression is \(V=\dfrac{kQ}{r}\).
  • Remember that electric field varies as \(1/r^2\), whereas potential varies as \(1/r\) for a point charge.
  • Electric potential is scalar, so potentials are added algebraically.
  • Electric field is a vector, so electric fields must be added vectorially.
  • Do not forget the negative sign in \(E=-dV/dx\) when direction is required.
  • For the magnitude of a uniform electric field, use \(E=\Delta V/\Delta x\).
  • Convert centimetres into metres before calculating electric field using \(E=V/d\).
  • Do not say that an equipotential surface has zero electric field. The field is perpendicular to the surface and may be non-zero.
  • No work is done in moving a charge along an equipotential surface because \(\Delta V=0\).
  • One electron volt is \(1.602\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{J}\), not \(1.602\times10^{19}\ \mathrm{J}\).
  • For an electron, the charge is negative: \(q=-e\). Therefore, its potential energy is \(U=-eV\).
  • The electric field always points in the direction of decreasing potential.
