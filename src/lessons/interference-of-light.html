Interference is one of the important phenomena of wave optics. It provides direct evidence for the wave nature of light. When two or more coherent light waves superpose, the resultant intensity is redistributed, producing alternate bright and dark regions.

11.1 Explain the Phenomenon of Interference

Introduction

Interference of light is the phenomenon of redistribution of intensity produced when two or more coherent light waves travelling through the same region of space superpose.

Definition: The phenomenon of redistribution of intensity of light due to the superposition of two or more coherent light waves is called interference of light.

According to the principle of superposition, when two or more waves meet at a point, the resultant displacement is equal to the algebraic sum of the individual displacements.

\[ y = y_1+y_2 \]

If two waves of the same frequency and amplitude interfere, they produce alternate regions of maximum and minimum intensity.

Constructive and Destructive Interference

Consider two waves having the same frequency and amplitude:

\[ y_1=a\sin\omega t \] \[ y_2=a\sin(\omega t+\phi) \] where \(a\) is the amplitude, \(\omega\) is the angular frequency and \(\phi\) is the phase difference between the waves.

The resultant displacement is

\[ y=y_1+y_2 \] \[ y=2a\cos\frac{\phi}{2}\sin\left(\omega t+\frac{\phi}{2}\right) \] \[ A=2a\cos\frac{\phi}{2} \] where \(A\) is the amplitude of the resultant wave.

Since intensity is proportional to the square of amplitude,

\[ I\propto A^2 \] \[ I=4I_0\cos^2\frac{\phi}{2} \] for two identical waves, \(I_0\) is the intensity due to either wave alone.

Constructive Interference

When the two waves arrive at a point in the same phase, their amplitudes reinforce each other. The resultant amplitude and intensity are maximum.

Constructive interference: The interference in which the resultant intensity is maximum is called constructive interference.

For constructive interference,

\[ \phi=2n\pi \] \[ \Delta=n\lambda \] \[ n=0,1,2,3,\ldots \] \(\Delta\) is the path difference and \(\lambda\) is the wavelength.

The resultant amplitude is

\[ A_{\max}=2a \]

Therefore,

\[ I_{\max}=4I_0 \]

Destructive Interference

When the two waves arrive at a point in opposite phase, their amplitudes cancel each other partially or completely. For waves of equal amplitude, complete cancellation occurs.

Destructive interference: The interference in which the resultant intensity is minimum is called destructive interference.

For destructive interference,

\[ \phi=(2n+1)\pi \] \[ \Delta=\left(n+\frac12\right)\lambda \] \[ \Delta=\frac{(2n+1)\lambda}{2} \] where \(n=0,1,2,3,\ldots\).

For two waves of equal amplitude,

\[ A_{\min}=0 \] \[ I_{\min}=0 \]

Conditions for Sustained Interference

  • The sources must be coherent.
  • The two sources should have the same frequency or wavelength.
  • The phase difference between the sources should remain constant.
  • For clear fringes, the amplitudes or intensities of the two waves should be comparable.
  • The waves should have the same state of polarization for maximum visibility.
Important: Two independent ordinary light sources generally do not produce sustained interference because their phase difference changes randomly with time.

11.2 Understand the Meaning of Coherent Sources

Coherent Sources

Definition: Two sources of light are said to be coherent sources if they emit light waves of the same frequency and maintain a constant phase difference between them.

For sustained interference, the phase difference between the waves reaching a particular point must remain constant.

\[ \Delta\phi=\text{constant} \]

Thus, the two important conditions for coherent sources are:

  1. They must have the same frequency or wavelength.
  2. They must maintain a constant phase difference.

Production of Coherent Sources

Two independent light sources are generally not coherent. In Young's double-slit experiment, two coherent sources are obtained by illuminating two narrow slits with light from the same source.

Why is a single source used? Since both slits receive light originating from the same source, the phase relationship between the waves emerging from the two slits remains constant. Hence, the two slits act as coherent sources.

11.3 Describe Young's Double-Slit Experiment and Obtain the Expression for nth Order Maxima

Young's Double-Slit Experiment

Thomas Young demonstrated the interference of light using two narrow slits. The experiment is known as Young's double-slit experiment (YDSE).

Principle: Young's double-slit experiment demonstrates the wave nature of light by producing a stable pattern of alternate bright and dark fringes due to interference.

Experimental Arrangement

A monochromatic light source illuminates two narrow and closely spaced slits \(S_1\) and \(S_2\). The slits act as coherent sources. A screen is placed at a distance \(D\) from the slits, where \(D\) is much greater than the separation \(d\) between the slits.

Let:

  • \(d\) = separation between the two slits
  • \(D\) = distance between the slit plane and the screen
  • \(P\) = an arbitrary point on the screen
  • \(y\) = perpendicular distance of \(P\) from the central point \(O\)
  • \(\lambda\) = wavelength of the monochromatic light

Formation of Interference Fringes

The waves emerging from \(S_1\) and \(S_2\) interfere on the screen. At some points, the waves arrive in phase and produce bright fringes. At other points, they arrive in opposite phase and produce dark fringes.

The central point \(O\) is equidistant from \(S_1\) and \(S_2\). Therefore, the path difference at \(O\) is zero and a central bright fringe is formed there.

Path Difference

Consider a point \(P\) on the screen. The distances travelled by the waves from \(S_1\) and \(S_2\) to reach \(P\) are \(S_1P\) and \(S_2P\).

Therefore, the path difference is

\[ \Delta=S_2P-S_1P \]

From the geometry of the experiment, if \(\theta\) is the angle made by \(S_1P\) with the central axis,

\[ \Delta=d\sin\theta \]

For small angles,

\[ \sin\theta\approx\tan\theta \]

Since

\[ \tan\theta=\frac{y}{D} \] where \(y\) is the distance of the point \(P\) from the central bright fringe.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\Delta=\frac{dy}{D}} \]

Condition for Bright Fringe

For constructive interference, the path difference must be an integral multiple of the wavelength:

\[ \Delta=n\lambda \] \[ n=0,\pm1,\pm2,\pm3,\ldots \]

Using

\[ \Delta=\frac{dy}{D} \]

we get

\[ \frac{dy}{D}=n\lambda \]

Hence,

\[ \boxed{y_n=\frac{n\lambda D}{d}} \] where \(y_n\) is the position of the \(n\)th bright fringe from the central bright fringe.

Expression for nth-Order Maximum

The \(n\)th-order maximum is a bright fringe produced when the path difference is \(n\lambda\).

For the \(n\)th maximum,

\[ \Delta=n\lambda \]

But from Young's double-slit geometry,

\[ \Delta=d\sin\theta \]

Therefore,

\[ d\sin\theta_n=n\lambda \]

Hence, the exact angular position of the \(n\)th maximum is

\[ \boxed{\sin\theta_n=\frac{n\lambda}{d}} \]

For small angles, \(\sin\theta_n\approx\tan\theta_n\), and

\[ \tan\theta_n=\frac{y_n}{D} \]

Therefore,

\[ \frac{y_n}{D}=\frac{n\lambda}{d} \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{y_n=\frac{n\lambda D}{d}} \]
Position of nth-order maximum: \[ \boxed{y_n=\frac{n\lambda D}{d}} \]

The corresponding condition for maximum intensity is

\[ \boxed{d\sin\theta_n=n\lambda} \]

For small angles,

\[ \boxed{\theta_n\approx\frac{n\lambda}{d}} \]

Positions of Bright Fringes

Order Path difference Position from central maximum
Central maximum, \(n=0\) \(0\) \(y_0=0\)
First maximum, \(n=1\) \(\lambda\) \(y_1=\frac{\lambda D}{d}\)
Second maximum, \(n=2\) \(2\lambda\) \(y_2=\frac{2\lambda D}{d}\)
nth maximum \(n\lambda\) \(y_n=\frac{n\lambda D}{d}\)

Fringe Width

The distance between two successive bright fringes or two successive dark fringes is called the fringe width.

For two successive bright fringes,

\[ \beta=y_{n+1}-y_n \]

Using

\[ y_n=\frac{n\lambda D}{d} \]

we get

\[ \beta= \frac{(n+1)\lambda D}{d} - \frac{n\lambda D}{d} \] \[ \boxed{\beta=\frac{\lambda D}{d}} \]
Important conclusion: The fringe width is independent of the order of the fringe. Therefore, the bright and dark fringes are equally spaced on the screen under the usual small-angle approximation.

Positions of Dark Fringes

For destructive interference,

\[ \Delta=\left(n+\frac12\right)\lambda \]

Since

\[ \Delta=\frac{dy}{D} \]

we obtain

\[ \boxed{ y_n= \left(n+\frac12\right) \frac{\lambda D}{d} } \] where \(n=0,1,2,3,\ldots\).

Important Results of Young's Double-Slit Experiment

  • Condition for bright fringe: \(\Delta=n\lambda\).
  • Condition for dark fringe: \(\Delta=(2n+1)\lambda/2\).
  • Position of nth bright fringe: \(y_n=n\lambda D/d\).
  • Position of nth dark fringe: \(y_n=(n+\frac12)\lambda D/d\).
  • Fringe width: \(\beta=\lambda D/d\).
  • Fringe width increases with wavelength.
  • Fringe width increases when the screen distance \(D\) is increased.
  • Fringe width decreases when slit separation \(d\) is increased.
  • The central fringe is bright because the path difference at the centre is zero.

Worked Example 1 — Position of First Maximum

Problem: In Young's double-slit experiment, the wavelength of light is \(600\,\text{nm}\), the slit separation is \(0.5\,\text{mm}\), and the screen is \(2\,\text{m}\) away. Find the distance of the first bright fringe from the central maximum.
Show solution

Given:

\[ \lambda=600\times10^{-9}\,\text{m} \] \[ d=0.5\times10^{-3}\,\text{m} \] \[ D=2\,\text{m} \]

For the first maximum, \(n=1\).

\[ y_1=\frac{\lambda D}{d} \] \[ y_1= \frac{600\times10^{-9}\times2} {0.5\times10^{-3}} \] \[ y_1=2.4\times10^{-3}\,\text{m} \] \[ \boxed{y_1=2.4\,\text{mm}} \]

Worked Example 2 — Fringe Width

Problem: In Young's double-slit experiment, the wavelength of light is \(500\,\text{nm}\), the distance between the slits is \(0.25\,\text{mm}\), and the screen is \(1.5\,\text{m}\) from the slits. Calculate the fringe width.
Show solution \[ \beta=\frac{\lambda D}{d} \] Given, \[ \lambda=500\times10^{-9}\,\text{m} \] \[ D=1.5\,\text{m} \] \[ d=0.25\times10^{-3}\,\text{m} \] Therefore, \[ \beta= \frac{500\times10^{-9}\times1.5} {0.25\times10^{-3}} \] \[ \beta=3.0\times10^{-3}\,\text{m} \] \[ \boxed{\beta=3\,\text{mm}} \]

Worked Example 3 — Finding Wavelength

Problem: In a Young's double-slit experiment, the slit separation is \(0.4\,\text{mm}\), the screen is \(2\,\text{m}\) away, and the fringe width is \(3\,\text{mm}\). Find the wavelength of light used.
Show solution \[ \beta=\frac{\lambda D}{d} \] Therefore, \[ \lambda=\frac{\beta d}{D} \] Substituting, \[ \lambda= \frac{(3\times10^{-3})(0.4\times10^{-3})}{2} \] \[ \lambda=0.6\times10^{-6}\,\text{m} \] \[ \boxed{\lambda=600\,\text{nm}} \]

Common Mistakes

  • Do not confuse path difference with phase difference.
  • For a bright fringe, use \(\Delta=n\lambda\), not \((n+\frac12)\lambda\).
  • For a dark fringe, use \(\Delta=(n+\frac12)\lambda\).
  • In \(y_n=n\lambda D/d\), \(D\) is the distance from the double slit to the screen.
  • Use SI units when calculating numerical answers.
  • The exact condition for the \(n\)th maximum is \(d\sin\theta_n=n\lambda\). The expression \(y_n=n\lambda D/d\) uses the small-angle approximation.
  • The central maximum corresponds to \(n=0\), not \(n=1\).
Exam Note: In a derivation of the position of the \(n\)th-order maximum, clearly show the steps \[ \Delta=d\sin\theta \] \[ \Delta=n\lambda \] \[ d\sin\theta=n\lambda \] and, for small angles, \[ \boxed{y_n=\frac{n\lambda D}{d}}. \] This is the standard result required for Young's double-slit interference.

Key Points Summary

  • Interference is the redistribution of intensity due to superposition of coherent waves.
  • Constructive interference produces maximum intensity.
  • Destructive interference produces minimum intensity.
  • Coherent sources have the same frequency and a constant phase difference.
  • In YDSE, the two slits act as coherent sources because they are illuminated by the same source.
  • Path difference at a point \(P\) is \(\Delta=d\sin\theta\).
  • For small angles, \(\Delta=dy/D\).
  • Condition for nth maximum: \(d\sin\theta_n=n\lambda\).
  • Position of nth maximum: \(\boxed{y_n=n\lambda D/d}\).
  • Position of nth minimum: \(\boxed{y_n=(n+\frac12)\lambda D/d}\).
  • Fringe width: \(\boxed{\beta=\lambda D/d}\).
