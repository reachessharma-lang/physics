19. Alternating Currents

An alternating current (AC) is an electric current whose magnitude and direction change periodically with time. In a sinusoidal AC circuit, the instantaneous current and voltage are represented by sinusoidal functions.

\[ i=I_0\sin\omega t \]where \(I_0\) is the peak current and \(\omega\) is the angular frequency.
\[ v=V_0\sin\omega t \]where \(V_0\) is the peak voltage.

The angular frequency is related to frequency \(f\) and time period \(T\) by

\[ \omega=2\pi f=\frac{2\pi}{T} \]where \(f\) is frequency and \(T\) is the time period.

19.1 Peak and RMS Values of AC Current and Voltage

Peak Value

The peak value or maximum value of an alternating current is the greatest value attained by the current during a cycle. It is denoted by \(I_0\).

Similarly, the maximum value of AC voltage is denoted by \(V_0\).

Root Mean Square (RMS) Value

The rms value of an alternating current is the value of steady DC current that produces the same heating effect in a given resistor as the AC current over one complete cycle.

For a sinusoidal alternating current,

\[ I_{\mathrm{rms}}=\frac{I_0}{\sqrt{2}} \]where \(I_0\) is the peak current.

Similarly, the rms value of sinusoidal AC voltage is

\[ V_{\mathrm{rms}}=\frac{V_0}{\sqrt{2}} \]where \(V_0\) is the peak voltage.

Therefore,

\[ I_0=\sqrt{2}\,I_{\mathrm{rms}} \]where \(I_0\) is the peak current.
\[ V_0=\sqrt{2}\,V_{\mathrm{rms}} \]\[ i=I_0\sin\omega t \]where \(I_0\) is the maximum current.

The mean square current over one complete cycle is

\[ I_{\mathrm{rms}}^2=\frac{1}{T}\int_0^T i^2\,dt \]where \(T\) is the time period.

Substituting \(i=I_0\sin\omega t\),

\[ I_{\mathrm{rms}}^2=\frac{I_0^2}{T}\int_0^T\sin^2\omega t\,dt \]where the average value of \(\sin^2\omega t\) over one complete cycle is \(1/2\).
\[ I_{\mathrm{rms}}^2=\frac{I_0^2}{2} \]where \(I_0\) is the peak current.
\[ I_{\mathrm{rms}}=\frac{I_0}{\sqrt2} \]where \(I_{\mathrm{rms}}\) is the rms current.
Important: AC ammeters and voltmeters normally read rms values. Thus a household AC supply rated at \(230\,\text{V}\) has an rms voltage of approximately \(230\,\text{V}\), not \(230\sqrt2\,\text{V}\).
Example 1: Peak and RMS values

An AC current has a peak value of \(10\,\text{A}\). Find its rms value.

Show solution
\[ I_{\mathrm{rms}}=\frac{I_0}{\sqrt2} =\frac{10}{\sqrt2} =7.07\,\text{A} \]where \(I_0=10\,\text{A}\).

Answer: \(I_{\mathrm{rms}}=7.07\,\text{A}\).

19.2 AC Through a Resistor, Capacitor and Inductor

AC Through a Pure Resistor

Consider a pure resistance \(R\) connected to an AC source.

If

\[ v=V_0\sin\omega t \]where \(V_0\) is the peak voltage.

Using Ohm's law,

\[ i=\frac{v}{R} =\frac{V_0}{R}\sin\omega t \]where \(R\) is the resistance.

Therefore,

\[ i=I_0\sin\omega t \] v and i In phase
\[ V_{\mathrm{rms}}=I_{\mathrm{rms}}R \]where \(R\) is the resistance.
For a pure resistor: Voltage and current are in phase, so the phase difference is \(\phi=0^\circ\).

AC Through a Pure Inductor

When an AC voltage is applied to a pure inductor of inductance \(L\), a self-induced emf is produced that opposes the change in current.

For an inductor,

\[ v=L\frac{di}{dt} \]where \(L\) is the inductance.

Let

\[ v=V_0\sin\omega t \]where \(V_0\) is the peak applied voltage.

Then

\[ i=I_0\sin\left(\omega t-\frac{\pi}{2}\right) \]\[ X_L=\omega L=2\pi fL \]\[ I_{\mathrm{rms}}=\frac{V_{\mathrm{rms}}}{X_L} \] Voltage v Current i i lags v by 90°

AC Through a Pure Capacitor

For a capacitor of capacitance \(C\),

\[ q=Cv \]where \(q\) is charge and \(v\) is potential difference.

Since

\[ i=\frac{dq}{dt} \]\[ i=C\frac{dv}{dt} \]\[ v=V_0\sin\omega t \]where \(V_0\) is the peak voltage.

then

\[ i=I_0\sin\left(\omega t+\frac{\pi}{2}\right) \]\[ X_C=\frac{1}{\omega C}=\frac{1}{2\pi fC} \]\[ I_{\mathrm{rms}}=\frac{V_{\mathrm{rms}}}{X_C} \] Current i Voltage v i leads v by 90°

Comparison of R, L and C Circuits

Element Reactance/Resistance Phase relation Frequency dependence
Resistor \(R\) Current and voltage are in phase Independent of frequency for an ideal resistor
Inductor \(X_L=\omega L\) Current lags voltage by \(90^\circ\) Increases with frequency
Capacitor \(X_C=1/(\omega C)\) Current leads voltage by \(90^\circ\) Decreases with frequency

19.3 Phasor Diagram in RC and RL Circuits

Phasor

A phasor is a rotating vector used to represent the magnitude and phase of a sinusoidally varying quantity.

In AC circuit analysis, phasors make it convenient to represent the phase relationship between voltage and current.

RL Series Circuit

In a series RL circuit, the same current flows through both the resistor and inductor. Take current \(I\) as the reference phasor.

The resistor voltage \(V_R\) is in phase with current, while the inductor voltage \(V_L\) leads current by \(90^\circ\).

From the right-angled phasor triangle,

\[ V=\sqrt{V_R^2+V_L^2} \]\[ V=I\sqrt{R^2+X_L^2} \]\[ Z=\sqrt{R^2+X_L^2} \]\[ \tan\phi=\frac{X_L}{R} \] V_R V_C V I φ
\[ V=\sqrt{V_R^2+V_C^2} \]where \(V\) is the rms supply voltage.
\[ Z=\sqrt{R^2+X_C^2} \]\[ \tan\phi=\frac{X_C}{R} \] Common mistake: In an RL circuit, current lags voltage. In an RC circuit, current leads voltage. Remember: ELI the ICE man — in an inductor, \(E\) (voltage) leads \(I\); in a capacitor, \(I\) leads \(E\).

19.4 Series Circuit Containing Resistance, Capacitance and Inductance

A circuit containing resistance \(R\), inductance \(L\), and capacitance \(C\) connected in series is called a series RLC circuit.

Reactance

The net reactance of a series RLC circuit is the difference between inductive and capacitive reactances.

\[ X=X_L-X_C \]\[ X_L=\omega L \]\[ X_C=\frac{1}{\omega C} \]\[ X=\omega L-\frac{1}{\omega C} \]\[ Z=\sqrt{R^2+(X_L-X_C)^2} \]\[ I_{\mathrm{rms}}=\frac{V_{\mathrm{rms}}}{Z} \]\[ \tan\phi=\frac{X_L-X_C}{R} \] V_R V_L−V_C V I φ

19.5 Series Resonance and Its Applications

Condition for Resonance

In a series RLC circuit, resonance occurs when the inductive reactance becomes equal to the capacitive reactance.

\[ X_L=X_C \]\[ \omega L=\frac{1}{\omega C} \]\[ \omega_0=\frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}} \]\[ f_0=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}} \]\[ Z=R \]\[ I_{\max}=\frac{V}{R} \]\[ \phi=0^\circ \] f₀ Maximum current Frequency →

Applications of Series Resonance

  • Selection or tuning of a desired radio or television frequency.
  • Frequency-selective circuits.
  • Radio receivers and transmitters.
  • Communication circuits.
  • Electronic oscillators and filters.

  • For sinusoidal AC, \(I_{\mathrm{rms}}=I_0/\sqrt2\) and \(V_{\mathrm{rms}}=V_0/\sqrt2\).
  • In a pure resistor, current and voltage are in phase.
  • In a pure inductor, current lags voltage by \(90^\circ\).
  • Inductive reactance is \(X_L=\omega L\).
  • In a pure capacitor, current leads voltage by \(90^\circ\).
  • Capacitive reactance is \(X_C=1/\omega C\).
  • For a series RLC circuit, \(Z=\sqrt{R^2+(X_L-X_C)^2}\).
  • At resonance, \(X_L=X_C\).
  • The resonant frequency is \(f_0=1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})\).
  • At resonance, impedance is minimum and current is maximum.
  • At resonance, voltage and current are in phase and power factor is unity.
  • Q-factor measures the sharpness or selectivity of resonance.
  • For a series RLC circuit, \(Q=\omega_0L/R\).
  • Average AC power is \(P=V_{\mathrm{rms}}I_{\mathrm{rms}}\cos\phi\).
  • Power factor is \(\cos\phi\).
  • Inductive circuits have lagging power factor, while capacitive circuits have leading power factor.

    • Common Mistakes to Avoid

    • Do not confuse peak value with rms value.
    • Do not write \(X_L=1/\omega L\); that expression belongs to capacitive reactance in reciprocal form. The correct inductive reactance is \(X_L=\omega L\).
    • Do not write \(X_C=\omega C\); the correct capacitive reactance is \(X_C=1/\omega C\).
    • Remember that current lags voltage in an inductor.
    • Remember that current leads voltage in a capacitor.
    • At resonance, do not say that \(X_L\) and \(X_C\) individually become zero. They are equal in magnitude and opposite in effect, so their net reactance is zero.
    • For a series RLC circuit, use \(Z=\sqrt{R^2+(X_L-X_C)^2}\), not \(R+X_L+X_C\).
    • Power factor is \(\cos\phi\), not simply the phase angle \(\phi\).
    • At resonance in a series RLC circuit, the power factor is unity.
