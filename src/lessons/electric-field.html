20.1 Electric Field

An electric field is the region surrounding an electric charge in which another electric charge placed in that region experiences an electric force.

Definition: An electric field is a region of space around a charged body in which another charge experiences an electric force.

An electric field is produced by electric charges. To investigate the field at any point, a small positive test charge is imagined to be placed at that point.

Electric field as a physical quantity

The electric field exists whether or not a test charge is actually placed in it. The test charge is used only to detect or measure the field.

  • A positive charge produces a field directed away from it.
  • A negative charge produces a field directed towards it.
  • The electric field is a vector quantity.
  • The SI unit of electric field is \( \mathrm{N\,C^{-1}} \), which is equivalent to \( \mathrm{V\,m^{-1}} \).

20.2 Electric Field Strength

Electric field strength at a point is defined as the force experienced by a unit positive test charge placed at that point.

Electric field strength: The electric field strength at a point is the force experienced by a unit positive charge placed at that point.

If a positive test charge \(q_0\) placed at a point experiences an electric force \(F\), then the electric field strength \(E\) at that point is

\[ E=\frac{F}{q_0} \] where \(E\) is electric field strength, \(F\) is electric force and \(q_0\) is the positive test charge.

Therefore, the force acting on a charge \(q\) placed in an electric field is

\[ F=qE \] where \(q\) is the charge placed in the electric field.

For a positive charge, the force is in the direction of the electric field. For a negative charge, the force is opposite to the direction of the electric field.

SI unit and dimensions

\[ [E]=\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}} \]
\[ 1\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}=1\,\mathrm{V\,m^{-1}} \]

Force on a charge in a uniform electric field

In a uniform electric field, the field strength has the same magnitude and direction at every point. A charge \(q\) placed in the field experiences a force

\[ F=qE \]

The magnitude of the force is

\[ |F|=|q|E \]

If the charge has mass \(m\), its acceleration is

\[ a=\frac{F}{m}=\frac{qE}{m} \]

Example 1: Force on a charge

A charge of \(2\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm C\) is placed in a uniform electric field of strength \(5\times10^4\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}\). Find the force acting on it.

Show solution

Given:

\(q=2\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm C\)

\(E=5\times10^4\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}\)

Using

\[ F=qE \]

Therefore,

\[ F=(2\times10^{-6})(5\times10^4) \] $$ F=0.10\,\mathrm N $$

Answer: \(F=0.10\,\mathrm N\), in the direction of the electric field because the charge is positive.

20.3 Force on Charges in a Uniform Electric Field

When a charged particle is placed in a uniform electric field, it experiences a constant electric force if its charge remains constant.

\[ F=qE \]

Using Newton's second law,

\[ ma=qE \] $$ a=\frac{qE}{m} $$

Motion of a positive charge

A positive charge accelerates in the direction of the electric field.

Motion of a negative charge

A negative charge accelerates opposite to the direction of the electric field.

Important result: In a uniform electric field, a charged particle of charge \(q\) and mass \(m\) has constant acceleration \(a=qE/m\), provided no other force is considered.

20.4 Electric Field Strength of a Point Charge

Consider a point charge \(Q\) placed in free space or air. Let a small positive test charge \(q_0\) be placed at a distance \(r\) from \(Q\).

According to Coulomb's law, the force acting on the test charge is

\[ F=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{Qq_0}{r^2} \] where \(\varepsilon_0\) is the permittivity of free space.

Since

\[ E=\frac{F}{q_0} \]

we obtain

\[ E=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{Q}{r^2} \]

Since

\[ \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}=9\times10^9\,\mathrm{N\,m^2\,C^{-2}} \]

therefore,

\[ E=\frac{9\times10^9Q}{r^2} \]

Electric field due to a point charge:

\[ \boxed{E=\frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2}} \]

The magnitude of the field follows the inverse-square law:

\[ E\propto\frac{1}{r^2} \]

Direction of electric field

  • For \(Q>0\), the field is directed radially outward.
  • For \(Q<0\), the field is directed radially inward.

Example 2: Electric field due to a point charge

Find the electric field strength at a point \(0.20\,\mathrm m\) from a point charge of \(4\,\mu\mathrm C\) in air.

Show solution

Given:

\(Q=4\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm C\)

\(r=0.20\,\mathrm m\)

\[ E=\frac{9\times10^9Q}{r^2} \] $$ E=\frac{9\times10^9(4\times10^{-6})}{(0.20)^2} $$ $$ E=9.0\times10^5\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}} $$

Answer: \(E=9.0\times10^5\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}\), directed away from the positive charge.

20.5 Variation of Electric Field Strength with Distance

For a point charge \(Q\),

\[ E=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{Q}{r^2} \]

For a fixed charge \(Q\),

\[ E\propto\frac{1}{r^2} \]

Therefore, if the distance is doubled, the field becomes one-fourth of its original value. If the distance is tripled, the field becomes one-ninth.

Distance Electric field strength
\(r\) \(E\)
\(2r\) \(E/4\)
\(3r\) \(E/9\)
\(4r\) \(E/16\)

Exam tip: Do not draw \(E\) versus \(r\) as a straight line. The correct graph is a decreasing inverse-square curve.

20.6 Electric Field Lines

An electric field can be represented graphically by imaginary lines called electric field lines or lines of force.

Electric field line: An electric field line is an imaginary curve drawn in an electric field such that the tangent to the curve at any point gives the direction of the electric field at that point.

Properties of electric field lines

  1. Electric field lines originate from positive charges and terminate on negative charges or at infinity.
  2. The tangent to a field line at any point gives the direction of the electric field at that point.
  3. The field lines never intersect each other.
  4. The closer the field lines, the stronger the electric field.
  5. The farther apart the field lines, the weaker the electric field.
  6. Field lines are perpendicular to the surface of a conductor in electrostatic equilibrium.
  7. There are no electric field lines inside a conductor in electrostatic equilibrium.
  8. Electric field lines do not form closed loops.

Important: Field lines are a graphical representation. They are not physical strings or material objects.

20.7 Motion of Charged Particles in a Uniform Electric Field

Consider a particle of charge \(q\) and mass \(m\) placed in a uniform electric field \(E\).

The electric force is

\[ F=qE \]

Hence, its acceleration is

\[ a=\frac{qE}{m} \]

Particle initially at rest

If a charged particle is initially at rest in a uniform electric field, it moves with constant acceleration.

Using the equations of uniformly accelerated motion,

\[ v=u+at \]

For \(u=0\),

\[ v=\frac{qE}{m}t \]

and

\[ s=ut+\frac12at^2 \] $$ s=\frac{qE}{2m}t^2 $$

Charged particle entering perpendicular to the electric field

If a charged particle enters a uniform electric field with an initial velocity perpendicular to the field, its motion is analogous to projectile motion.

There is no force in the direction perpendicular to the electric field, so the velocity component in that direction remains constant. The particle accelerates in the direction of the electric force.

Example 3: Acceleration of an electron

An electron is placed in a uniform electric field of strength \(2\times10^4\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}\). Find the magnitude of its acceleration. Take \(e=1.6\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm C\) and \(m_e=9.1\times10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}\).

Show solution
\[ a=\frac{|q|E}{m} \] $$ a=\frac{(1.6\times10^{-19})(2\times10^4)} {9.1\times10^{-31}} $$ $$ a\approx3.52\times10^{15}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}} $$

Answer: \(a\approx3.52\times10^{15}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\).

Since an electron is negatively charged, its acceleration is opposite to the direction of the electric field.

20.8 Electric Flux

Electric flux is a measure of the electric field passing through a surface.

Electric flux: Electric flux through a surface is a measure of the number of electric field lines passing normally through the surface.

For a uniform electric field \(E\) passing through a plane surface of area \(A\), let the angle between the electric field and the normal to the surface be \(\theta\).

\[ \boxed{\Phi_E=EA\cos\theta} \] where \(\Phi_E\) is electric flux, \(E\) is electric field strength, \(A\) is area and \(\theta\) is the angle between the field and the normal to the surface.

Special cases

Condition Angle Flux
Field perpendicular to surface \(\theta=0^\circ\) \(\Phi_E=EA\)
Field parallel to surface \(\theta=90^\circ\) \(\Phi_E=0\)
Field makes an angle \(\theta\) with normal \(\theta\) \(\Phi_E=EA\cos\theta\)

Common examination error: In \( \Phi_E=EA\cos\theta \), \(\theta\) is measured between the electric field and the normal to the surface, not between the electric field and the surface itself.

20.9 Gauss's Law

Gauss's law relates the total electric flux through a closed surface to the total charge enclosed by that surface.

Gauss's law: The total electric flux through any closed surface is equal to \(1/\varepsilon_0\) times the net charge enclosed by the surface.

\[ \boxed{\oint \vec E\cdot d\vec A=\frac{Q_{\mathrm{enc}}}{\varepsilon_0}} \]

For a closed surface through which the electric field has the same magnitude everywhere and is perpendicular to the surface,

\[ \Phi_E=EA \] $$ EA=\frac{Q_{\mathrm{enc}}}{\varepsilon_0} $$

Gaussian surface

A Gaussian surface is an imaginary closed surface chosen to apply Gauss's law conveniently. The shape of the Gaussian surface should match the symmetry of the charge distribution.

  • Point or spherical charge distribution → spherical Gaussian surface.
  • Long straight line charge → cylindrical Gaussian surface.
  • Infinite plane charge → cylindrical pillbox surface.

Electric field due to a charged spherical conductor

Consider an isolated spherical conducting shell of radius \(R\) carrying a total charge \(Q\).

1. Field outside the charged sphere \((r>R)\)

Choose a spherical Gaussian surface of radius \(r\), where \(r>R\).

By symmetry, the electric field has the same magnitude \(E\) at every point of the Gaussian surface and is perpendicular to the surface.

The surface area is

\[ A=4\pi r^2 \]

Using Gauss's law,

\[ EA=\frac{Q}{\varepsilon_0} \] $$ E(4\pi r^2)=\frac{Q}{\varepsilon_0} $$ $$ \boxed{E=\frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2}} $$

Thus, outside a uniformly charged spherical conductor, the electric field is the same as that produced by a point charge \(Q\) placed at its centre.

2. Field at the surface \((r=R)\)

\[ \boxed{E=\frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R^2}} \]

3. Field inside the charged conductor \((r

In electrostatic equilibrium, all excess charge resides on the outer surface of the conductor. Therefore, a Gaussian surface inside the conductor encloses no charge.

\[ Q_{\mathrm{enc}}=0 \] $$ \boxed{E=0} $$

Electric field due to a long straight line charge

Consider a very long straight line charge having uniform linear charge density \(\lambda\), measured in \(\mathrm{C\,m^{-1}}\).

Choose a cylindrical Gaussian surface of radius \(r\) and length \(L\), coaxial with the charged line.

The curved surface area is

\[ A=2\pi rL \]

The electric field is radial and perpendicular to the curved surface. There is no flux through the two flat ends because the field is parallel to those surfaces.

The charge enclosed by the Gaussian cylinder is

\[ Q_{\mathrm{enc}}=\lambda L \]

Using Gauss's law,

\[ E(2\pi rL)=\frac{\lambda L}{\varepsilon_0} \] $$ \boxed{E=\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0r}} $$

Electric field due to an infinitely long straight line charge:

\[ \boxed{E=\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0r}} \]

Hence,

\[ E\propto\frac{1}{r} \]

Example 4: Field due to a line charge

A very long straight line has a uniform charge density \(2\times10^{-8}\,\mathrm{C\,m^{-1}}\). Find the electric field at a distance \(0.10\,\mathrm m\) from it. Take \(\varepsilon_0=8.85\times10^{-12}\,\mathrm{C^2N^{-1}m^{-2}}\).

Show solution
\[ E=\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0r} \] $$ E= \frac{2\times10^{-8}} {2\pi(8.85\times10^{-12})(0.10)} $$ $$ E\approx3.60\times10^3\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}} $$

Answer: \(E\approx3.60\times10^3\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}\).

20.10 Uniform Electric Field Between Charged Parallel Plates

Two large parallel conducting plates carrying equal and opposite charges produce an approximately uniform electric field in the region between them, provided the separation is small compared with the dimensions of the plates.

Uniform electric field: A uniform electric field is one in which the magnitude and direction of the electric field are the same at every point in the region.

The electric field between two oppositely charged parallel plates is approximately uniform except near the edges, where fringing occurs.

Electric field between parallel plates

If the surface charge density on each plate is \(\sigma\), the magnitude of the electric field between two oppositely charged large parallel plates is

\[ \boxed{E=\frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0}} \]

If a potential difference \(V\) is maintained between two parallel plates separated by distance \(d\), the uniform field between them is

\[ \boxed{E=\frac{V}{d}} \]

This relation is useful when the potential difference and plate separation are known.

Direction of the electric field

The electric field between parallel plates is directed from the positive plate towards the negative plate.

  • Field lines are straight.
  • Field lines are parallel.
  • Field lines are equally spaced in the central region.
  • Field lines are perpendicular to the conducting plates.
  • The field is approximately uniform away from the edges.

Force on a charge between parallel plates

A charge \(q\) placed between the plates experiences a force

\[ F=qE \]

Using \(E=V/d\),

\[ \boxed{F=\frac{qV}{d}} \]

For a particle of mass \(m\), its acceleration is

\[ \boxed{a=\frac{qV}{md}} \]

Example 5: Electric field between parallel plates

Two parallel plates are maintained at a potential difference of \(600\,\mathrm V\) and are separated by \(3\,\mathrm{cm}\). Find the electric field between them.

Show solution

Given:

\(V=600\,\mathrm V\)

\(d=3\,\mathrm{cm}=0.03\,\mathrm m\)

\[ E=\frac{V}{d} \] $$ E=\frac{600}{0.03} $$ $$ \boxed{E=2.0\times10^4\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}} $$

Answer: \(E=2.0\times10^4\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}\), directed from the positive plate to the negative plate.

Fringing of electric field

Near the edges of finite parallel plates, the electric field lines bend outward. This effect is called fringing.

For ideal calculations involving parallel plates, the field is generally considered uniform between the plates and edge effects are neglected.

Important Formulae

  • Electric field strength: \(E=F/q\)
  • Force on a charge: \(F=qE\)
  • Acceleration of a charged particle: \(a=qE/m\)
  • Point-charge field: \(E=Q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2)\)
  • Point-charge field variation: \(E\propto1/r^2\)
  • Electric flux: \(\Phi_E=EA\cos\theta\)
  • Gauss's law: \(\oint\vec E\cdot d\vec A=Q_{\mathrm{enc}}/\varepsilon_0\)
  • Field outside a charged sphere: \(E=Q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2)\)
  • Field inside a charged conducting sphere: \(E=0\)
  • Field due to a long straight line charge: \(E=\lambda/(2\pi\varepsilon_0r)\)
  • Field between parallel plates: \(E=\sigma/\varepsilon_0\)
  • Field between plates using potential difference: \(E=V/d\)
  • Force between parallel plates: \(F=qV/d\)

Common Examination Mistakes

  • Writing \(E=Fq\) instead of \(E=F/q\).
  • Forgetting that the electric field direction is defined as the force direction on a positive test charge.
  • Using the direction of force on a negative charge as the direction of the electric field.
  • Using \(E=Q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0r)\) instead of the correct inverse-square relation \(E=Q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2)\).
  • Using the wrong angle in the electric-flux formula. The angle is measured between the electric field and the normal to the surface.
  • Forgetting that Gauss's law applies to a closed surface.
  • Using a spherical Gaussian surface for a line charge. A cylindrical Gaussian surface is appropriate for a long straight line charge.
  • For a charged conducting sphere, writing a non-zero electric field inside the conductor in electrostatic equilibrium.
  • Drawing non-parallel or unequally spaced field lines when representing a uniform electric field.
  • Drawing electric field lines crossing each other. Two field lines cannot intersect because the electric field has only one definite direction at a point.

Key Points for Examination

  • Electric field is the region around a charge in which another charge experiences electric force.
  • Electric field strength is force per unit positive test charge: \(E=F/q\).
  • The force on a charge in an electric field is \(F=qE\).
  • The field of a point charge follows the inverse-square law: \(E\propto1/r^2\).
  • Field lines originate from positive charges and terminate on negative charges or infinity.
  • Field lines never intersect one another.
  • Closer field lines indicate a stronger electric field.
  • A positive charge moves in the direction of the electric field, while a negative charge moves opposite to it.
  • Electric flux through a plane surface is \(\Phi_E=EA\cos\theta\).
  • In the flux formula, \(\theta\) is the angle between the electric field and the normal to the surface.
  • Gauss's law states that the net electric flux through a closed surface equals the enclosed charge divided by \(\varepsilon_0\).
  • For a charged spherical conductor, \(E=0\) inside the conductor.
  • Outside a charged spherical conductor, the field is the same as that of a point charge at its centre.
  • For a long straight line charge, \(E=\lambda/(2\pi\varepsilon_0r)\).
  • Between large oppositely charged parallel plates, the electric field is approximately uniform.
  • For parallel plates, \(E=V/d\), and the field is directed from the positive plate to the negative plate.
