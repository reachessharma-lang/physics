10.1 Theories of Propagation of Light

Light is a form of energy that produces the sensation of vision. Since ancient times, scientists have proposed different theories to explain the nature and propagation of light. The major theories developed from the corpuscular theory of Newton to the electromagnetic theory of Maxwell.

Development of Theories of Light

Theory Scientist Main idea Important limitation/contribution
Corpuscular theory Isaac Newton Light consists of very small particles called corpuscles emitted by luminous bodies. Could explain rectilinear propagation and reflection reasonably well, but could not adequately explain interference and diffraction.
Wave theory Christiaan Huygens Light propagates as a wave through a medium. Successfully explained reflection and refraction and provided the basis for understanding interference and diffraction.
Interference and diffraction theory Thomas Young and Augustin Fresnel Light shows interference and diffraction, supporting its wave nature. Strong experimental evidence for the wave nature of light.
Electromagnetic theory James Clerk Maxwell Light is an electromagnetic wave consisting of mutually perpendicular electric and magnetic fields. Explained light as an electromagnetic phenomenon and predicted electromagnetic waves.

10.1.1 Newton's Corpuscular Theory

Newton proposed the corpuscular theory of light in the seventeenth century. According to this theory, luminous bodies emit extremely small particles called corpuscles. These corpuscles travel through space with very high speed and produce the sensation of vision when they enter the eye.

Main assumptions of Newton's corpuscular theory:

  • Light consists of tiny material particles called corpuscles.
  • Corpuscles are emitted by luminous objects.
  • The corpuscles travel in straight lines in a homogeneous medium.
  • Reflection occurs when corpuscles are repelled by the reflecting surface.
  • Refraction occurs because the speed of corpuscles changes when they enter another medium.

Successes of Corpuscular Theory

  • It explained the rectilinear propagation of light.
  • It provided a simple explanation of reflection.
  • It could qualitatively explain refraction.

Limitations of Corpuscular Theory

  • It could not satisfactorily explain interference of light.
  • It could not explain diffraction.
  • It could not satisfactorily explain polarization.
  • The prediction that light should travel faster in a denser medium is contrary to experimental observation.

10.1.2 Huygens' Wave Theory of Light

Christiaan Huygens proposed the wave theory of light in 1678. According to Huygens, light is a wave motion that propagates through space. He introduced the important concepts of wavefronts and secondary wavelets to describe the propagation of light.

Huygens' wave theory: Light propagates in the form of waves, and every point on a wavefront acts as a source of secondary spherical wavelets. The new wavefront at a later instant is the common tangent surface to these secondary wavelets.

Wavefront

A wavefront is an imaginary surface joining all the points of a wave that are in the same phase of vibration.

In a two-dimensional diagram, a wavefront is represented by a line. The direction of propagation of the wave is perpendicular to the wavefront.

Important: Wavefronts represent surfaces of equal phase, whereas rays represent the direction of propagation of the wave. Therefore, rays are normal to wavefronts.

Types of Wavefronts

1. Spherical Wavefront

When light is emitted from a point source, the wavefronts are spherical surfaces centred on the source.

2. Cylindrical Wavefront

When light originates from a long narrow source, the wavefronts may be cylindrical.

3. Plane Wavefront

At a sufficiently large distance from a point source, a small portion of a spherical wavefront can be considered approximately plane. A plane wavefront is represented by a set of parallel straight lines in a two-dimensional diagram.

Huygens' Principle

According to Huygens' principle, every point on a given wavefront acts as a source of secondary wavelets that spread in the forward direction with the same speed as the wave in that medium. The new wavefront is the forward envelope or common tangent to these secondary wavelets.

Construction of a New Wavefront

Consider a plane wavefront \(AB\) propagating through a homogeneous medium. After a time interval \(t\), every point on \(AB\) produces a secondary wavelet of radius \(vt\), where \(v\) is the speed of light in the medium. The common tangent to these wavelets gives the new wavefront \(A'B'\).

\[ r=vt \]where \(r\) is the radius of each secondary wavelet, \(v\) is the speed of light in the medium, and \(t\) is the time interval.

Important Consequences of Huygens' Principle

  • Every point on a wavefront behaves as a source of secondary wavelets.
  • Secondary wavelets propagate with the wave speed of the medium.
  • The forward envelope of the secondary wavelets forms the new wavefront.
  • The direction of propagation is normal to the wavefront.
  • The principle can be used to derive the laws of reflection and refraction.

10.2 Huygens' Principle Applied to Reflection and Refraction

Reflection of Light Using Huygens' Principle

Consider a plane wavefront \(AB\) incident obliquely on a plane reflecting surface.

Let \(A\) touch the reflecting surface first. During the time taken by the disturbance at \(B\) to reach the surface, the secondary wavelet originating from \(A\) travels a distance \(AD=vt\). The tangent drawn to the secondary wavelet gives the reflected wavefront.

Since rays are normal to wavefronts, the incident and reflected rays are perpendicular to their respective wavefronts.

Derivation of the Law of Reflection

Let the incident wavefront make an angle \(i\) with the normal at the reflecting surface. Let \(BC\) be the distance travelled by the wave at \(B\) while the secondary wavelet from \(A\) travels from \(A\) to the reflecting surface.

If the speed of light in the medium is \(v\), then for the same time interval \(t\):

\[ BC=AD=vt \]

From the geometry of Huygens' construction:

\[ \sin i=\sin r \]

Hence,

\[ \boxed{i=r} \]where \(i\) is the angle of incidence and \(r\) is the angle of reflection, both measured from the normal.

Law of reflection:

  1. The incident ray, reflected ray and normal at the point of incidence all lie in the same plane.
  2. The angle of incidence is equal to the angle of reflection.

Refraction of Light Using Huygens' Principle

When a wavefront passes obliquely from one transparent medium into another, its speed changes. Consequently, the direction of propagation of the wave changes. This phenomenon is called refraction.

Let a plane wavefront travel from medium 1 into medium 2. Let the speeds of light in the two media be \(v_1\) and \(v_2\), respectively.

If \(v_2<v_1\), the light bends towards the normal. If \(v_2>v_1\), the light bends away from the normal.

Derivation of Snell's Law Using Huygens' Principle

Suppose a plane wavefront is incident obliquely on the boundary between two media. Let \(v_1\) and \(v_2\) be the speeds of light in the first and second media, respectively.

During the same time interval \(t\), the point of the wavefront travels a distance \(v_1t\) in the first medium, while the secondary wavelet in the second medium travels a distance \(v_2t\).

\[ BC=v_1t \]
\[ AD=v_2t \]

From the geometry of Huygens' construction:

\[ \sin i=\frac{BC}{AC} \]
\[ \sin r=\frac{AD}{AC} \]

Therefore,

\[ \frac{\sin i}{\sin r} = \frac{BC}{AD} = \frac{v_1t}{v_2t} \]
\[ \boxed{\frac{\sin i}{\sin r}=\frac{v_1}{v_2}} \]

The refractive index of medium 2 with respect to medium 1 is

\[ \mu_{21}=\frac{v_1}{v_2} \]

Hence,

\[ \boxed{\frac{\sin i}{\sin r}=\mu_{21}} \]

Thus, Huygens' principle leads directly to Snell's law of refraction:

\[ \boxed{n_1\sin i=n_2\sin r} \]where \(n_1\) and \(n_2\) are the absolute refractive indices of the first and second media.

Snell's law: For a given pair of transparent media, the ratio of the sine of the angle of incidence to the sine of the angle of refraction is constant.

Why Does Light Bend During Refraction?

The frequency of light remains unchanged when light passes from one medium to another, but its speed and wavelength change.

\[ v=f\lambda \]

Since the frequency \(f\) remains constant:

\[ \frac{v_1}{v_2}=\frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_2} \]

Therefore, when light enters a denser optical medium, its speed decreases and its wavelength decreases. When it enters a rarer optical medium, its speed increases and its wavelength increases.

Remember: During refraction, the frequency of light does not change. The speed and wavelength change.

Worked Example 1: Refractive Index from Angles

Question: A ray of light passes from air into glass with an angle of incidence of \(45^\circ\) and an angle of refraction of \(28^\circ\). Find the refractive index of glass with respect to air.

Show solution ```

Using Snell's law,

\[ \mu=\frac{\sin i}{\sin r} \]

Substituting \(i=45^\circ\) and \(r=28^\circ\),

\[ \mu=\frac{\sin45^\circ}{\sin28^\circ} \]
\[ \mu\approx\frac{0.7071}{0.4695}\approx1.51 \]

Answer: The refractive index of glass is approximately \(1.51\).

Worked Example 2: Speed of Light in a Medium

Question: The refractive index of a transparent material is \(1.5\). Find the speed of light in the material. Take the speed of light in vacuum as \(3\times10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).

Show solution ```

We know that

\[ n=\frac{c}{v} \]

Therefore,

\[ v=\frac{c}{n} \]
\[ v=\frac{3\times10^8}{1.5} =2\times10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} \]

Answer: The speed of light in the material is \(2\times10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).

```

Worked Example 3: Refraction of a Wave

Question: The speed of light in medium A is \(3\times10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), while its speed in medium B is \(2\times10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). If the angle of incidence is \(30^\circ\), find the angle of refraction.

Show solution ```

Using Huygens' form of Snell's law,

\[ \frac{\sin i}{\sin r}=\frac{v_1}{v_2} \]

Therefore,

\[ \sin r=\sin i\frac{v_2}{v_1} \]
\[ \sin r = \sin30^\circ \frac{2\times10^8}{3\times10^8} \]
\[ \sin r=\frac12\times\frac23=\frac13 \]
\[ r=\sin^{-1}\left(\frac13\right)\approx19.5^\circ \]

Answer: The angle of refraction is approximately \(19.5^\circ\).

```

Applications of Huygens' Principle

  • It explains the propagation of light waves.
  • It provides a geometrical construction of wavefronts.
  • It explains reflection of light.
  • It explains refraction of light.
  • It provides the basis for understanding interference and diffraction.
  • It establishes the relation between wavefronts and rays.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Do not confuse a wavefront with a ray. A ray is normal to the wavefront.
  • Angles of incidence and refraction are measured from the normal, not from the surface.
  • In refraction, frequency remains unchanged.
  • Speed and wavelength change when light enters a different medium.
  • For light entering a denser medium, \(v\) and \(\lambda\) decrease.
  • For light entering a rarer medium, \(v\) and \(\lambda\) increase.
  • In Snell's law, make sure the refractive indices and angles correspond to the same media.
  • In Huygens' construction, the new wavefront is the forward envelope of the secondary wavelets.
