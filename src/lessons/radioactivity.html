24.1 Meaning of Radioactivity

Radioactivity is an important nuclear phenomenon in which an unstable atomic nucleus spontaneously disintegrates and emits energetic particles or electromagnetic radiation. In 1896, Henri Becquerel discovered the spontaneous emission of invisible radiation from uranium salts. The phenomenon was later named radioactivity by Marie Curie.

Radioactivity is the spontaneous and random disintegration of an unstable atomic nucleus accompanied by the emission of energetic particles or electromagnetic radiation.

Radioactivity is a nuclear phenomenon because it originates from the nucleus and is determined by the nuclear composition. The rate of radioactive decay is essentially independent of ordinary physical conditions such as temperature, pressure, chemical combination, and physical state.

Natural Radioactivity

The spontaneous disintegration of naturally occurring unstable nuclei without any external cause is called natural radioactivity.

Examples of naturally radioactive elements include uranium, thorium, radium, and polonium. Many naturally occurring radioactive nuclei are heavy nuclei with high atomic numbers.

Artificial or Induced Radioactivity

The radioactivity produced artificially by bombarding a stable nucleus with suitable particles such as alpha particles, neutrons, or protons is called artificial or induced radioactivity.

Artificial radioactivity was discovered by Irène Joliot-Curie and Frédéric Joliot in 1934.

For example, when aluminium-27 is bombarded with alpha particles, radioactive phosphorus-30 is produced:

\[ {}^{27}_{13}\mathrm{Al}+{}^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He}\rightarrow{}^{30}_{15}\mathrm{P}+{}^{1}_{0}\mathrm{n} \] where the produced \(^{30}_{15}\mathrm{P}\) is radioactive.

The radioactive phosphorus subsequently undergoes beta-plus decay:

\[ {}^{30}_{15}\mathrm{P}\rightarrow{}^{30}_{14}\mathrm{Si}+e^+ + \nu_e \] where \(e^+\) is a positron and \(\nu_e\) is an electron neutrino.

Exam tip: Remember the distinction: natural radioactivity occurs spontaneously in naturally occurring unstable nuclei, whereas artificial radioactivity is produced by bombarding a nucleus with suitable particles.

24.2 Types of Radiations and Their Properties

When radiation emitted by a radioactive substance is passed through a strong electric or magnetic field, it can be separated into three principal types: alpha (\(\alpha\)) particles, beta (\(\beta\)) particles, and gamma (\(\gamma\)) rays.

Alpha (\(\alpha\)) Particles

  • Alpha particles are doubly ionized helium nuclei, represented by \(^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He}^{2+}\).
  • They have charge \(+2e\), approximately \(+3.2\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}\).
  • Their mass is approximately \(4\,\mathrm{u}\), or \(6.64\times10^{-27}\,\mathrm{kg}\).
  • Their speed is of the order of \(10^7\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).
  • They possess very high ionizing power.
  • They possess very low penetrating power and can be stopped by a sheet of paper or a few centimetres of air.
  • They are deflected by electric and magnetic fields.
  • Because of their large mass and charge, their deflection is much smaller than that of beta particles for comparable fields.

Beta (\(\beta\)) Particles

  • Beta particles are high-speed electrons (\(\beta^-\)) or positrons (\(\beta^+\)) emitted from the nucleus.
  • A beta-minus particle has charge \(-e\), while a beta-plus particle has charge \(+e\).
  • The mass of an electron or positron is approximately \(1/1836\) of the mass of a proton.
  • Their speed can be very high, approaching the speed of light.
  • They have moderate ionizing power.
  • Their penetrating power is greater than that of alpha particles but less than that of gamma rays.
  • They can pass through paper but are significantly attenuated by a few millimetres of aluminium or other suitable shielding materials.
  • They are strongly deflected by electric and magnetic fields because of their small mass.
  • \(\beta^-\) and \(\beta^+\) are deflected in opposite directions in the same field.

Gamma (\(\gamma\)) Rays

  • Gamma rays are high-energy electromagnetic radiation consisting of photons.
  • They have no electric charge and no rest mass.
  • They travel through vacuum at the speed of light, \(c=3\times10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).
  • They have the lowest ionizing power among the three.
  • They have the highest penetrating power.
  • They require dense and sufficiently thick shielding such as lead or concrete for effective attenuation.
  • They are not deflected by electric or magnetic fields.

Common mistake: Do not say that gamma rays are particles with zero charge. Gamma rays are electromagnetic photons; they have zero charge and zero rest mass.

24.3 Radioactive Disintegration Law

Rutherford-Soddy radioactive disintegration law: The rate of disintegration of a radioactive substance at any instant is directly proportional to the number of undecayed radioactive nuclei present at that instant.

Mathematical Derivation of Radioactive Decay Law

Let \(N\) be the number of undecayed nuclei present at time \(t\).

According to the radioactive disintegration law,

\[ -\frac{dN}{dt}\propto N \] where the negative sign indicates that the number of undecayed nuclei decreases with time.

Introducing the proportionality constant \(\lambda\), called the decay constant,

\[ -\frac{dN}{dt}=\lambda N \] \(\lambda\) is the decay constant.

Therefore,

\[ \frac{dN}{N}=-\lambda\,dt \] This is the differential form of the radioactive decay law.

Integrating from \(N_0\) at \(t=0\) to \(N\) at time \(t\),

\[ \int_{N_0}^{N}\frac{dN}{N}=-\lambda\int_0^t dt \] \(N_0\) is the initial number of radioactive nuclei.
\[ \ln N-\ln N_0=-\lambda t \]
\[ \ln\left(\frac{N}{N_0}\right)=-\lambda t \]

Taking the exponential of both sides,

\[ \frac{N}{N_0}=e^{-\lambda t} \]

Hence, the radioactive decay equation is

\[ \boxed{N=N_0e^{-\lambda t}} \] where \(N\) is the number of undecayed nuclei after time \(t\), \(N_0\) is the initial number of nuclei, and \(\lambda\) is the decay constant.

Decay in Terms of Activity

The activity \(A\) of a radioactive sample is the number of disintegrations occurring per unit time.

\[ A=-\frac{dN}{dt} \] \(A\) is the activity of the radioactive sample.

Using the radioactive decay law,

\[ A=\lambda N \] Activity is proportional to the number of undecayed nuclei.

Since \(N=N_0e^{-\lambda t}\),

\[ A=A_0e^{-\lambda t} \] \(A_0=\lambda N_0\) is the initial activity.

Exponential Nature of Radioactive Decay

Radioactive decay is exponential rather than linear. Equal intervals of time remove equal fractions of the remaining nuclei, not equal numbers of nuclei.

24.4 Half-life, Decay Constant and Mean Life

Decay Constant \(\lambda\)

The decay constant \(\lambda\) is the fractional rate of radioactive disintegration per unit time. It represents the probability per unit time that a particular radioactive nucleus will decay.

\[ \boxed{\lambda=-\frac{1}{N}\frac{dN}{dt}} \] The SI unit of \(\lambda\) is \(\mathrm{s^{-1}}\).

The decay constant is characteristic of a particular radioactive nuclide. A larger value of \(\lambda\) indicates a faster decay.

Half-life \(T_{1/2}\)

The half-life of a radioactive substance is the time required for the number of undecayed radioactive nuclei to become half of its initial value.

From the decay equation,

\[ N=N_0e^{-\lambda t} \]

At \(t=T_{1/2}\),

\[ N=\frac{N_0}{2} \]

Therefore,

\[ \frac{N_0}{2}=N_0e^{-\lambda T_{1/2}} \]
\[ \frac{1}{2}=e^{-\lambda T_{1/2}} \]

Taking natural logarithm,

\[ \ln\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)=-\lambda T_{1/2} \]

Since \(\ln 2=0.693\),

\[ \boxed{T_{1/2}=\frac{\ln2}{\lambda}=\frac{0.693}{\lambda}} \] \(T_{1/2}\) is the half-life and \(\lambda\) is the decay constant.

Mean Life \(\tau\)

The mean life of a radioactive substance is the average lifetime of all the radioactive nuclei present initially.

Suppose \(dN\) nuclei decay between \(t\) and \(t+dt\). The number of nuclei decaying is

\[ -dN=\lambda N\,dt \]

Since \(N=N_0e^{-\lambda t}\),

\[ -dN=\lambda N_0e^{-\lambda t}dt \]

The total lifetime of these nuclei is \(t(-dN)\). Therefore, the total lifetime of all the nuclei is

\[ \int_0^\infty t\lambda N_0e^{-\lambda t}dt \]

Using

\[ \int_0^\infty te^{-\lambda t}dt=\frac{1}{\lambda^2} \]

we obtain

\[ \text{Total lifetime}=\frac{N_0}{\lambda} \]

Hence, mean life is

\[ \boxed{\tau=\frac{1}{\lambda}} \] \(\tau\) is the mean life of the radioactive nucleus.

Relation Between Half-life and Mean Life

Since

\[ T_{1/2}=\frac{0.693}{\lambda} \quad\text{and}\quad \tau=\frac{1}{\lambda} \]

we get

\[ \boxed{\tau=\frac{T_{1/2}}{0.693}\approx1.44T_{1/2}} \] Thus mean life is approximately \(1.44\) times the half-life.

Useful Radioactive Decay Relations

\[ \boxed{N=N_0e^{-\lambda t}} \] Number of undecayed nuclei after time \(t\).
\[ \boxed{A=A_0e^{-\lambda t}} \] Activity after time \(t\).
\[ \boxed{N=N_0\left(\frac12\right)^{t/T_{1/2}}} \] Number remaining after \(t\) expressed in terms of half-life.
\[ \boxed{A=A_0\left(\frac12\right)^{t/T_{1/2}}} \] Activity after \(t\) expressed in terms of half-life.
\[ \boxed{\lambda=\frac{0.693}{T_{1/2}}=\frac{1}{\tau}} \] Relations among decay constant, half-life and mean life.

Important: Half-life does not depend on the initial amount of the radioactive substance. Every sample of a particular radioactive nuclide has the same half-life under the same decay process.

24.5 Working of Geiger-Muller Tube

The Geiger-Muller (GM) tube is an instrument used to detect and count ionizing radiation such as alpha particles, beta particles and suitable gamma radiation.

Principle: A GM tube works on the principle of gas ionization followed by Townsend avalanche multiplication. An incident ionizing radiation produces ion pairs in the gas, and the strong electric field produces a large avalanche of secondary ionization, resulting in an electrical pulse.

Construction

  • A GM tube consists of a hollow metal cylinder that acts as the cathode.
  • A thin central wire, commonly tungsten, acts as the anode.
  • The anode and cathode are electrically insulated from each other.
  • The tube contains a low-pressure inert gas such as argon.
  • A small quantity of quenching gas, such as alcohol vapour or a suitable halogen, is added.
  • A thin mica window may be provided so that radiation can enter the tube, particularly for detecting alpha and beta radiation.
  • A high potential difference, typically of several hundred volts, is applied between the anode and cathode.
  • The output is connected to a counting or measuring circuit.

Working

  1. When ionizing radiation enters the tube, it collides with gas atoms and produces electron-ion pairs.
  2. The free electrons are accelerated towards the central anode by the strong electric field.
  3. The electrons gain sufficient kinetic energy to ionize additional gas atoms.
  4. This produces a chain reaction called a Townsend avalanche.
  5. A large number of electrons rapidly reach the anode, producing a short electrical pulse.
  6. The positive ions move towards the cathode.
  7. The electrical pulse is amplified and counted by an external counter or scaler.
  8. Each detected radiation event produces a measurable pulse, allowing the radiation to be counted.

Role of Quenching Gas

During the avalanche, excited gas atoms can emit ultraviolet photons. These photons may produce further unwanted ionization or secondary electrons from the cathode, causing a continuous discharge.

The quenching gas absorbs the ultraviolet radiation and helps neutralize excited or positive gas species without producing an unwanted continuing discharge.

Exam sequence: Radiation enters → gas ionization → electrons accelerated → secondary ionization → Townsend avalanche → electrical pulse → pulse counted.

24.6 Radiocarbon Dating

Radiocarbon dating is a radiometric method used to estimate the age of formerly living carbon-containing materials. It is particularly useful for archaeological and geological samples containing organic carbon and is generally useful over a timescale of up to about \(50,000\) years.

Production of Carbon-14

Cosmic rays produce neutrons in the upper atmosphere. These neutrons interact with atmospheric nitrogen to produce radioactive carbon-14:

\[ {}^{14}_{7}\mathrm{N}+{}^{1}_{0}\mathrm{n}\rightarrow{}^{14}_{6}\mathrm{C}+{}^{1}_{1}\mathrm{H} \] Carbon-14 is continuously produced in the atmosphere.

Carbon-14 combines with oxygen to form radioactive carbon dioxide, which becomes part of the atmospheric carbon cycle.

Principle of Radiocarbon Dating

  1. Living organisms continuously exchange carbon with their surroundings through processes such as photosynthesis and feeding.
  2. Therefore, the ratio of radioactive \(^{14}\mathrm{C}\) to stable carbon in living organisms remains approximately in equilibrium with the environment.
  3. When an organism dies, it stops exchanging carbon with the environment.
  4. The \(^{14}\mathrm{C}\) already present in the organism continues to decay.
  5. Stable carbon isotopes such as \(^{12}\mathrm{C}\) remain essentially unchanged.
  6. By measuring the remaining \(^{14}\mathrm{C}\) activity and comparing it with the activity in living material, the age of the sample can be calculated.

Carbon-14 undergoes beta-minus decay:

\[ {}^{14}_{6}\mathrm{C}\rightarrow{}^{14}_{7}\mathrm{N}+e^-+\bar{\nu}_e \] \(e^-\) is a beta-minus particle and \(\bar{\nu}_e\) is an electron antineutrino.

The half-life of carbon-14 is approximately

\[ \boxed{T_{1/2}=5730\ \text{years}} \]

Age Equation for Radiocarbon Dating

If \(A_0\) is the activity of a living sample and \(A\) is the activity of the archaeological sample after time \(t\), then

\[ A=A_0e^{-\lambda t} \]

Therefore,

\[ \frac{A}{A_0}=e^{-\lambda t} \]

Taking natural logarithm,

\[ \ln\left(\frac{A}{A_0}\right)=-\lambda t \]

Hence,

\[ \boxed{t=\frac{1}{\lambda}\ln\left(\frac{A_0}{A}\right)} \] \(A_0\) is the initial activity and \(A\) is the measured activity of the sample.

Since

\[ \lambda=\frac{0.693}{T_{1/2}} \]

the age can also be written as

\[ \boxed{t=\frac{T_{1/2}}{0.693}\ln\left(\frac{A_0}{A}\right)} \] For carbon-14 dating, \(T_{1/2}\approx5730\) years.

Shortcut: If the remaining activity is exactly a simple fraction of the original activity, use half-lives directly. For example, \(A/A_0=1/2\) means one half-life, \(1/4\) means two half-lives, and \(1/8\) means three half-lives.

24.7 Medical Uses and Health Hazards of Nuclear Radiation

Medical Uses

Ionizing radiation can be harmful at excessive doses, but controlled radiation is extremely useful in modern medicine.

  • Radiotherapy: High-energy radiation, including gamma radiation from suitable radioactive sources and radiation from medical accelerators, is used to destroy cancer cells.
  • Diagnostic radiotracers: Radioactive isotopes are used as tracers to investigate the functioning of organs. Technetium-99m is widely used in nuclear medicine imaging.
  • Thyroid diagnosis and treatment: Radioactive iodine, particularly iodine-131, can be used in the diagnosis and treatment of certain thyroid disorders.
  • PET scanning: Positron-emitting radionuclides such as fluorine-18 are used in positron emission tomography to study metabolic activity.
  • Sterilization: Gamma radiation can be used to sterilize medical equipment and materials that may be damaged by heat sterilization.

Health Hazards

Ionizing radiation can remove electrons from atoms and molecules in living tissue. Excessive exposure can therefore damage cells and DNA.

  • Cell and DNA damage: Radiation can break chemical bonds and damage DNA.
  • Mutation: Improperly repaired DNA damage may result in mutations.
  • Cancer: Radiation-induced mutations may increase the probability of cancer.
  • Cell death: Large radiation doses can kill cells and damage tissues.
  • Acute radiation effects: Very high doses received over a short time may cause nausea, vomiting, skin injury, hair loss, bone-marrow damage, and potentially death.
  • Genetic effects: Radiation damage to reproductive cells can potentially cause genetic changes.

Protection from Radiation

The three basic principles of radiation protection are commonly summarized as time, distance, and shielding.

  • Time: Minimize the time spent near a radiation source.
  • Distance: Maximize the distance from the source whenever possible.
  • Shielding: Use appropriate shielding materials such as lead, concrete, or other suitable radiation barriers.

ALARA principle: Radiation exposure should be kept As Low As Reasonably Achievable, consistent with the required task.

Common mistake: Radiation is not always harmful. The effect depends strongly on the type of radiation, dose, exposure time, distance, shielding, and the biological tissue exposed.

24.8 Numerical Problems

Example 1: Finding the Number of Half-lives

Problem: The half-life of radon is \(3.8\) days. After how many days will only \(1/16\) of the original amount remain undecayed?

Show solution

Given:

  • \(T_{1/2}=3.8\) days
  • \(N=N_0/16\)

After \(n\) half-lives,

\[ N=N_0\left(\frac12\right)^n \]

Therefore,

\[ \frac{N_0}{16}=N_0\left(\frac12\right)^n \]
\[ \left(\frac12\right)^4=\left(\frac12\right)^n \]

Hence, \(n=4\).

\[ t=nT_{1/2}=4(3.8)=15.2\ \text{days} \]

Answer: \(15.2\) days.

Example 2: Half-life and Mean Life from Decay Constant

Problem: The decay constant of a radioactive isotope is \(1.5\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\). Calculate its half-life and mean life.

Show solution

Given:

\[ \lambda=1.5\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}} \]

Half-life:

\[ T_{1/2}=\frac{0.693}{\lambda} \]
\[ T_{1/2}=\frac{0.693}{1.5\times10^{-3}} =462\ \mathrm{s} \]

Mean life:

\[ \tau=\frac{1}{\lambda} \]
\[ \tau=\frac{1}{1.5\times10^{-3}} =666.7\ \mathrm{s} \]

Answer: Half-life \(=462\) s and mean life \(=666.7\) s.

Example 3: Activity after Several Half-lives

Problem: A radioactive sample has an initial activity of \(8\times10^6\) Bq. If its half-life is \(8\) days, find its activity after \(24\) days.

Show solution

Given:

  • \(A_0=8\times10^6\) Bq
  • \(T_{1/2}=8\) days
  • \(t=24\) days

Number of half-lives:

\[ n=\frac{t}{T_{1/2}}=\frac{24}{8}=3 \]

Therefore,

\[ A=A_0\left(\frac12\right)^n \]
\[ A=8\times10^6\left(\frac12\right)^3 \]
\[ A=8\times10^6\times\frac18=1\times10^6\ \mathrm{Bq} \]

Answer: \(1.0\times10^6\) Bq.

Example 4: Radiocarbon Dating

Problem: An ancient wooden artifact has a carbon-14 activity of \(3.825\) disintegrations per minute per gram of carbon. Fresh wood has an activity of \(15.3\) disintegrations per minute per gram. Calculate the age of the artifact. Take the half-life of carbon-14 as \(5730\) years.

Show solution

Given:

  • \(A_0=15.3\) dpm/g
  • \(A=3.825\) dpm/g
  • \(T_{1/2}=5730\) years

The ratio of activities is

\[ \frac{A}{A_0}=\frac{3.825}{15.3}=0.25=\frac14 \]

Since

\[ \frac14=\left(\frac12\right)^2 \]

two half-lives have elapsed.

\[ t=2T_{1/2}=2(5730)=11460\ \text{years} \]

Alternatively, using the logarithmic equation gives the same result:

\[ t=\frac{5730}{0.693}\ln\left(\frac{15.3}{3.825}\right) \approx11460\ \text{years} \]

Answer: The artifact is approximately \(11,460\) years old.

Example 5: Finding Remaining Nuclei

Problem: A radioactive sample initially contains \(6.4\times10^{20}\) nuclei. Its half-life is \(5\) days. How many nuclei remain after \(15\) days?

Show solution

Number of half-lives:

\[ n=\frac{15}{5}=3 \]

Therefore,

\[ N=N_0\left(\frac12\right)^3 \]
\[ N=6.4\times10^{20}\times\frac18 =0.8\times10^{20} \]
\[ \boxed{N=8.0\times10^{19}} \]

Answer: \(8.0\times10^{19}\) nuclei remain.

Example 6: Finding Decay Constant from Half-life

Problem: A radioactive substance has a half-life of \(20\) minutes. Calculate its decay constant in \(\mathrm{s^{-1}}\).

Show solution

Convert the half-life into seconds:

\[ T_{1/2}=20\times60=1200\ \mathrm{s} \]

Using

\[ \lambda=\frac{0.693}{T_{1/2}} \]
\[ \lambda=\frac{0.693}{1200} =5.775\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}} \]

Answer: \(\lambda\approx5.78\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\).

24.9 Conceptual Questions

Why is radioactivity a nuclear phenomenon?

Radioactivity originates from an unstable atomic nucleus. During radioactive decay, the nuclear composition changes, while the extranuclear electrons do not determine the decay probability. Hence, radioactivity is a nuclear rather than an ordinary atomic or chemical phenomenon.

Why is radioactive decay unaffected by temperature and pressure?

Radioactive decay depends on the internal structure and stability of the nucleus. Ordinary changes in temperature and pressure mainly affect electrons and molecular arrangements and do not significantly alter nuclear structure. Therefore, radioactive decay is essentially independent of temperature and pressure.

Why are gamma rays not deflected by electric or magnetic fields?

Gamma rays are photons and have no electric charge. The force on a charged particle in an electric and magnetic field is

\[ \vec F=q(\vec E+\vec v\times\vec B) \] Since \(q=0\) for gamma radiation, the Lorentz force is zero.

Therefore, gamma rays are not deflected by electric or magnetic fields.

Why are beta particles deflected more than alpha particles?

The magnitude of deflection in a magnetic or electric field depends strongly on the charge-to-mass ratio. Beta particles have a very small mass compared with alpha particles, so their charge-to-mass ratio is much larger. Hence, beta particles are deflected much more strongly.

Why is the penetrating power of alpha particles less than that of beta particles?

Alpha particles have a large mass and charge \(+2e\). They produce strong ionization as they pass through matter and consequently lose their kinetic energy rapidly. Beta particles have much smaller mass and generally produce less ionization per unit path, allowing them to travel farther through matter.

Why does a radioactive substance never completely disappear theoretically?

Radioactive decay follows an exponential law:

\[ N=N_0e^{-\lambda t} \]

For any finite value of \(t\), \(e^{-\lambda t}\) is greater than zero. Thus, the number of undecayed nuclei approaches zero asymptotically and does not become exactly zero at a finite time.

If the half-life of a substance is one hour, will the entire substance decay in two hours?

No. After one hour, half the substance remains. During the next hour, half of the remaining half decays, leaving one-fourth of the original amount.

\[ N=N_0\left(\frac12\right)^2=\frac{N_0}{4} \] Thus \(25\%\) remains after two half-lives.

What is the purpose of quenching gas in a GM tube?

The quenching gas prevents continuous unwanted discharge after an avalanche. It absorbs ultraviolet photons and helps neutralize excited or positive gas species, so that a single incoming radiation event produces a distinct countable electrical pulse.

Why does the activity of a radioactive sample decrease with time?

Activity is given by

\[ A=\lambda N \] \(A\) is activity, \(\lambda\) is decay constant and \(N\) is the number of undecayed nuclei.

As radioactive nuclei decay, \(N\) decreases. Since \(\lambda\) remains constant for a particular nuclide, the activity also decreases exponentially.

Can a radioactive nucleus emit alpha and beta particles during the same decay step?

A particular radioactive decay step has a definite decay mode. An alpha decay and a beta decay are different nuclear transformations. A nucleus may undergo a sequence of different decays through a decay chain, but an individual decay event is characterized by its particular decay process.

Why is radiocarbon dating useful for archaeological materials?

Living organisms continually exchange carbon with their environment. After death, this exchange stops and the radioactive carbon-14 already present begins to decrease by radioactive decay. Measuring the remaining carbon-14 and comparing it with the initial level provides an estimate of the time elapsed since death.

What are the three basic principles of radiation protection?

The three basic principles are time, distance, and shielding: reduce exposure time, increase distance from the source, and use appropriate shielding.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Do not confuse half-life with mean life. Remember \(T_{1/2}=0.693/\lambda\) and \(\tau=1/\lambda\).
  • Do not use \(N=N_0(1/2)^t\) unless \(t\) has first been divided by the half-life.
  • Always write \(N=N_0(1/2)^{t/T_{1/2}}\) when using the half-life form.
  • Do not forget the negative sign in \(dN/dt=-\lambda N\); the number of undecayed nuclei decreases with time.
  • Do not state that gamma rays have mass equal to the electron or proton. Photons have zero rest mass.
  • Do not say that gamma rays are deflected by electric or magnetic fields.
  • Do not interchange ionizing power and penetrating power. Alpha has the highest ionizing power but the lowest penetrating power.
  • In numerical problems, make sure all time units are consistent with the unit of \(\lambda\) or \(T_{1/2}\).
  • For radiocarbon dating, compare the measured activity with the activity of a living reference sample.
  • In GM-tube questions, mention gas ionization, Townsend avalanche, electrical pulse, and quenching.
