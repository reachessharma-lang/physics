--- title: "Dispersion" grade: XI chapters: [18] date: 2026-10-08 ---

When a narrow beam of white sunlight passes through a glass prism, it spreads out into a band of colours. This splitting of white light is called dispersion. It explains rainbows and the colour fringes around images in cheap lenses, and it is the reason good cameras and telescopes use combinations of lenses instead of a single lens. This chapter covers how a pure spectrum is obtained, how the spreading of colours is measured by dispersive power, why lenses form coloured and blurred images (aberrations), and how combining two lenses removes the colour defect (achromatism).

Sign convention used in these notes: for lenses, focal length \(f\) is positive for a convex lens and negative for a concave lens. Real distances are positive and virtual distances are negative (the same convention as Chapter 17). Colours are written as V (violet), I (indigo), B (blue), G (green), Y (yellow), O (orange), R (red), in the order VIBGYOR. Violet has the shortest wavelength and red the longest.

18.1 Pure Spectrum

Dispersion of light

Definition: Dispersion is the splitting of white (composite) light into its constituent colours when it passes through a refracting medium such as a glass prism.

The band of colours obtained on a screen is called a spectrum. Isaac Newton first showed that a prism does not create the colours. White light is already a mixture of colours, and the prism only separates them.

Cause of dispersion

  • In vacuum (and nearly in air) all colours travel with the same speed \(c\). In glass, different colours travel with different speeds. Red travels fastest and violet slowest.
  • Since \(n = \dfrac{c}{v}\), the refractive index of glass is different for each colour: \(n_v > n_r\). A larger \(n\) gives a larger bending, so violet is deviated more than red.
  • The variation of \(n\) with wavelength is given approximately by Cauchy's relation \( n = a + \dfrac{b}{\lambda^2} \), where \(a\) and \(b\) are constants of the material. A shorter wavelength therefore gives a larger \(n\).

Deviation by a thin prism

Let a ray enter a prism of small refracting angle \(A\) at a small angle of incidence \(i\). Let \(r_1\) be the angle of refraction at the first face, \(r_2\) the angle of incidence at the second face and \(e\) the angle of emergence. Then

\[ A = r_1 + r_2, \qquad \delta = i + e - A \]

For small angles, Snell's law gives \(i = n r_1\) and \(e = n r_2\). Hence

\[ \delta = n(r_1 + r_2) - A = nA - A \] \[ \boxed{\delta = (n-1)A} \]

The deviation is larger for a colour with larger \(n\). This result is used in the next section.

Definition: A pure spectrum is a spectrum in which the different colours are completely separated, with no overlapping of colours, so that each colour is seen distinctly.

Why the spectrum overlaps

A hole or wide source acts as many point sources. Each point forms its own complete spectrum, shifted a little from the others. These spectra overlap, so the spectrum is impure. Rays from one point also reach the prism at slightly different angles, so rays of the same colour are not all deviated equally.

Conditions and method for a pure spectrum

  1. Narrow slit: use a thin slit \(S\) as the source so that each colour forms a narrow image of the slit.
  2. Collimating lens (L₁): place the slit at the focus of a convex lens so that a parallel beam falls on the prism. Then all rays of the same colour meet the prism at the same angle and are deviated equally.
  3. Prism at minimum deviation: set the prism in the position of minimum deviation. The image is then sharpest and brightest.
  4. Focusing lens (L₂): a second convex lens collects each parallel coloured beam and focuses it to a separate point on the screen.

This is the arrangement used in a spectrometer. In the spectrometer, \(L_1\) is the collimator and \(L_2\) is the telescope objective.

18.2 Dispersive Power

Angular dispersion

Definition: Angular dispersion (\(\theta\)) is the angle between the emergent rays of two extreme colours, usually violet and red, after passing through the prism.

For a thin prism of refracting angle \(A\), using \(\delta = (n-1)A\):

\[ \delta_v = (n_v - 1)A, \qquad \delta_r = (n_r - 1)A \] \[ \theta = \delta_v - \delta_r = (n_v - n_r)A \]

Mean deviation

The deviation of the mean colour, usually yellow (\(n_y\) or \(n\) for the D-line of sodium), is the mean deviation:

\[ \delta_y = (n_y - 1)A \]

Dispersive power

Definition: The dispersive power (\(\omega\)) of the material of a prism is the ratio of the angular dispersion between two extreme colours (violet and red) to the mean deviation.

\[ \omega = \frac{\delta_v - \delta_r}{\delta_y} \]

Expression for dispersive power

For a thin prism,

\[ \omega = \frac{(n_v - 1)A - (n_r - 1)A}{(n_y - 1)A} \] \[ \boxed{\omega = \frac{n_v - n_r}{n_y - 1}} \]

The prism angle \(A\) cancels out.

Important points

  • \(\omega\) has no unit. It is a pure number.
  • \(\omega\) depends only on the material of the prism (or lens). It does not depend on the refracting angle \(A\) or the size of the prism. A prism of larger \(A\) gives a larger angular dispersion \(\theta\), but the same \(\omega\).
  • A material with a larger \(\omega\) spreads the colours more for the same mean deviation. Flint glass has a larger \(\omega\) than crown glass.
  • The mean refractive index \(n_y\) is sometimes taken as \(\dfrac{n_v + n_r}{2}\). The two forms give almost the same value.

18.3 Chromatic and Spherical Aberration

A real lens does not form a perfect point image of a point object. The defects are called aberrations. Two of them are chromatic aberration (caused by dispersion) and spherical aberration (caused by the spherical shape of the surfaces).

Chromatic aberration

Definition: Chromatic aberration is the defect of a lens in which white light from a point object is not brought to a single focus, so that the image is blurred and has coloured edges.

Cause. The focal length of a lens depends on the refractive index of its material. By the lens maker's formula, with \(K = \dfrac{1}{R_1} + \dfrac{1}{R_2}\),

\[ \frac{1}{f} = (n-1)K \]

Because \(n_v > n_r\), the focal length for violet is smaller than for red: \(f_v < f_r\). A convex lens therefore brings violet to a focus \(F_v\) nearer to the lens than the red focus \(F_r\).

Types of chromatic aberration

  • Longitudinal (axial) chromatic aberration: the difference between the focal lengths for red and violet, \(f_r - f_v\), measured along the principal axis.
  • Lateral (transverse) chromatic aberration: the difference in size (height) of the images formed in different colours, measured perpendicular to the axis. It shows up as coloured fringes at the edges of an image.

Expression for longitudinal chromatic aberration

For violet and red light, the lens maker's formula gives

\[ \frac{1}{f_v} = (n_v - 1)K, \qquad \frac{1}{f_r} = (n_r - 1)K \]

Subtract:

\[ \frac{1}{f_v} - \frac{1}{f_r} = (n_v - n_r)K \]

For the mean colour, \( \dfrac{1}{f} = (n_y - 1)K \), so \( K = \dfrac{1}{(n_y - 1)f} \). Substituting,

\[ \frac{1}{f_v} - \frac{1}{f_r} = \frac{n_v - n_r}{n_y - 1} \cdot \frac{1}{f} = \frac{\omega}{f} \quad \text{... (i)} \]

The left side equals \( \dfrac{f_r - f_v}{f_v f_r} \). Since \(f_v\) and \(f_r\) are both close to \(f\), take \(f_v f_r \approx f^2\):

\[ \frac{f_r - f_v}{f^2} = \frac{\omega}{f} \] \[ \boxed{f_r - f_v = \omega f} \]

The longitudinal chromatic aberration of a lens is therefore equal to the product of the dispersive power of its material and its mean focal length. Equation (i) is also used in the next section.

Effect on a concave lens

A concave lens also has \(|f_v| < |f_r|\). The virtual focus for violet is closer to the lens than that for red.

Why mirrors have no chromatic aberration

Reflection does not depend on the wavelength, so a mirror forms the same image for all colours. This is one reason large astronomical telescopes use mirrors as objectives.

Spherical aberration

Definition: Spherical aberration is the defect of a lens (or mirror) with spherical surfaces in which rays from a point object that pass through different zones of the lens are focused at different points on the axis, so that the image is blurred.

This defect occurs even in monochromatic (single-colour) light.

Cause. The simple lens formula holds only for paraxial rays, which are close to the principal axis and make small angles. Rays near the edge (marginal rays) strike the surface at larger angles of incidence, where \(\sin\theta\) is no longer equal to \(\theta\). They are bent too much and cross the axis at a point \(F_m\) closer to the lens than the focus \(F_p\) of the paraxial rays.

The image of a point is a small blurred disc. The smallest disc, found between \(F_m\) and \(F_p\), is called the circle of least confusion.

Methods of reducing spherical aberration

  1. Using a stop (diaphragm): a circular opening in front of the lens cuts off the marginal rays. This reduces the aberration but also reduces the brightness of the image.
  2. Using a plano-convex lens: when a parallel beam falls on a plano-convex lens with its curved face towards the incident light, the bending is shared between the two surfaces and the aberration is much smaller.
  3. Using two lenses separated by a distance: two suitable lenses with a gap between them, as in the Huygens eyepiece, share the total bending and reduce the aberration.
  4. Using a convex and a concave lens combination: a converging and a diverging lens of suitable curvature can have opposite spherical aberrations that cancel.
  5. Using a lens with non-spherical (parabolic) surfaces: such surfaces bring all parallel rays to one point. Parabolic mirrors are used in searchlights and reflecting telescopes.

18.4 Achromatism in Lenses and its Applications

Definition: Achromatism is the removal of chromatic aberration. An achromatic lens (achromatic doublet) is a combination of two lenses of different materials, usually a convex lens of crown glass and a concave lens of flint glass, that forms the image of a point source at the same point for two chosen colours (red and violet), so the image has no coloured fringes.

Principle

A convex lens alone bends violet more than red. A concave lens bends violet more than red as well, but in the opposite sense: it diverges violet more. If a convex lens and a concave lens of suitable focal lengths and dispersive powers are placed in contact, the extra convergence of violet from the convex lens is cancelled by the extra divergence of violet from the concave lens. The combination then has the same focal length for violet and red.

Condition for achromatism

Consider two thin lenses in contact. Lens 1 is made of a material of dispersive power \(\omega_1\) and mean focal length \(f_1\). Lens 2 has \(\omega_2\) and \(f_2\). Both \(f_1\) and \(f_2\) carry their signs. The combined focal length \(F\) satisfies

\[ \frac{1}{F} = \frac{1}{f_1} + \frac{1}{f_2} \]

This holds for each colour separately:

\[ \frac{1}{F_v} = \frac{1}{f_{1v}} + \frac{1}{f_{2v}}, \qquad \frac{1}{F_r} = \frac{1}{f_{1r}} + \frac{1}{f_{2r}} \]

For no chromatic aberration, \(F_v = F_r\). Subtracting the two equations, this requires

\[ \left(\frac{1}{f_{1v}} - \frac{1}{f_{1r}}\right) + \left(\frac{1}{f_{2v}} - \frac{1}{f_{2r}}\right) = 0 \]

From equation (i) of the previous section, \( \dfrac{1}{f_v} - \dfrac{1}{f_r} = \dfrac{\omega}{f} \). Applying it to each lens,

\[ \frac{\omega_1}{f_1} + \frac{\omega_2}{f_2} = 0 \] \[ \boxed{\frac{\omega_1}{f_1} + \frac{\omega_2}{f_2} = 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \frac{f_1}{f_2} = -\frac{\omega_1}{\omega_2}} \]

In terms of power \(P = 1/f\), this condition is \( \omega_1 P_1 + \omega_2 P_2 = 0 \).

Conclusions from the condition

  • \(\omega_1\) and \(\omega_2\) are both positive, so \(f_1\) and \(f_2\) must have opposite signs. One lens must be convex and the other concave.
  • The magnitudes satisfy \( \dfrac{|f_1|}{|f_2|} = \dfrac{\omega_1}{\omega_2} \). The lens with the smaller dispersive power has the smaller focal length (greater power).
  • For the combination to be converging, the convex lens must be the stronger one. It must therefore be made of the material of lower dispersive power, which is crown glass. The concave lens is made of flint glass.
  • The focal length of the combination is found from \( \dfrac{1}{F} = \dfrac{1}{f_1} + \dfrac{1}{f_2} \).

Achromatic doublet in practice

  • The two lenses are usually cemented together with transparent Canada balsam, so they are in contact.
  • The doublet is achromatic only for the two chosen colours (red and violet). A small residual colour error for other colours remains. It is called the secondary spectrum.
  • Better corrected lenses, called apochromats, use three different glasses to bring three colours to one focus.
  • The doublet removes chromatic aberration but not the dispersion of the glasses themselves. The two lenses are designed so the colour errors cancel while the combination still converges light.

Applications of achromatic lenses

  1. Telescopes: the objective of a refracting telescope is an achromatic doublet, so stars and distant objects have sharp images without coloured edges.
  2. Microscopes: objectives and eyepieces use achromatic combinations to give clear, colour-free magnified images.
  3. Cameras and projectors: camera lenses contain several lens elements, including achromatic doublets, so photographs are free from colour fringes.
  4. Binoculars and spectacles: binoculars use achromatic lenses, and good-quality spectacle lenses are designed to limit colour fringes.
  5. Spectrometers: the collimator and telescope lenses are achromatic so that all colours in the spectrum are sharp.

Worked Examples

Example 1: dispersive power of crown glass

A thin crown-glass prism of refracting angle \(6^\circ\) has refractive indices 1.514, 1.517 and 1.523 for red, yellow and violet light. Find (a) the deviation of yellow light, (b) the angular dispersion, (c) the dispersive power.

Solution. \(A = 6^\circ\), \(n_r = 1.514\), \(n_y = 1.517\), \(n_v = 1.523\).

(a) \( \delta_y = (n_y - 1)A = 0.517 \times 6^\circ = 3.10^\circ \)

(b) \( \theta = (n_v - n_r)A = (1.523 - 1.514) \times 6^\circ = 0.009 \times 6^\circ = 0.054^\circ \)

(c) \( \omega = \dfrac{n_v - n_r}{n_y - 1} = \dfrac{0.009}{0.517} = 0.0174 \)

Check using the definition: \( \omega = \dfrac{\theta}{\delta_y} = \dfrac{0.054}{3.102} = 0.0174 \). ✓

Example 2: crown and flint prisms compared

Two thin prisms, one of crown glass (\(n_r = 1.514\), \(n_y = 1.517\), \(n_v = 1.523\)) and one of flint glass (\(n_r = 1.644\), \(n_y = 1.650\), \(n_v = 1.664\)), each have refracting angle \(5^\circ\). Find the dispersive power and angular dispersion of each.

Solution.

Crown: \( \omega = \dfrac{1.523 - 1.514}{0.517} = \dfrac{0.009}{0.517} = 0.0174 \), and \( \theta = 0.009 \times 5^\circ = 0.045^\circ \).

Flint: \( \omega = \dfrac{1.664 - 1.644}{0.650} = \dfrac{0.020}{0.650} = 0.0308 \), and \( \theta = 0.020 \times 5^\circ = 0.100^\circ \).

Flint glass has the larger dispersive power (about 1.8 times that of crown glass), so it spreads the colours more.

Example 3: chromatic aberration of an equiconvex lens

An equiconvex lens has both radii of curvature 20 cm. The refractive indices of its glass are 1.514 for red and 1.523 for violet. Find the focal length for each colour and the longitudinal chromatic aberration.

Solution. \( K = \dfrac{1}{R_1} + \dfrac{1}{R_2} = \dfrac{2}{20} = 0.1\ \text{cm}^{-1} \).

Red: \( \dfrac{1}{f_r} = (1.514 - 1)(0.1) = 0.0514 \Rightarrow f_r = 19.46\ \text{cm} \)

Violet: \( \dfrac{1}{f_v} = (1.523 - 1)(0.1) = 0.0523 \Rightarrow f_v = 19.12\ \text{cm} \)

Longitudinal chromatic aberration \( = f_r - f_v = 19.46 - 19.12 = 0.34\ \text{cm} \).

Check with \( \omega f \): the mean focal length is \( f = \dfrac{1}{0.517 \times 0.1} = 19.34\ \text{cm} \), so \( \omega f = 0.0174 \times 19.34 = 0.34\ \text{cm} \). ✓

Example 4: using \(f_r - f_v = \omega f\)

A convex lens of mean focal length 20 cm is made of glass of dispersive power 0.02. Find the distance between the red and violet foci.

Solution.

\[ f_r - f_v = \omega f = 0.02 \times 20 = 0.4\ \text{cm} \]

The violet focus is 0.4 cm closer to the lens than the red focus.

Example 5: achromatic doublet, finding both focal lengths

An achromatic doublet of focal length 50 cm is to be made from a crown lens (\(\omega_1 = 0.02\)) and a flint lens (\(\omega_2 = 0.04\)) in contact. Find the focal length of each lens.

Solution. Condition for achromatism:

\[ \frac{f_1}{f_2} = -\frac{\omega_1}{\omega_2} = -\frac{0.02}{0.04} = -\frac{1}{2} \;\Rightarrow\; f_2 = -2f_1 \]

Combined focal length:

\[ \frac{1}{F} = \frac{1}{f_1} + \frac{1}{f_2} = \frac{1}{f_1} - \frac{1}{2f_1} = \frac{1}{2f_1} \] \[ \frac{1}{50} = \frac{1}{2f_1} \;\Rightarrow\; f_1 = 25\ \text{cm}, \qquad f_2 = -50\ \text{cm} \]

The crown lens is convex with \(f_1 = +25\ \text{cm}\) and the flint lens is concave with \(f_2 = -50\ \text{cm}\).

Check: \( \dfrac{\omega_1}{f_1} + \dfrac{\omega_2}{f_2} = \dfrac{0.02}{25} - \dfrac{0.04}{50} = 0.0008 - 0.0008 = 0 \). ✓

Example 6: achromatic doublet using powers

An achromatic doublet of power +5 D is made of a crown-glass convex lens (\(\omega = 0.018\)) and a flint-glass concave lens (\(\omega = 0.036\)). Find the power and focal length of each lens.

Solution. Condition: \( \omega_1 P_1 + \omega_2 P_2 = 0 \).

\[ 0.018\,P_1 + 0.036\,P_2 = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; P_2 = -\frac{P_1}{2} \]

The total power is \( P_1 + P_2 = 5\ \text{D} \):

\[ P_1 - \frac{P_1}{2} = 5 \;\Rightarrow\; P_1 = +10\ \text{D}, \qquad P_2 = -5\ \text{D} \] \[ f_1 = \frac{1}{10} = 0.10\ \text{m} = +10\ \text{cm}, \qquad f_2 = \frac{1}{-5} = -0.20\ \text{m} = -20\ \text{cm} \]

The convex crown lens has focal length 10 cm and the concave flint lens has focal length 20 cm.

Example 7: ratio of focal lengths

The dispersive powers of the glasses of the two lenses of an achromatic combination are 0.012 and 0.020. If the convex lens has a focal length of 18 cm, find the focal length of the other lens and the focal length of the combination.

Solution. The lens with the smaller \(\omega\) must be the convex lens, so \(\omega_1 = 0.012\), \(f_1 = +18\ \text{cm}\), \(\omega_2 = 0.020\).

\[ f_2 = -f_1 \frac{\omega_2}{\omega_1} = -18 \times \frac{0.020}{0.012} = -30\ \text{cm} \] \[ \frac{1}{F} = \frac{1}{18} - \frac{1}{30} = \frac{5 - 3}{90} = \frac{2}{90} = \frac{1}{45} \;\Rightarrow\; F = +45\ \text{cm} \]

The second lens is concave with focal length 30 cm, and the combination is a convex lens of focal length 45 cm.

Common Mistakes

  • Saying that the prism "adds" colours to white light. The prism only separates colours that are already present in white light.
  • Saying that violet has a larger focal length than red. Violet is bent more, so \(f_v < f_r\), and the violet focus is nearer to the lens.
  • Saying that all colours have different speeds in vacuum. They have the same speed \(c\) in vacuum. They differ only in a material medium.
  • Confusing angular dispersion \(\theta = (n_v - n_r)A\) with dispersive power \(\omega = \dfrac{n_v - n_r}{n_y - 1}\). \(\theta\) depends on the prism angle. \(\omega\) depends only on the material and has no unit.
  • Writing the mean deviation as \((n_v - n_r)A\) instead of \((n_y - 1)A\), or using \(n_v\) in the denominator of \(\omega\).
  • Describing a pure spectrum without all four requirements: narrow slit, collimating lens, prism at minimum deviation and focusing lens. Leaving out any of them is a common loss of marks.
  • Forgetting the sign of \(f\) when applying \( \dfrac{\omega_1}{f_1} + \dfrac{\omega_2}{f_2} = 0 \). The two focal lengths must have opposite signs, so one lens is convex and the other concave.
  • Making the concave lens of crown glass and the convex lens of flint. For a converging achromatic doublet, the convex lens is crown (smaller \(\omega\)) and the concave lens is flint (larger \(\omega\)).
  • Claiming that an achromatic doublet also removes spherical aberration. It removes chromatic aberration for two colours only.
  • Saying that spherical aberration is caused by colour. It is caused by the spherical shape of the surfaces and occurs even for single-colour light.
  • In diagrams: forgetting arrowheads on the rays, not labelling \(F_v\) and \(F_r\) (or \(F_m\) and \(F_p\)), or drawing the violet focus farther from the lens than the red focus.

Key Points Summary

  • Dispersion is the splitting of white light into its colours (VIBGYOR) on passing through a prism. It happens because the refractive index is different for each colour (\(n_v > n_r\)). Violet deviates the most and red the least.
  • For a thin prism, deviation \( \delta = (n-1)A \).
  • A pure spectrum has distinct, non-overlapping colours. It requires a narrow slit, a collimating lens, a prism at minimum deviation and a focusing lens. In an impure spectrum the colours overlap.
  • Angular dispersion: \( \theta = \delta_v - \delta_r = (n_v - n_r)A \). Mean deviation: \( \delta_y = (n_y - 1)A \).
  • Dispersive power: \( \omega = \dfrac{n_v - n_r}{n_y - 1} = \dfrac{\delta_v - \delta_r}{\delta_y} \). It has no unit and depends only on the material. Flint glass has a larger \(\omega\) than crown glass.
  • Chromatic aberration is the failure of a lens to focus all colours at one point because \(f\) depends on \(n\). Violet focuses closest to the lens (\(f_v < f_r\)). Longitudinal chromatic aberration is \( f_r - f_v = \omega f \).
  • Spherical aberration is the failure to focus marginal and paraxial rays at one point. Marginal rays focus closer to the lens. It is reduced by stops, plano-convex lenses, lens combinations and aspherical surfaces.
  • Achromatism is the removal of chromatic aberration. The condition for two thin lenses in contact is \( \dfrac{\omega_1}{f_1} + \dfrac{\omega_2}{f_2} = 0 \), or \( \omega_1 P_1 + \omega_2 P_2 = 0 \).
  • An achromatic doublet is a convex crown-glass lens cemented to a concave flint-glass lens. The two lenses have opposite signs of focal length, and \( \dfrac{|f_1|}{|f_2|} = \dfrac{\omega_1}{\omega_2} \). The combined focal length is given by \( \dfrac{1}{F} = \dfrac{1}{f_1} + \dfrac{1}{f_2} \).
  • The doublet is achromatic for two colours only. A small residual error (secondary spectrum) remains. Apochromats correct three colours.
  • Mirrors have no chromatic aberration. Achromatic lenses are used in telescopes, microscopes, cameras, binoculars and spectrometers.
