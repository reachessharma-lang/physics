Magnetic materials respond differently when placed in an external magnetic field. Their magnetic behaviour is described using quantities such as magnetic intensity, magnetization, magnetic susceptibility and relative permeability. On the basis of their magnetic response, materials are broadly classified as diamagnetic, paramagnetic and ferromagnetic.

16.1 Relative Permeability and Relative Susceptibility

Magnetic Field Intensity

The magnetic field intensity or magnetizing field is represented by \(H\). It describes the strength of the externally applied magnetic field.

For a long solenoid,

\[ H=nI \] where \(n\) is the number of turns per unit length and \(I\) is the current.

The SI unit of \(H\) is \(\mathrm{A\,m^{-1}}\).

Magnetic Induction

The magnetic flux density or magnetic induction is represented by \(B\). In vacuum,

\[ B_0=\mu_0H \] \(\mu_0\) is the permeability of free space.

For a magnetic material,

\[ B=\mu H \] \(\mu\) is the permeability of the material.

Permeability

Permeability: The permeability of a material is a measure of the ability of the material to allow magnetic field lines to pass through it.

It is defined as

\[ \boxed{\mu=\frac{B}{H}} \]

The SI unit of permeability is \(\mathrm{H\,m^{-1}}\) or equivalently \(\mathrm{N\,A^{-2}}\).

Relative Permeability

Relative permeability: The relative permeability of a magnetic material is the ratio of its permeability to the permeability of free space.
\[ \boxed{\mu_r=\frac{\mu}{\mu_0}} \]

Since

\[ \mu=\mu_0\mu_r \]

we can write

\[ \boxed{B=\mu_0\mu_rH} \]
Relative permeability is dimensionless because it is the ratio of two quantities having the same unit.

Magnetization

When a magnetic material is placed in an external magnetic field, its magnetic dipoles become preferentially oriented. The magnetic dipole moment produced per unit volume of the material is called its magnetization.

\[ \boxed{M=\frac{\text{magnetic dipole moment}}{\text{volume}}} \]

The SI unit of magnetization is \(\mathrm{A\,m^{-1}}\).

For a magnetic material, the relationship between \(B\), \(H\), and \(M\) is

\[ \boxed{B=\mu_0(H+M)} \]

Magnetic Susceptibility

Magnetic susceptibility: Magnetic susceptibility is the measure of the degree to which a material becomes magnetized when placed in an external magnetic field.

It is defined as the ratio of magnetization to magnetic field intensity.

\[ \boxed{\chi_m=\frac{M}{H}} \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{M=\chi_mH} \]
Magnetic susceptibility \(\chi_m\) is dimensionless because both \(M\) and \(H\) have the same SI unit \(\mathrm{A\,m^{-1}}\).

16.2 Relationship Between Relative Permeability and Magnetic Susceptibility

For a magnetic material,

\[ B=\mu_0(H+M) \]

Since

\[ M=\chi_mH \]

substituting this into the expression for \(B\),

\[ B=\mu_0(H+\chi_mH) \]

Therefore,

\[ B=\mu_0(1+\chi_m)H \]

But

\[ B=\mu H=\mu_0\mu_rH \]

Comparing the two equations,

\[ \mu_0\mu_rH=\mu_0(1+\chi_m)H \]

Hence,

\[ \boxed{\mu_r=1+\chi_m} \]
Important relationship: \[ \boxed{\mu_r=1+\chi_m} \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\chi_m=\mu_r-1} \]

Interpretation of the Relationship

Material Magnetic susceptibility \(\chi_m\) Relative permeability \(\mu_r\) Magnetic behaviour
Diamagnetic Small and negative Slightly less than 1 Weakly repelled
Paramagnetic Small and positive Slightly greater than 1 Weakly attracted
Ferromagnetic Large and positive Much greater than 1 Strongly attracted
Example 1: Finding relative permeability

A material has magnetic susceptibility \(2.5\times10^{-3}\). Find its relative permeability.

Show solution

Using

\[ \mu_r=1+\chi_m \]

Given

\[ \chi_m=2.5\times10^{-3} \]

Therefore,

\[ \mu_r=1+2.5\times10^{-3} \] $$ \boxed{\mu_r=1.0025} $$

Answer: The relative permeability is \(1.0025\).

Example 2: Finding magnetic susceptibility

The relative permeability of a material is \(1.0008\). Find its magnetic susceptibility.

Show solution

Using

\[ \chi_m=\mu_r-1 \]

Therefore,

\[ \chi_m=1.0008-1 \] $$ \boxed{\chi_m=8\times10^{-4}} $$

Answer: The magnetic susceptibility is \(8\times10^{-4}\).

16.3 Hysteresis of Ferromagnetism

Magnetic hysteresis: The phenomenon in which the magnetization \(M\) or magnetic induction \(B\) of a ferromagnetic material lags behind the magnetizing field \(H\) when the magnetic field is changed is called magnetic hysteresis.

Ferromagnetic materials contain magnetic domains. In an unmagnetized specimen, the magnetic moments of different domains are oriented in different directions, resulting in very small net magnetization.

When an external magnetic field is applied, the magnetic domains gradually align with the applied field. As the field is increased, the magnetization increases until the material reaches saturation.

Hysteresis Loop

The hysteresis loop represents the variation of magnetic induction \(B\) with the magnetizing field \(H\).

Stages of the Hysteresis Cycle

1. Initial Magnetization

Starting from an unmagnetized state, as \(H\) is increased, \(B\) increases. Initially, the increase may be rapid because magnetic domains begin to align with the applied field.

2. Saturation

When nearly all magnetic domains are aligned, further increase in \(H\) produces only a small increase in \(B\). This condition is called magnetic saturation.

3. Retentivity or Remanence

When the magnetizing field is reduced to zero after the material has been magnetized, some magnetism remains in the material. The ability of a material to retain magnetism is called retentivity or remanence.

Retentivity: The ability of a magnetic material to retain magnetism after the external magnetizing field is removed is called retentivity.

4. Coercivity

To reduce the residual magnetism to zero, a reverse magnetic field must be applied. The magnitude of the reverse field required to make the magnetization or magnetic induction zero is called the coercive field or coercivity.

Coercivity: The magnitude of the reverse magnetizing field required to reduce the residual magnetism of a material to zero is called coercivity.

5. Reverse Saturation

If the reverse magnetic field is increased further, the material becomes saturated in the opposite direction.

When the reverse field is removed and then restored in the original direction, the complete hysteresis loop is obtained.

Energy Loss Due to Hysteresis

During repeated magnetization and demagnetization, some energy is lost in the form of heat. This energy loss is called hysteresis loss.

Important: The energy loss per unit volume per cycle is proportional to the area enclosed by the hysteresis loop.
\[ \boxed{W_h\propto\text{area of hysteresis loop}} \]

A narrow hysteresis loop represents small hysteresis loss, whereas a wide hysteresis loop represents large hysteresis loss.

Soft and Hard Magnetic Materials

Property Soft magnetic material Hard magnetic material
Coercivity Small Large
Retentivity Relatively small Large
Hysteresis loop Narrow Wide
Hysteresis loss Small Large
Magnetization Easy Difficult
Demagnetization Easy Difficult
Applications Transformer cores, electromagnets Permanent magnets
Example 3: Hysteresis loss

A magnetic material has a hysteresis-loop area of \(0.20\,\mathrm{J\,m^{-3}}\) per cycle. If the material undergoes \(50\) magnetization cycles per second, find the hysteresis power loss per unit volume.

Show solution

Energy loss per unit volume per cycle is

\[ W=0.20\,\mathrm{J\,m^{-3}} \]

Frequency of magnetization is

\[ f=50\,\mathrm{s^{-1}} \]

Therefore, power loss per unit volume is

\[ P= Wf \]
\[ P=0.20\times50 \] $$ \boxed{P=10\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-3}}} $$

Answer: The hysteresis power loss is \(10\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-3}}\).

16.4 Diamagnetic, Paramagnetic and Ferromagnetic Materials

Magnetic materials are classified according to their response to an external magnetic field into three main groups:

  • Diamagnetic materials
  • Paramagnetic materials
  • Ferromagnetic materials

Diamagnetic Materials

Diamagnetic materials: Materials that are weakly repelled by an external magnetic field and acquire magnetization opposite to the applied field are called diamagnetic materials.

In diamagnetic materials, the magnetic susceptibility is small and negative.

\[ \boxed{\chi_m<0} \]

Since

\[ \mu_r=1+\chi_m \]

and \(\chi_m\) is negative,

\[ \boxed{\mu_r<1} \]

Diamagnetic materials tend to move from a region of stronger magnetic field toward a region of weaker magnetic field.

Examples include bismuth, copper, silver, gold, water and quartz.

Paramagnetic Materials

Paramagnetic materials: Materials that are weakly attracted by an external magnetic field and acquire magnetization in the direction of the applied field are called paramagnetic materials.

For paramagnetic materials, magnetic susceptibility is small and positive.

\[ \boxed{\chi_m>0} \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\mu_r>1} \]

Paramagnetic materials tend to move from a region of weaker magnetic field toward a region of stronger magnetic field.

Examples include aluminium, platinum, oxygen and manganese salts.

Ferromagnetic Materials

Ferromagnetic materials: Materials that are strongly attracted by an external magnetic field and can retain strong magnetization after the external field is removed are called ferromagnetic materials.

Ferromagnetic materials have very large positive magnetic susceptibility and relative permeability much greater than unity.

\[ \boxed{\chi_m\gg1} \]
\[ \boxed{\mu_r\gg1} \]

The strong magnetic behaviour of ferromagnetic materials is associated with the alignment of magnetic domains.

Examples include iron, cobalt, nickel and several alloys.

Magnetic Domains

A ferromagnetic material consists of small regions called magnetic domains. Within each domain, the magnetic moments of many atoms are aligned in the same direction.

In an unmagnetized ferromagnetic material, the domains are oriented in different directions, so the net magnetic moment is small.

When an external magnetic field is applied, domains favourably oriented with the field grow and rotate toward the field direction. As a result, the material becomes strongly magnetized.

Comparison of Magnetic Materials

Property Diamagnetic Paramagnetic Ferromagnetic

Relative permeability \(\mu_r\) Slightly less than 1 Slightly greater than 1 Much greater than 1

Example 4: Identifying a magnetic material

A material has magnetic susceptibility \(\chi_m=-2\times10^{-5}\). Determine its relative permeability and identify the type of magnetic material.

Show solution

Using

\[ \mu_r=1+\chi_m \]

Therefore,

\[ \mu_r=1-2\times10^{-5} \] $$ \boxed{\mu_r=0.99998} $$

Since the susceptibility is negative and the relative permeability is slightly less than unity, the material is diamagnetic.

Answer: \(\mu_r=0.99998\), and the material is diamagnetic.

Example 5: Finding magnetization

A magnetic material has magnetic susceptibility \(4\times10^{-3}\) and is placed in a magnetic field of intensity \(500\,\mathrm{A\,m^{-1}}\). Find its magnetization.

Show solution

Using

\[ M=\chi_mH \]

Given

\[ \chi_m=4\times10^{-3}, \qquad H=500\,\mathrm{A\,m^{-1}} \]

Therefore,

\[ M=(4\times10^{-3})(500) \] $$ \boxed{M=2\,\mathrm{A\,m^{-1}}} $$

Answer: The magnetization is \(2\,\mathrm{A\,m^{-1}}\).

Important Applications

Soft Magnetic Materials

  • Transformer cores
  • Electromagnets
  • Electric motors and generators
  • Magnetic shielding

Hard Magnetic Materials

  • Permanent magnets
  • Loudspeakers
  • Magnetic recording devices
  • Compass needles

Common Mistakes

  • Do not confuse magnetic permeability \(\mu\) with relative permeability \(\mu_r\).
  • Relative permeability is dimensionless.
  • Magnetic susceptibility is also dimensionless.
  • The correct relationship is \(\mu_r=1+\chi_m\).
  • Diamagnetic materials have negative susceptibility.
  • Paramagnetic materials have small positive susceptibility.
  • Ferromagnetic materials have large positive susceptibility.
  • Retentivity measures the ability to retain magnetism.
  • Coercivity is the reverse magnetic field required to remove the residual magnetism.
  • The area of a hysteresis loop represents energy loss per unit volume per magnetization cycle.
  • Soft magnetic materials have narrow hysteresis loops and small hysteresis losses.
  • Hard magnetic materials have wide hysteresis loops and high retentivity and coercivity.

Key Points Summary

  • Magnetic induction is given by \(B=\mu H\).
  • Relative permeability is \(\mu_r=\mu/\mu_0\).
  • Magnetic susceptibility is \(\chi_m=M/H\).
  • The fundamental relationship is \(\mu_r=1+\chi_m\).
  • Diamagnetic materials have \(\chi_m<0\) and \(\mu_r<1\).
  • Paramagnetic materials have small positive \(\chi_m\) and \(\mu_r>1\).
  • Ferromagnetic materials have large positive \(\chi_m\) and \(\mu_r\gg1\).
  • Ferromagnetic materials consist of magnetic domains.
  • Hysteresis is the lagging of magnetization or magnetic induction behind the magnetizing field.
  • Retentivity is the ability of a material to retain magnetism.
  • Coercivity is the reverse field required to reduce residual magnetism to zero.
  • The area of the hysteresis loop represents energy loss per unit volume per cycle.
  • Soft magnetic materials are suitable for transformer cores and electromagnets.
  • Hard magnetic materials are suitable for permanent magnets.
