23.1 Bohr's Model of the Hydrogen Atom

Rutherford's nuclear atom could not explain why an orbiting electron (which should radiate and spiral into the nucleus) does not collapse, nor why atoms emit only discrete lines. In 1913 Niels Bohr combined classical mechanics with quantum ideas.

Bohr's postulates
  1. An electron revolves around the nucleus in certain circular orbits (stationary orbits); the necessary centripetal force is provided by the Coulomb attraction.
  2. Quantum condition: only those orbits are allowed in which the angular momentum of the electron is an integral multiple of h/2π:
    mvr = nh/2π   (n = 1, 2, 3, …)
  3. While in a stationary orbit the electron does not radiate energy. Radiation is emitted or absorbed only when the electron jumps between orbits:
    hf = Ei − Ef   (Ei > Ef)

23.2 Radius, Velocity and Energy of the nth Orbit

Let an electron (mass m, charge −e) move with speed v in an orbit of radius r around a nucleus of charge +e.

Radius. Coulomb force = centripetal force:

mv²/r = e²/(4πε0r²)  ⟹  mv² = e²/(4πε0r)  …(1)

Bohr's condition: v = nh/(2πmr)  …(2). Substituting (2) in (1):

m·n²h²/(4π²m²r²) = e²/(4πε0r)
rn = ε0n²h² / (πme²) = 0.529 n² Å

For n = 1, r1 = 0.529 Å (Bohr radius a0). Thus rn ∝ n²: rn = a0n².

Velocity. Put rn in (2):

vn = e² / (2ε0nh) = 2.19×106/n m s−1

So vn ∝ 1/n; the electron is fastest in the first orbit (about c/137).

Total energy. From (1), kinetic energy K = ½mv² = e²/(8πε0r). Potential energy U = −e²/(4πε0r). Hence

E = K + U = −e²/(8πε0r)

Substituting rn:

En = − me⁴ / (8ε0²h²n²) = − 13.6 / n² eV

The negative sign shows that the electron is bound. Also K = −E and U = 2E. E1 = −13.6 eV (ground state), E2 = −3.4 eV (first excited state), E3 = −1.51 eV, E∞ = 0.

23.3 Wavelength of a Spectral Line

When an electron falls from an outer orbit ni to an inner orbit nf, the photon emitted has energy

hf = Ei − Ef = (me⁴/8ε0²h²)(1/nf² − 1/ni²)

Using f = c/λ:

1/λ = R (1/nf² − 1/ni²),   R = me⁴/(8ε0²h³c) = 1.097×107 m−1

R is the Rydberg constant, and this agrees with the experimental value.

23.5 Excitation and Ionization Potentials

Excitation potential: the potential difference through which an electron must be accelerated so that, on colliding with an atom in the ground state, it raises the atom to an excited state. For hydrogen, the first excitation potential = (E2 − E1)/e = 10.2 V.

Ionization potential: the potential difference through which an electron must be accelerated so that it can remove an electron completely from the ground-state atom. For hydrogen = 13.6 V (ionization energy 13.6 eV).

Excitation potentialIonization potential
Takes the electron to a higher bound orbitRemoves the electron from the atom (n = 1 → ∞)
Many values (10.2 V, 12.09 V, …)Single value for a given atom (13.6 V for H)
Atom stays neutral and returns by emitting lightAtom becomes a positive ion

23.6 Emission and Absorption Spectra

Emission spectra are produced when a source emits light. (i) Continuous: hot solids/liquids (all wavelengths, e.g. filament lamp). (ii) Line: excited atoms in gas (bright lines, e.g. hydrogen, sodium). (iii) Band: excited molecules (closely spaced lines forming bands).

Absorption spectra are obtained when white light passes through a cool gas: the atoms absorb exactly those wavelengths which they can emit, giving dark lines on a bright continuous background. The dark Fraunhofer lines in sunlight show the elements present in the Sun's atmosphere.

23.7 de Broglie Hypothesis

Wave–particle duality

Light shows interference and diffraction (wave nature) but also the photoelectric and Compton effects (particle nature). A photon of frequency f and wavelength λ has momentum p = E/c = hf/c = h/λ. In 1924 Louis de Broglie reasoned that nature is symmetric: if radiation behaves as particles, then moving material particles should behave as waves.

de Broglie hypothesis: every moving particle of momentum p is associated with a wave (matter wave) of wavelength
λ = h/p = h/mv
where h is Planck's constant. For a relativistic or non-relativistic particle of kinetic energy K, p = √(2mK), so λ = h/√(2mK).

Expressions for de Broglie wavelength

  1. Charged particle accelerated through potential difference V: qV = ½mv² ⟹ p = √(2mqV)
    λ = h/√(2mqV)
    • Electron: λ = 12.27/√V Å
    • Proton: λ = 0.286/√V Å
    • Alpha particle: λ = 0.101/√V Å
  2. Particle of kinetic energy K: λ = h/√(2mK).
  3. Thermal (gas) particle at temperature T: average K = (3/2)kT ⟹ λ = h/√(3mkT) (k = 1.38×10−23 J/K).

Observations. (i) λ is inversely proportional to mass and speed, so lighter and faster particles have longer wavelengths. (ii) For a macroscopic body (for example a 0.1 kg ball at 10 m/s) λ ≈ 10−34 m, far too small to detect, so the wave nature of everyday objects is never noticed. (iii) For electrons accelerated through a few tens of volts, λ is about 1 Å, comparable to atomic spacing in crystals, so diffraction by crystals is observable. (iv) Matter waves are not electromagnetic waves; they are not emitted by the particle and apply even to uncharged particles such as neutrons.

de Broglie's explanation of Bohr's quantum condition

An electron in a stable orbit behaves as a standing wave: the orbit must contain a whole number of wavelengths, otherwise the wave would interfere destructively with itself after each revolution.

2πr = nλ   (n = 1, 2, 3, …)

With λ = h/mv: 2πr = nh/mv ⟹ mvr = nh/2π, which is exactly Bohr's second postulate. Thus the angular momentum is quantized because the electron wave must fit the orbit.

Experimental verification: Davisson–Germer experiment (1927)

A beam of electrons from an electron gun, accelerated through a variable potential V, strikes a nickel crystal. A movable detector measures the intensity of scattered electrons at different angles φ. The scattered intensity is a maximum at φ = 50° when V = 54 V: a diffraction peak that only waves can produce.

Check: theory gives λ = 12.27/√54 = 1.67 Å. From the crystal surface spacing D = 2.15 Å, D sin φ = λ gives λ = 2.15 × sin 50° = 1.65 Å. The two values agree, confirming de Broglie's hypothesis. G. P. Thomson independently obtained electron diffraction rings from thin metal foils.

Worked examples

Example 1. Find the de Broglie wavelength of an electron of kinetic energy 54 eV.
V = 54 V ⟹ λ = 12.27/√54 = 12.27/7.35 = 1.67 Å.
Example 2. Find the potential difference needed to give electrons a wavelength of 0.5 Å.
V = (12.27/λ)² = (12.27/0.5)² = (24.54)² ≈ 602 V.
Example 3. Calculate the de Broglie wavelength of a 50 g bullet moving at 400 m/s. Why is its wave nature not observed?
p = 0.05 × 400 = 20 kg m/s; λ = 6.63×10−34/20 = 3.3×10−35 m. This is far smaller than any atomic or nuclear dimension, so no diffraction or interference effect can be detected.
Example 4. A proton and an electron are accelerated through the same potential difference. Find the ratio λe/λp.
λ = h/√(2mqV), same q and V ⟹ λe/λp = √(mp/me) = √1836 ≈ 42.8.
Example 5. Find the de Broglie wavelength of a thermal neutron at 300 K (mn = 1.675×10−27 kg).
λ = h/√(3mnkT) = 6.63×10−34/√(3 × 1.675×10−27 × 1.38×10−23 × 300) = 6.63×10−34/4.56×10−24 = 1.45×10−10 m = 1.45 Å, comparable to crystal spacing, hence neutron diffraction.

23.7A Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle (extension)

Statement

In classical physics the position and momentum of a particle can both be known exactly at the same time. For matter waves this is impossible. In 1927 Werner Heisenberg stated:

It is impossible to determine simultaneously and with perfect accuracy both the position and the momentum of a particle. If Δx and Δpx are the uncertainties in position and in the x-component of momentum,
Δx · Δpx ≥ h/4π   (= ħ/2)
Similarly, for energy and time, and for angular position and angular momentum:
ΔE · Δt ≥ h/4π     ΔL · Δθ ≥ h/4π
Many textbooks give the order-of-magnitude form Δx · Δp ≈ h/2π (≈ ħ); the strict inequality above is the exact result.

The principle is not due to faulty instruments. It is a fundamental property of nature: a particle described by a wave packet cannot have both a sharply defined position (narrow packet) and a sharply defined wavelength, i.e. momentum (long wave train).

Illustration: γ-ray microscope (thought experiment)

To locate an electron we must "see" it with light. A microscope of aperture half-angle θ using light of wavelength λ can locate the electron only to within its resolving limit

Δx ≈ λ/(2 sin θ)

Shorter λ (γ-rays) gives a smaller Δx. But the photon has momentum h/λ and transfers part of it to the electron (Compton recoil). The scattered photon may enter the lens anywhere within the cone, so its x-momentum can lie between −(h/λ) sin θ and +(h/λ) sin θ; the electron's recoil momentum is therefore uncertain by

Δpx ≈ 2(h/λ) sin θ

Multiplying: Δx · Δpx ≈ [λ/(2 sin θ)] × [2h sin θ/λ] = h. A more precise position (smaller λ) disturbs the momentum more, and the product can never be made smaller than about h (and rigorously never below h/4π).

Consequences and applications

  1. Electrons cannot exist inside the nucleus. With Δx ≈ 10−14 m (nuclear size), Δp ≥ h/4πΔx ≈ 5.3×10−21 kg m/s, which corresponds to a kinetic energy of about 96 MeV. β-electrons from nuclei have only a few MeV, so electrons are not nuclear constituents; they are created at the moment of decay.
  2. Bohr's sharp orbits are replaced by probability. The path (position and momentum together) of an electron has no meaning; we speak of the probability of finding it in a region.
  3. Stability and size of the atom. Confining an electron within radius r gives p ≈ ħ/r and kinetic energy ≈ ħ²/2mr²; minimizing E = ħ²/2mr² − e²/4πε0r gives r = ħ²/(m e²/4πε0) = 0.53 Å and E = −13.6 eV, matching Bohr's ground state. The electron cannot fall into the nucleus because that would make its kinetic energy huge.
  4. Natural line width. An excited state with mean life Δt has energy uncertainty ΔE ≥ h/4πΔt, so spectral lines are never perfectly sharp.
  5. Macroscopic bodies. Because h is tiny (6.63×10−34 J s), the uncertainties for ordinary objects are far below any measurable limit, so classical physics works.

Worked examples

Example 1. The position of an electron is known to within 1 Å. Find the minimum uncertainty in its momentum and velocity.
Δp ≥ h/(4πΔx) = 6.63×10−34/(4π × 10−10) = 5.3×10−25 kg m/s; Δv = Δp/m = 5.3×10−25/9.1×10−31 ≈ 5.8×105 m/s.
Example 2. A 0.1 kg ball is located to within 1 μm. Find the minimum uncertainty in its speed.
Δv ≥ h/(4π m Δx) = 5.28×10−35/(0.1 × 10−6) ≈ 5.3×10−28 m/s, completely negligible.
Example 3. An excited atomic state lives for 10−8 s. Find the minimum uncertainty in its energy.
ΔE ≥ h/(4πΔt) = 5.28×10−35/10−8 = 5.3×10−27 J = 3.3×10−8 eV.
Example 4. The speed of an electron is 5.00×103 m/s, measured to an accuracy of 0.003%. Find the minimum uncertainty in its position.
Δv = 0.003% of 5000 = 0.15 m/s; Δx ≥ h/(4π m Δv) = 5.28×10−35/(9.1×10−31 × 0.15) ≈ 3.9×10−5 m.

23.8 X-rays

Definition: X-rays are high-energy electromagnetic waves of very short wavelength (about 0.1 Å to 100 Å), produced when fast-moving electrons are suddenly stopped by a heavy metal target. They were discovered by W. C. Röntgen in 1895.

23.9 Coolidge Tube

The Coolidge tube is a highly evacuated glass tube containing:

  • Cathode: a tungsten filament heated by a low-voltage battery; it emits electrons by thermionic emission. A molybdenum focusing cup directs the electrons into a narrow beam.
  • Anode (target): a tungsten block set at a slant in a copper stem, kept at a high positive potential (tens of kV) relative to the cathode. Tungsten has a high atomic number and a high melting point.
  • Cooling: over 99% of the beam energy becomes heat, so the copper stem carries heat to cooling fins/oil or water.

Quantity and quality of X-rays

  • Quantity (intensity): number of X-ray photons per second. It is controlled by the filament current: higher current → more electrons → more X-rays.
  • Quality (penetrating power): controlled by the accelerating voltage. High voltage → short λ, high energy, highly penetrating hard X-rays. Low voltage → long λ, less penetrating soft X-rays.

Origin and X-ray spectrum

Continuous spectrum arises from bremsstrahlung (braking radiation) as electrons decelerate in the target. The photon energy cannot exceed the electron energy eV:

eV = hc/λmin  ⟹  λmin = hc/eV = 12400/V Å (V in volt)

λmin depends only on V, not on the target material. Characteristic spectrum: sharp lines (Kα, Kβ, …) appear when an incident electron knocks out an inner-shell electron and an outer electron fills the vacancy; their wavelengths depend on the target element.

23.10 Properties and Applications of X-rays

Properties

  • Travel in straight lines at the speed of light; they are electromagnetic waves.
  • Not deflected by electric or magnetic fields (uncharged).
  • Highly penetrating through low-density materials such as flesh; absorbed by dense materials such as bone and lead.
  • Ionize gases; affect photographic plates; cause fluorescence in ZnS, barium platinocyanide.
  • Show reflection, refraction (very slightly), interference, polarization and diffraction by crystals (Laue, Bragg).
  • Eject photoelectrons from metals.
  • Harmful to living tissue in large doses.

Applications

  • Medicine: radiography of bones and fractures, CT scans, dental imaging; radiotherapy of cancer.
  • Industry: detecting cracks/flaws in welds and castings.
  • Science: crystal structure and atomic spacing (X-ray diffraction), studying DNA structure.
  • Security: luggage and cargo scanning at airports.
  • Art: detecting forgery and hidden layers in paintings.

23.10A Bragg's Law of X-ray Diffraction (extension)

X-rays have wavelengths (about 1 Å) comparable to the spacing between atomic planes in a crystal, so a crystal acts as a natural three-dimensional diffraction grating. W. H. Bragg and W. L. Bragg (1913) explained the diffraction as "reflection" from parallel atomic planes.

Derivation

Consider a monochromatic X-ray beam of wavelength λ incident on a set of parallel atomic planes of interplanar spacing d, making a glancing angle θ with the planes (θ is measured from the plane, not from the normal). Each plane partially reflects the beam with the angle of reflection equal to the angle of incidence.

Ray 1 reflects from plane 1 at P and ray 2 from plane 2 at Q, where PQ = d. Draw PA perpendicular to the incident ray 2 and PC perpendicular to the reflected ray 2. Ray 2 travels the extra path AQ + QC. PA is perpendicular to the ray and PQ is perpendicular to the plane, so angle APQ = θ (and likewise angle CPQ = θ). In the right-angled triangles PAQ and PCQ, therefore,

AQ = QC = d sin θ  ⟹  path difference = AQ + QC = 2d sin θ

The reflected rays reinforce (constructive interference, a bright maximum) when the path difference is a whole number of wavelengths:

2d sin θ = nλ   (n = 1, 2, 3, …)
This is Bragg's law; n is the order of reflection and θ the Bragg (glancing) angle.

Points to note

  • Since sin θ ≤ 1, nλ ≤ 2d. Hence the wavelength must satisfy λ ≤ 2d, and the maximum possible order is nmax < 2d/λ. This is why visible light (λ ≈ 5000 Å) cannot be diffracted by crystals, while X-rays can.
  • For a given crystal and λ, reflections occur only at the particular angles θ1, θ2, … that satisfy the law; at other angles the reflected rays cancel out.
  • For first order, sin θ = λ/2d; second order, sin θ = λ/d, and so on.

Applications

  • Measuring X-ray wavelength: using a crystal of known spacing d (for example rock salt, d = 2.82 Å) in a Bragg spectrometer, λ = 2d sin θ/n.
  • Crystal structure determination: using X-rays of known λ, measure θ to find d and hence the arrangement and spacing of atoms (this led to the structures of NaCl, diamond, DNA and proteins).
  • Study of alloys, minerals and defects in materials.

Worked examples

Example 1. X-rays of wavelength 1.54 Å show a first-order Bragg reflection at 15° from a crystal. Find the interplanar spacing.
d = λ/(2 sin θ) = 1.54/(2 × 0.2588) = 2.98 Å.
Example 2. X-rays of wavelength 1.54 Å fall on rock salt (d = 2.82 Å). Find the Bragg angles for first and second order.
First order: sin θ = λ/2d = 1.54/5.64 = 0.273 ⟹ θ ≈ 15.8°. Second order: sin θ = 2λ/2d = 0.546 ⟹ θ ≈ 33.1°.
Example 3. What is the highest order of reflection possible for the case in Example 2?
nλ = 2d sin θ ≤ 2d ⟹ n ≤ 2d/λ = 5.64/1.54 = 3.66, so nmax = 3.
