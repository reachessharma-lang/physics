25.1 Energy Levels and Energy Bands in Solids

Energy Level

Energy level: The definite value of energy that an electron can possess in an isolated atom is called an energy level.

In an isolated atom, electrons can occupy only certain discrete energy states. These allowed states are separated from one another by forbidden regions of energy.

Energy Band

Energy band: The closely spaced range of allowed energy values formed when a large number of atoms come together to form a solid is called an energy band.

When atoms are brought close together, their outer electron orbitals interact. Because of the interaction between neighbouring atoms and the exclusion principle, each discrete atomic energy level splits into a very large number of closely spaced levels.

For a solid containing a very large number of atoms, these closely spaced levels appear practically as continuous energy bands.

Formation of Energy Bands

  1. An isolated atom has discrete energy levels.
  2. When two atoms approach each other, their corresponding energy levels split into slightly different levels.
  3. As the number of atoms increases, each original energy level splits into a very large number of closely spaced levels.
  4. In a solid containing an enormous number of atoms, these levels form nearly continuous energy bands.
  5. Between two allowed bands there may be a range of energies that electrons cannot possess. This is called the forbidden energy gap.

Valence Band

Valence band: The highest energy band that is normally occupied by valence electrons at low temperature is called the valence band.

Conduction Band

Conduction band: The allowed energy band above the valence band in which electrons can move through the solid and contribute to electrical conduction is called the conduction band.

Forbidden Energy Gap

Forbidden energy gap: The energy interval between the top of the valence band and the bottom of the conduction band in which no allowed electron energy states exist is called the forbidden energy gap or band gap.
\[ \boxed{E_g=E_C-E_V} \] where \(E_g\) is the band-gap energy, \(E_C\) is the bottom energy of the conduction band and \(E_V\) is the top energy of the valence band.

25.2 Classification of Materials on the Basis of Energy Bands

The electrical behaviour of solids can be understood by considering the arrangement and separation of their valence and conduction bands.

Conductors

Conductors: Materials that allow electric current to flow easily because they have a large number of mobile charge carriers are called conductors.

In conductors, the valence band and conduction band may overlap, or the conduction band may be partially filled. Therefore, electrons can acquire slightly higher energies and move easily through the material.

Semiconductors

Semiconductors: Materials whose electrical conductivity lies between that of conductors and insulators are called semiconductors.

In a semiconductor, the valence band is almost completely filled and the conduction band is almost empty at very low temperature. The two bands are separated by a small forbidden energy gap.

The small band gap allows some electrons to acquire enough thermal energy to move from the valence band to the conduction band. Therefore, the conductivity of a semiconductor increases with temperature.

Insulators

Insulators: Materials that have very low electrical conductivity because their valence band is separated from the conduction band by a large forbidden energy gap are called insulators.

The energy required to move an electron from the valence band to the conduction band is very large under ordinary conditions.

Comparison of Conductors, Semiconductors and Insulators

Property Conductors Semiconductors Insulators
Band gap Zero or overlapping bands Small Large
Free charge carriers Large number Relatively small number Extremely small number
Conductivity High Intermediate Very low
Effect of temperature Conductivity generally decreases as temperature increases Conductivity increases as temperature increases Usually remains very low under ordinary conditions
Examples Copper, aluminium, silver Silicon, germanium Glass, rubber, mica
The term conductor is used for materials that conduct electricity readily. In the energy-band classification, the three main categories are conductors, semiconductors and insulators. Do not write “metals, semiconductors and conductors” as three separate band categories.

25.3 Intrinsic and Extrinsic Semiconductors

Intrinsic Semiconductor

Intrinsic semiconductor: A pure semiconductor without any intentional impurity added to it is called an intrinsic semiconductor.

Common examples are pure silicon (Si) and pure germanium (Ge).

At very low temperature, an intrinsic semiconductor behaves approximately as an insulator because very few electrons have enough energy to cross the forbidden gap. At higher temperatures, some covalent bonds break and electron-hole pairs are generated.

Electron-Hole Pair

When a valence electron receives sufficient energy to move into the conduction band, it leaves behind an empty state in the valence band. This vacancy behaves as a positive charge carrier and is called a hole.

Hole: The vacancy created by the removal of a valence electron from a covalent bond is called a hole. A hole behaves as a positive charge carrier.

In an intrinsic semiconductor, the number of free electrons is equal to the number of holes.

\[ \boxed{n_e=n_h=n_i} \] where \(n_i\) is the intrinsic carrier concentration.

Extrinsic Semiconductor

Extrinsic semiconductor: A semiconductor whose electrical conductivity has been increased by adding a small, controlled amount of suitable impurity is called an extrinsic semiconductor.

The process of adding a suitable impurity to a pure semiconductor is called doping.

Doping: The controlled addition of a small amount of impurity to a pure semiconductor to modify its electrical conductivity is called doping.

Extrinsic semiconductors are of two types:

  • n-type semiconductor
  • p-type semiconductor

Intrinsic vs Extrinsic Semiconductor

Intrinsic semiconductor Extrinsic semiconductor
Pure semiconductor Impurity is deliberately added
Examples: pure Si, pure Ge Examples: doped Si, doped Ge
Electron concentration equals hole concentration Electron and hole concentrations are generally unequal
Conductivity is relatively low Conductivity is increased by doping

25.4 Formation of p-Type and n-Type Semiconductors

Formation of n-Type Semiconductor

Silicon and germanium are tetravalent elements. Each atom forms four covalent bonds with neighbouring atoms.

When a small amount of a pentavalent impurity such as phosphorus (P), arsenic (As), or antimony (Sb) is added to pure silicon or germanium, an n-type semiconductor is formed.

A pentavalent impurity has five valence electrons. Four of them participate in covalent bonding with neighbouring semiconductor atoms. The fifth electron is weakly bound and can become a free electron with a small amount of energy.

Donor impurity: A pentavalent impurity that contributes an extra electron to a semiconductor is called a donor impurity.

Examples of donor impurities are phosphorus, arsenic and antimony.

In an n-type semiconductor, electrons are the majority carriers and holes are the minority carriers.

Formation of p-Type Semiconductor

When a small amount of a trivalent impurity such as boron (B), aluminium (Al), gallium (Ga), or indium (In) is added to pure silicon or germanium, a p-type semiconductor is formed.

A trivalent impurity has only three valence electrons. It forms three covalent bonds with neighbouring semiconductor atoms, leaving one bond incomplete. This deficiency of an electron behaves as a hole.

Acceptor impurity: A trivalent impurity that creates holes by accepting an electron from a neighbouring bond is called an acceptor impurity.

Examples of acceptor impurities are boron, aluminium, gallium and indium.

In a p-type semiconductor, holes are the majority carriers and electrons are the minority carriers.

Comparison of p-Type and n-Type Semiconductors

Property n-Type p-Type
Impurity Pentavalent Trivalent
Examples P, As, Sb B, Al, Ga, In
Impurity type Donor Acceptor

Charge of the semiconductor The semiconductor as a whole remains electrically neutral.
Important: “n-type” does not mean the semiconductor as a whole is negatively charged, and “p-type” does not mean it is positively charged. Both materials are electrically neutral overall. The names indicate the dominant charge carriers.

Conceptual Questions and Clear Interpretations

Question 1: Why does a solid have energy bands?

In an isolated atom, electrons occupy discrete energy levels. When a very large number of atoms come close together to form a solid, each atomic level splits into many closely spaced levels. These levels collectively form energy bands.

Question 2: Why can metals conduct electricity easily?

In metals, electrons have access to partially filled bands or overlapping valence and conduction bands. Therefore, electrons can move to nearby available energy states even under a small applied electric field.

Question 3: Why does the conductivity of a semiconductor increase with temperature?

As temperature increases, more valence electrons receive sufficient energy to cross the small forbidden energy gap into the conduction band. This increases the number of free electrons and holes, so conductivity increases.

Question 4: Why is a semiconductor different from an insulator?

Both have a forbidden energy gap, but the band gap of a semiconductor is relatively small, while that of an insulator is large. Therefore, thermal energy can produce appreciable charge carriers in a semiconductor but usually cannot do so in an insulator.

Question 5: Why is silicon called an intrinsic semiconductor in its pure form?

Pure silicon contains no intentionally added impurity. Its charge carriers are generated naturally by thermal energy, so it is called an intrinsic semiconductor.

Question 6: Why is phosphorus called a donor impurity?

Phosphorus has five valence electrons. Four form covalent bonds with neighbouring silicon atoms, while the fifth can become a free electron. Therefore, phosphorus donates an electron and is called a donor impurity.

Question 7: Why is boron called an acceptor impurity?

Boron has three valence electrons and forms only three covalent bonds with neighbouring silicon atoms. It can accept an electron to complete the fourth bond, thereby producing a hole. Hence it is called an acceptor impurity.

Question 8: What is the main difference between p-type and n-type semiconductors?

The main difference is the majority charge carrier. Electrons are the majority carriers in an n-type semiconductor, whereas holes are the majority carriers in a p-type semiconductor.

Common Mistakes

  • Do not confuse an energy level of an isolated atom with an energy band in a solid.
  • Do not say that the forbidden gap is an allowed energy region. It is a range in which no allowed electron energy states exist.
  • Do not call a metal a semiconductor merely because both can conduct electricity.
  • Remember: semiconductor has a small band gap; insulator has a large band gap.
  • Intrinsic semiconductor means pure semiconductor.
  • Extrinsic semiconductor means semiconductor modified by doping.
  • Pentavalent impurity produces an n-type semiconductor.
  • Trivalent impurity produces a p-type semiconductor.
  • Electrons are majority carriers in n-type material.
  • Holes are majority carriers in p-type material.
  • n-type and p-type semiconductors are not negatively and positively charged materials respectively; they are electrically neutral overall.

Key Points

  • An isolated atom has discrete energy levels.
  • When atoms form a solid, their energy levels split into closely spaced levels, producing energy bands.
  • The valence band contains the normally occupied valence-electron states.
  • The conduction band contains states in which electrons can contribute to electrical conduction.
  • The forbidden energy gap is \(E_g=E_C-E_V\).
  • Conductors have overlapping or partially filled bands.
  • Semiconductors have a small forbidden energy gap.
  • Insulators have a large forbidden energy gap.
  • Pure silicon and germanium are intrinsic semiconductors.
  • Doping produces extrinsic semiconductors.
  • Pentavalent impurities such as P, As and Sb are donor impurities and produce n-type semiconductors.
  • Trivalent impurities such as B, Al, Ga and In are acceptor impurities and produce p-type semiconductors.
  • Electrons are majority carriers in n-type semiconductors.
  • Holes are majority carriers in p-type semiconductors.
  • Both p-type and n-type semiconductors are electrically neutral overall.
